From 3D-printed prototype to injection-moulded part: what has to change

Why a part that prints well can be impossible or expensive to mould, the design changes moulding usually needs, how to sequence the redesign with your moulder, and the bridge options that keep you moving while the tool is made.

Quick answer

A printed part proves the idea, not the mouldability: before tooling it usually needs draft, even cored-out walls, properly proportioned ribs and bosses, radiused corners and fewer undercuts.

Printing builds a part layer by layer, so almost any shape can be made. Moulding fills a steel or aluminium tool and must then pull the part straight out of it, so the geometry has to suit flow, cooling and ejection. Redesign the CAD for moulding, review it with your moulder, choose the moulding grade, and expect the first tool samples to need some adjustment.

Why a good print can be a bad moulding

A printer adds material only where the file says, so a solid block, a sharp corner, a sideways hook or an untapered wall costs it nothing extra. Those are exactly the features that cause trouble in a mould tool.

In moulding, molten plastic is forced into a closed tool, cools against its walls, shrinks slightly, and is pushed out once the tool opens. Injection moulding explained covers the cycle. The plastic must flow into every feature, cool evenly (cooling is usually the longest step, set by the thickest wall), and come straight out of the tool. A print that ignores these can mould with sink marks, voids and warping, stick in the tool, or need costly moving tool parts.

Changes are also cheaper on the model than on the tool: once it is cut, removing material from the part can mean welding and re-machining the tool, or a new insert.

Draft on faces in the direction of pull

Draft is the slight taper on faces that run in the direction the tool opens. Prints rarely have it. Without it, a moulded part drags and sticks on ejection and scuffs its cosmetic faces. The injection moulding DFM checklist gives a common starting point of at least 0.5 to 1 degree on every face parallel to the direction the tool opens, with more for deep features and textured surfaces; a textured face can need several degrees, and the texture supplier will usually state a minimum.

Add draft in CAD early: retrofitting it ripples through mating dimensions and clearances.

Even walls: coring out thick sections

Prototypes are often printed thick, for strength or because solid was easier to model. In moulding, even wall thickness is the most important single rule. A thick section stays molten longest and, as it cools and shrinks, pulls the surface into a sink mark or leaves an internal void, and lengthens the cycle every part pays for.

The fix is to core thick features out so they are made of walls close to the nominal thickness, and to blend any change in thickness gradually rather than in steps. The checklist's typical range is around 1 to 4 mm for many small and medium parts, depending on material and size; the material datasheet and your moulder set the actual figure.

Ribs, bosses and corners

With thinner walls, stiffness and fixings come from ribs and bosses; too thick, they sink the face opposite. The DFM checklist's typical starting points are:

  • Ribs: thickness about 40 to 60 per cent of the adjoining wall; height up to about 3 times the wall; spaced at least about 2 wall thicknesses apart; drafted.
  • Bosses: outside diameter around twice the hole; boss wall thinner than the main wall; supported by gusset ribs or joined to nearby walls; cored from below. Follow the screw manufacturer's hole size for your chosen plastic, not the hole that happened to work in the print.
  • Corners: inside radius at least about half the wall; outside radius about inside radius plus the wall, so the wall stays even around the bend. Sharp printed corners concentrate stress, restrict flow and are harder to fill and eject.

The Injection Moulding DFM Checker applies these rules to your own dimensions and explains each result.

Undercuts: side actions or redesign

An undercut is anything that stops the part pulling straight out of the tool: a side-facing clip, a side hole, an internal groove. Prototypes collect them freely. In a tool each usually needs a side action, a lifter or another moving part, which adds cost, lead time and maintenance.

Before accepting a side action, ask whether the feature can be redesigned. A snap-fit hook can often be moulded straight-pull by putting a hole in the wall behind it, so a core passes through and forms the hook's underside. Some joins are better changed altogether, to screws into bosses, heat-set inserts or a different snap arrangement; joining methods compared sets out the options. Keep a side action only where the feature earns its cost.

Parting lines, gates and ejector marks

A moulded part carries three marks a print never has:

  • Parting line: a fine line where the two halves of the tool meet, usually around the widest outline of the part.
  • Gate: a small blemish where the plastic entered, left when the feed channel is cut or broken off.
  • Ejector marks: small round witness marks from the pins that push the part out, usually on the inside faces.

Agree gate, ejector and parting-line positions with the moulder, away from cosmetic faces; moving them after the tool is cut is costly. Surface finishes for plastic and metal parts covers polish and texture choices, and why texture needs extra draft.

Shrinkage, warp and tolerances

Every moulded plastic shrinks as it cools, by an amount that depends on the material, the wall thickness and the process. The toolmaker allows for it when cutting the tool, which is one reason the tool is cut for a specific material and choosing the material late causes trouble. Uneven walls and large flat areas shrink unevenly and can warp; ribs or gentle curvature help flat areas stay flat.

Fits proven in a print do not carry over: printed clearances reflect the printer, orientation and finishing, so a snap that works printed may be tight or loose moulded. Call up tight tolerances only where the function needs them, check critical fits with the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator, and state them on a drawing as described in tolerances and engineering drawings.

Splitting or merging parts

A prototype's split into parts often reflects the printer's build volume rather than production. Merging parts can remove assembly steps and fasteners but makes a more complex tool; splitting one can turn deep pockets or undercuts into two simple straight-pull parts. Each part normally needs its own tool or cavity, so balance tool cost, assembly time and servicing.

Printing materials are not moulding grades

Printing filaments, powders and resins are formulated for printing. Even where a material shares a name with a moulding plastic, such as nylon, its strength, stiffness, toughness and heat resistance differ, and resins can behave quite unlike any moulded thermoplastic. Prints are also weaker between layers, so a printed failure, or success, may not repeat when moulded.

Choose the moulding grade from the product's requirements, not from what printed well, using choosing plastic materials and the Plastic Material Selector. Then re-check the wall thickness, ribs and snap fits against that grade's datasheet, since materials flow and shrink differently.

Sequencing the redesign

  1. Freeze the function first. Use prints to settle what the part must do. Redesigning for moulding while the requirements are still moving means doing it twice.
  2. Redesign the CAD for moulding. Choose the material, set the nominal wall, add draft, core out thick sections, then size ribs, bosses and radii, and remove undercuts where you can.
  3. Print the redesigned part to confirm assembly, treating strength and fits as indicative.
  4. DFM review with the moulder. Most toolmakers provide a DFM report proposing gate, parting line and ejector positions; for complex parts, ask for a mould-flow simulation as well. Read it before approving the tool design.
  5. First samples (T1). Check the first parts off the tool against your drawings and plan for at least one modification round. Quality control and first-article inspection explains how parts are signed off.

Bridge options while the tool is made

If you need parts before the production tool is ready, two common bridges are:

  • Vacuum casting: silicone moulds, often made from a printed master, produce tens of parts in production-like plastics. Good for samples and trials, though not in the moulding grade.
  • Aluminium or soft-steel bridge tools: real injection-moulded parts in the production material for the first few thousand parts, at lower tool cost and shorter lead time, with a shorter tool life. They need the same DFM as a production tool.

The risk-progressive manufacturing strategy explains how to stage these commitments, and prototyping vs production compares the routes by volume and cost.

Worked example: a printed enclosure lid

Illustrative and qualitative. A start-up's handheld device has a lid printed in nylon powder that fits and survived early user testing. They plan to mould it in PC/ABS. The redesign changes:

  • Thick walls thinned to an even nominal wall, avoiding sink and shortening the cycle, with three internal ribs to restore stiffness.
  • Solid screw pillars cored out into bosses joined to the side walls with gussets, so the outer face does not sink and the bosses do not crack under screw torque.
  • Draft added to the side walls, with more on the textured top face, so the lid releases cleanly without drag marks.
  • Sharp inside corners radiused relative to the wall, for better flow and lower stress.
  • Two sideways clips redesigned as straight-pull snaps with pass-through cores, removing two side actions from the tool.
  • Snap and lid-to-base clearances re-specified on the drawing for the moulded material, rather than copied from what worked printed.
  • Gate and ejector positions agreed on the inside of the lid, and the parting line placed on an edge where it will not be seen.

None of these changes alters what the lid does. The team prints the redesign to check assembly, then requests quotes and a DFM review.

Common mistakes

Sending the print file as the tooling file. A print mesh, or a model with no draft and solid sections, gives the toolmaker nothing sound to quote or cut from. Send a CAD model redesigned for moulding, with a drawing.
Keeping thick printed walls. Walls made thick to strengthen a print will sink, void and lengthen every cycle when moulded. Core them out and stiffen with ribs.
Zero draft. Prints need none, so prototypes rarely have it. Without it, parts stick and scuff; adding it late changes every mating dimension.
Assuming printed fits will match moulded fits. Clearances, snap forces and stiffness all change with the process and material. Re-specify critical fits and confirm them on the first moulded samples.

FAQ

Can I injection mould my 3D-printed design as it is?

Rarely. Printed designs usually lack draft, have thick or uneven walls, sharp corners and undercuts that printing makes freely. Those cause sink, warping, sticking and costly side actions in a tool, so the CAD normally needs redesigning for moulding first.

How much draft do I need to add to a printed part?

A common starting point is at least 0.5 to 1 degree on faces parallel to the direction the tool opens, with more for deep features and textured surfaces. Textured faces can need several degrees; the texture supplier will usually state a minimum.

Will a moulded part be stronger than my 3D print?

Often, but not always, and not in a predictable way. Printed parts are weaker between layers and printing materials differ from moulding grades. Choose the moulding grade from the requirements and test critical features on the first moulded samples.

Will snap fits that work printed also work moulded?

Not necessarily. Clearances, stiffness and snap forces change with the material and process. Re-specify the fit for the moulding grade, design the snap to mould straight-pull where possible, and confirm it on the first samples.

What can I use while the injection mould is being made?

Vacuum casting gives tens of parts in production-like plastics from silicone moulds, and an aluminium or soft-steel bridge tool gives real moulded parts in the production material for the first few thousand, at lower tool cost but with a shorter tool life.
