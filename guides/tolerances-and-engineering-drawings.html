Tolerances and engineering drawings: the basics

What a tolerance is, why every dimension has one, how tolerances drive cost, what fits and datums mean, how tolerances add up across an assembly, and what a drawing must show before a supplier can quote it properly.

Quick answer

A tolerance is the amount a dimension is allowed to vary and still be acceptable. Every dimension has one, either written next to it or set by the general tolerance in the title block.

Keep most dimensions on a sensible general tolerance and call out tighter tolerances only on the few features that control fit, function or safety, measured from clearly stated datums. Tighter tolerances cost more to make and inspect, so a drawing for a quote should show material, finish, critical dimensions and their tolerances, revision and quantities, and nothing tighter than the product needs.

What a tolerance is and why every dimension has one

No process makes two parts exactly the same: tools wear, plastics shrink as they cool and sheet springs back after bending. A tolerance accepts that and states how far a dimension may move from its nominal (intended) size before the part stops working or stops fitting.

A dimension written as 20 ± 0.1 mm means any part measuring between 19.9 and 20.1 mm is acceptable. Tolerances can also be one-sided, such as 20 +0.1/−0, or written as two limits. The total allowed variation, here 0.2 mm, is the tolerance band. A dimension without a tolerance is not exact; the supplier simply has to guess, and two suppliers may guess differently.

General tolerances vs specific callouts

Rather than writing a tolerance next to every dimension, most drawings state a general tolerance in the title block, the box of information in the corner of the sheet. It applies to every dimension that has no tolerance of its own. Many drawings do this by referring to a recognised standard such as ISO 2768, which sets general tolerances by size range in a few classes from fine to coarse.

Specific callouts are then added only where the general tolerance is not good enough: a bearing bore, a sealing face, a hole pattern that must line up with another part. A good drawing has few of these, and each one should be traceable to a reason. If you cannot say why a dimension needs a tight tolerance, it probably does not.

Tolerances, processes and cost

Each manufacturing process holds some dimensions easily and others only with extra effort. Asking for more than a process naturally gives means slower cutting, extra operations, more inspection, more rejected parts, or a change of process altogether. The comparison below is qualitative and consistent with the process guides on this site; always confirm what a particular supplier can hold.

Process What it holds well Where tight tolerances get expensive
CNC machining Generally the tightest of common processes; features cut in the same set-up hold well relative to each other Tight callouts mean slower cutting, more inspection and sometimes extra operations; features on different sides add set-up variation
Sheet metal Features cut in the same flat blank, such as holes in one face Dimensions across bends are looser, because each bend adds variation; machining-level tolerances on bent features are not economic
Injection moulding Very repeatable once the tool and process are settled Plastics shrink and can warp, so tight tolerances raise tool and inspection cost and may need tool adjustment after trials
Practical rule: cost rises much faster than precision. Halving a tolerance that the process already holds comfortably may cost little; halving one that is already near the process limit can mean a different process or full inspection of every part.

Fits: clearance, transition and interference

Where one part goes into another, such as a shaft in a hole or a pin in a slot, what matters is how the two tolerance bands relate. There are three kinds of fit:

  • Clearance fit: the largest shaft is always smaller than the smallest hole, so there is always a gap. Used for parts that slide, rotate or must assemble by hand.
  • Interference fit: the smallest shaft is always larger than the largest hole, so the parts must be pressed or shrunk together. Used for parts that must stay put without fasteners, such as bearings or dowels.
  • Transition fit: the bands overlap, so some pairs have a small gap and some a slight press. Used for accurate location where parts still need to come apart.

For round parts, the ISO system of limits and fits (ISO 286) gives standard hole and shaft codes for each kind of fit, so you can choose a recognised fit rather than inventing limits.

Datums: where measurement starts

A dimension is a distance between two things, so every measurement has to start somewhere. A datum is the feature, usually a face, edge, hole or axis, that the drawing declares as the reference. Datums are normally the features that locate the part in the assembly: the face it sits on, the hole a pin goes through.

Without stated datums, the supplier and the inspector may measure from different faces and get different answers, both right by their own reading. Dimensioning critical features from the same datum also stops tolerances accumulating along a chain of dimensions.

Geometric tolerancing in concept

Plus-or-minus tolerances control size and distance, but not shape or orientation. A plate can measure correctly in thickness everywhere and still be bowed; a hole can be the right diameter and still be in the wrong place. Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (often called GD&T, defined in ISO 1101 and ASME Y14.5) adds controls for this, written in a small frame on the drawing that refers to datums.

  • Flatness limits how far a surface may deviate from a perfect plane, which matters for sealing faces and mounting surfaces.
  • Position defines a zone, measured from the datums, within which the centre of a hole or pin must lie. It suits hole patterns that must line up with another part better than chains of ± dimensions do.

You do not need every symbol to start; know that shape, orientation and location can be controlled separately from size, and ask for that control only where a function depends on it.

Tolerance stack-ups

In an assembly, tolerances add up. A gap between a lid and a housing depends on several dimensions on several parts, and the gap varies by the combined variation of all of them. A worst-case stack-up adds every tolerance, assuming every part is at its limit at the same time; it guarantees assembly but can demand tight, expensive tolerances on long chains. An RSS (root sum of squares) stack-up assumes independent, random, centred variation, so it predicts a narrower range that most assemblies will fall within. Use worst case for safety-critical fits, short chains and low volumes; RSS suits longer chains from stable processes in reasonable volumes, and many engineers check both. The formulas are on the engineering estimation formulas page, and the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator works through both for up to six dimensions.

What a drawing for a quote must show

A 3D model shows the shape; the drawing tells the supplier what matters. For a firm quote, each part drawing should include:

  1. Material, specific enough to buy (grade, not just "aluminium" or "plastic").
  2. Finish: surface finish, coating, colour or texture, and which faces are cosmetic.
  3. Critical dimensions, clearly marked, with their tolerances and datums.
  4. General tolerance in the title block for everything else.
  5. Revision letter or number, matching the 3D file name.
  6. Quantities, either on the drawing or in the request, including prototype and production batch sizes.

Send the drawing as a PDF alongside a neutral 3D file such as STEP. The documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer covers the rest of the pack, and the RFQ Pack Builder helps you assemble a request for quotation that suppliers can compare like for like.

Worked example: a sliding insert in a housing

Illustrative example, using the default figures in the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator. A removable insert slides into a pocket in a machined housing. The pocket is 60 ± 0.15 mm long and the insert is 59.4 ± 0.15 mm. The insert must slide in by hand without rattling, which the team defines as a gap of 0.2 to 1.0 mm.

The nominal gap is 60 − 59.4 = 0.6 mm. Worst case, it is 0.6 ± 0.30 mm, so 0.30 to 0.90 mm, inside the required range. RSS gives about 0.6 ± 0.21 mm, roughly 0.39 to 0.81 mm. If both lengths had instead been left on a looser tolerance of, say, ± 0.3 mm each (an illustrative figure), the worst-case gap would be 0.0 to 1.2 mm: some inserts would jam and some would rattle.

The useful decision is which dimensions earn a callout:

Feature In the gap chain? Tolerance decision
Pocket length Yes Specific callout, ± 0.15 mm, measured from the pocket end wall as datum
Insert length Yes Specific callout, ± 0.15 mm
Housing outside size and wall thickness No General tolerance
Chamfers, finger recess, label area No General tolerance; mark the label face as cosmetic
Mounting holes in the housing base No, but they must line up with another part Positional tolerance from the base datums, not a tight ± on every hole

Two callouts and one positional control cover what the function needs; everything else stays on the general tolerance.

Common mistakes

Tolerancing everything tightly. It feels safe, but it raises machining, tooling and inspection cost on features that do not matter, and it hides the few that do.
No datums. Without a stated reference, the supplier and the inspector can measure the same part differently and disagree about whether it passes.
Missing material or finish. Suppliers either guess, which makes quotes hard to compare, or come back with questions, which delays them.
Sending CAD only, with no drawing. A 3D model shows nominal geometry but not which features are critical, what the tolerances are, or which faces are cosmetic. Suppliers then quote to their own assumptions.

Decision rules

  • Start from a general tolerance that the chosen process holds comfortably, and state it in the title block.
  • Add a specific callout only where you can name the fit, function or safety reason for it.
  • Choose datums from the features that locate the part in the assembly, and dimension critical features from them.
  • Use geometric controls such as flatness or position where shape or location matters more than size.
  • Check every critical gap with a stack-up, worst case first for short or safety-critical chains.
  • Ask the supplier which callouts drive their price.

FAQ

What is a tolerance on an engineering drawing?

The amount a dimension may vary from its nominal size and still be acceptable, for example 20 ± 0.1 mm. Every dimension has one, either written next to it or covered by the general tolerance in the title block.

What is a general tolerance?

A tolerance stated once in the title block, often by reference to a standard such as ISO 2768, that applies to every dimension without its own tolerance. It keeps the drawing readable and leaves specific callouts for the features that matter.

Why do tight tolerances cost more?

Holding a tighter tolerance than the process naturally gives means slower cutting, extra operations, more inspection and more rejected parts, or a different process altogether. Cost rises much faster than precision.

What is a datum?

The feature, usually a face, hole or axis, that a drawing declares as the reference that measurements start from. Datums are normally the features that locate the part in the assembly.

Do I need a drawing if I send a 3D model?

For a rough budget price, a 3D model with material, finish and volumes may be enough. For a firm quote or production, send a drawing too: it shows the critical dimensions, tolerances, datums, finish and revision that a model alone does not.
