Choosing a 3D printing process for prototypes: FDM, SLA, SLS, MJF and metal

What the main 3D printing processes are good and bad at, how to pick one by the question your prototype must answer, how to design and prepare parts for printing, and when to move on to machining, casting or tooling.

Quick answer

Choose the process by the question the prototype must answer: filament (FDM) for quick fit and form checks, resin (SLA) for appearance and fine detail, powder nylon (SLS or MJF) for tougher functional parts and small batches, and metal printing only for complex metal parts.

No printing process behaves exactly like a moulded or machined production part, so treat printed results on strength, stiffness and finish as indicative. Most products use more than one process across their prototype rounds, and printing usually gives way to machining, vacuum casting or bridge tooling as the design settles.

The main processes in brief

"3D printing" covers several different processes. All build a part layer by layer, but their materials, surfaces and strength differ enough to change what a prototype can tell you.

FDM: filament printing

Fused deposition modelling (FDM) melts a plastic filament and lays it down in lines, layer by layer. It is the process behind most desktop printers and usually the quickest, cheapest way to get a part in your hand.

Layer lines are visible, fine detail is limited, and parts are anisotropic: noticeably weaker between layers than along them, so a clip can snap along a layer line under a load it would otherwise carry. FDM is good for fit checks, form studies, jigs and early mechanism layouts.

SLA: resin printing

Stereolithography (SLA) and related resin processes cure a liquid photopolymer with light, layer by layer. They give fine detail, crisp edges and smooth surfaces that take paint well, which makes them a common choice for looks-like models, small detailed parts and masters for vacuum casting.

Many standard resins are more brittle than production plastics, and some soften, yellow or embrittle with heat and ultraviolet light. Tougher engineering resins exist but still do not behave quite like a moulded thermoplastic. Parts need washing, curing and support removal.

SLS and MJF: powder bed nylon

Selective laser sintering (SLS) and Multi Jet Fusion (MJF) fuse fine plastic powder, most commonly nylon, layer by layer. The surrounding unfused powder supports the part as it builds, so no support structures are needed and parts can be nested together in one build.

The result is tough nylon parts that suit functional prototypes, snap fits that must flex, trial housings and small batches. The surface is slightly grainy, often dyed or left grey or white, and fine detail is softer than resin.

Metal printing

Metal powder bed processes melt metal powder, typically steels, aluminium or titanium alloys, to build dense metal parts. They are specialist processes used for complex metal parts that would be hard or impossible to machine, such as internal channels or lightweight lattice structures.

Parts need supports and significant post-processing, often heat treatment, support removal and machining of critical faces. For most consumer product prototypes, a machined part is simpler and cheaper unless the geometry genuinely needs printing.

How they compare

These are qualitative, typical comparisons. Specific machines, materials and bureaus vary, so ask your supplier about the material they would actually use.

Process Surface Detail Strength Typical prototype use
FDM (filament) Visible layer lines Moderate Reasonable along layers, weaker between them Fit and form checks, jigs, quick iterations
SLA (resin) Smooth, paints well High Often brittle; varies by resin Looks-like models, small detailed parts, casting masters
SLS and MJF (powder nylon) Slightly grainy, matt Good Tough and fairly even in all directions Functional prototypes, trial housings, small batches
Metal printing Rough until finished Good High, after heat treatment Complex metal parts that are hard to machine

Choosing by the question the prototype must answer

The most useful starting point is not the process but the question. A prototype that answers the wrong question well is still wasted. Looks-like vs works-like prototypes explains how to separate appearance questions from function questions; the printing process then follows.

  • "Does it fit, and is the size right?" FDM is usually enough; speed matters more than finish.
  • "Does it look and feel right?" SLA gives surfaces that can be sanded and painted to represent the final finish for user sessions and photography.
  • "Does it work, flex or survive handling?" SLS or MJF nylon is the usual printed choice for clips, hinges, brackets and trial housings, with the caveat that nylon is not your production plastic.
  • "Will it survive in the production material?" This is often a question printing cannot answer. Machined parts in the real material, or vacuum castings in a resin chosen to mimic it, are usually better evidence.

Designing parts for printing

Printing removes many moulding constraints, but it adds a few of its own. These points are qualitative; ask your bureau for the minimum sizes their process and material support.

  • Wall thickness. Very thin walls can warp or break during cleaning, and very thick sections can distort. Keep walls reasonably even, and if the part will later be moulded, use the wall thickness intended for moulding so the prototype stays honest.
  • Orientation. Orientation decides where layer lines fall, which faces are smoothest and, for FDM, which directions are weak. Put loaded features such as clips and bosses so the load runs along the layers, and keep cosmetic faces away from supports.
  • Supports. FDM, SLA and metal printing need supports under overhangs, which leave marks. Chamfers instead of flat overhangs, or splitting a part in two, can reduce them. Powder processes need no supports, but enclosed hollows need escape holes for the powder.
  • Holes and threads. Small holes often print undersize or out of round; plan to drill or ream holes that matter. Printed threads are weak and wear quickly, so use heat-set or press-fit threaded inserts, or self-tapping screws into bosses, for anything assembled more than once.
  • Clearances. Parts that must slide or snap together need more clearance than the same parts moulded. Print a quick test piece for critical fits rather than relying on nominal sizes, and see tolerances and engineering drawings for how to state the fits that matter.
  • Post-processing. Sanding, painting, dyeing, vapour smoothing and machining critical faces all help. For looks-like models, finishing often takes longer than printing.

Preparing files and asking for quotes

A print bureau can only quote and build what the files and notes tell them. A clean package avoids delays and surprises:

  1. Export the right files. STL is the common format for printing; export at a fine enough resolution that curves are not faceted. Send STEP as well, because it keeps exact geometry.
  2. Check units and scale. State the units, and give one or two key overall dimensions so the bureau can check the scale.
  3. Name the process and material, or ask for advice. If you are unsure, describe what the part must do and let the bureau suggest options.
  4. Add orientation and finish notes. Mark cosmetic faces, faces that must be free of support marks, loaded features and any holes to be drilled or inserts to be fitted.
  5. Give quantity and deadline. Quantity changes the best process and the price per part.

When quotes come back, check they are for the same process, material, finish and quantity before comparing price. Getting and comparing manufacturing quotes explains how to compare like with like.

When to move beyond printing

Printing is strongest while the design is changing. As questions shift from "what should it be?" to "will it work as made?", other routes usually give better evidence:

  • CNC machining when you need real materials, tight geometry or durable functional parts, typically from one part up to low hundreds.
  • Vacuum casting when you need tens of looks-like or trial parts in production-like plastics; a silicone mould typically yields roughly 10 to 25 parts, often from an SLA master.
  • Bridge tooling when you need hundreds to several thousand moulded parts for pilots or early sales, and the design is stable enough to commit to a tool.

Worked example: three rounds of a handheld product

Illustrative example. A small team is developing a handheld battery-powered device with a two-part plastic housing, a button, a clip-on cover and a small circuit board. They plan three prototype rounds and choose a printing process, or not, for each based on the question that round must answer.

Round Question Process chosen Why
1. Form and fit Is the size comfortable in the hand, and do the board and battery fit inside? FDM, several variants Quick and cheap; layer lines do not matter for grip and packaging checks
2. Appearance and function Do users like the look and feel, and does the cover clip survive repeated use? SLA for painted looks-like models; SLS nylon for the working clip and housing Two questions, two processes: resin for finish, nylon for a clip that must flex
3. Field trial Does the product hold up in real use by a small group of users? Vacuum cast housings from SLA masters Production-like plastic and finish for a few dozen units

In round 2 the FDM clip from round 1 would have been misleading: it snapped along a layer line, while the nylon clip flexed as intended. The team still treats the nylon result as indicative and plans to re-test the clip in moulded parts before design freeze.

Common mistakes

Judging strength from FDM parts. A filament part that breaks along a layer line tells you about the print, not the design; one that survives may still fail when moulded. Use FDM for geometry and test strength in more representative material.
Expecting production finish. No printed part comes off the machine looking moulded. If appearance matters, budget for finishing, or use vacuum casting once the form is settled.
Using one process for every question. A single favourite process is convenient but answers some questions badly. Match the process to the question, round by round.
Ignoring orientation. Orientation changes strength, accuracy and where support marks fall. Left to chance, it can put a weak layer line through a clip or a support scar on a cosmetic face.

Decision rules

  • If the question is fit or size, use FDM and print variants quickly.
  • If the question is appearance or fine detail, use SLA and budget for finishing.
  • If the part must flex, carry load or survive handling, use SLS or MJF nylon and treat results as indicative.
  • If the part is metal and complex, compare metal printing with machining before choosing.
  • If the answer depends on the production material, move to machining, vacuum casting or tooling rather than printing again.

FAQ

Which 3D printing process is best for prototypes?

It depends on the question the prototype must answer. FDM suits quick fit and form checks, SLA suits appearance and fine detail, SLS or MJF nylon suits tougher functional parts and small batches, and metal printing suits complex metal parts. Many products use several processes across their prototype rounds.

What is the difference between FDM and SLA printing?

FDM lays down melted plastic filament, giving quick, low-cost parts with visible layer lines and weaker bonds between layers. SLA cures liquid resin with light, giving smoother surfaces and finer detail, but many resins are more brittle.

Are SLS and MJF parts strong enough for functional testing?

For many functional checks, yes. Powder bed nylon parts are tough and fairly even in strength in all directions, which suits clips, brackets and trial housings. They are still not your production material, so treat results as indicative and re-test critical features in production-intent parts.

What file format should I send to a 3D print bureau?

STL is the common format for printing, exported at a fine enough resolution that curves are smooth. Send STEP as well where possible, state the units, and add notes on orientation, cosmetic faces, holes to be drilled and inserts to be fitted.

When should I stop 3D printing and use another process?

When the questions depend on the production material, finish or quantity. CNC machining suits real-material functional parts, vacuum casting suits tens of production-like parts, and bridge tooling suits hundreds to several thousand moulded parts once the design is stable.
