How to write a product requirements document for a physical product

A practical guide to writing requirements for a physical product: what to include, how to make each requirement specific and testable, and how to keep the document useful as the project moves from idea to production.

Quick answer

A product requirements document says what the product must do, for whom, and how you will know it does it, without deciding how it will be designed.

Write down the user and the problem, the use environment, the functional and performance requirements, regulatory and safety needs, cost and volume targets, and constraints. Make each requirement specific and testable, mark which are essential, and keep it short enough that people actually read it. Update it as you learn, and use it to brief designers, judge concepts and plan tests.

Why write one

A product requirements document (PRD), sometimes called a product specification or design brief, turns an idea into something a team can design, cost and test against. Without one, decisions drift, each person imagines a different product, and nobody can say when the design is good enough. It is also the basis for a clear brief to a consultancy; see working with a product design consultancy.

What to include

Section What it answers
Purpose and user Who the product is for, the problem it solves and why they will choose it over their current alternative
Use environment Where and how it is used: indoors or outdoors, temperature, moisture, drops, cleaning, who handles it
Functional requirements What it must do
Performance requirements How well: capacity, speed, battery life, accuracy, strength, with numbers
Usability and appearance Size and weight limits, controls, accessibility, brand and finish expectations; check them through usability testing
Regulatory and safety Markets, likely regulations and standards, and safety requirements
Commercial targets Target retail price, target unit cost, launch volumes and timing
Life and sustainability Expected lifetime, servicing and repair, materials and end-of-life expectations
Constraints and assumptions Fixed decisions, existing parts or platforms, and what you have assumed but not yet confirmed

How to write good requirements

  • Specific and testable. "Survives a 1 m drop onto concrete on any face" can be tested; "robust" cannot.
  • What, not how. "Holds 500 ml" leaves room for design; "a 70 mm diameter cylinder" decides too early, unless it is a genuine constraint.
  • Prioritised. Mark each requirement as must-have or nice-to-have, so trade-offs are clear.
  • Justified. Note where each requirement comes from: user research, regulation, a retailer or a cost target.
  • Numbered. Give each requirement an ID so tests, concepts and decisions can refer to it.

Illustrative example for a portable outdoor light. Weak: "long battery life, tough, affordable". Better: "R1 Must give at least 8 hours on the medium setting. R2 Must survive a 1 m drop onto concrete on any face. R3 Must resist rain (target rating to be confirmed against the relevant standard). R4 Should retail at or below £40, with a target landed cost set from the route to market."

Practical rule: for every requirement, ask "how would we test this?" If there is no answer, rewrite it until there is.

Keep it alive

A PRD is not written once and filed. Early on it will contain targets and open questions; as research, prototypes and quotes come in, firm them up and record what changed and why. Use it to score concepts with the Pugh Concept Selector, and turn the testable requirements into a test plan with the Product Test Plan Builder.

Common mistakes

Writing the solution, not the need. It closes off better designs before anyone has explored them.
Vague words. "Durable", "premium" and "easy to use" mean different things to everyone until you add numbers or tests.
Everything is essential. Without priorities, the team cannot make sensible trade-offs.
No cost target. Designing without a target unit cost often leads to a product that cannot be sold profitably.
Too long to read. A focused few pages that people use beats a long document nobody opens.

FAQ

What is a product requirements document?

A document that sets out what a product must do, for whom, in what conditions and to what standard, along with cost, volume and regulatory targets, so a team can design, cost and test against it. It describes the need rather than the design.

How long should a product requirements document be?

Long enough to cover the essentials and short enough to be read and used. For many physical products, a focused few pages with numbered, prioritised requirements works better than a long document.

What makes a good requirement?

It is specific, testable, prioritised and says what is needed rather than how to achieve it. If you cannot describe how you would test it, rewrite it.

Is a PRD the same as a design brief?

They overlap. A design brief often focuses on the project, scope and goals for a designer, while a PRD focuses on the product's requirements. Many small teams combine them into one document.

When should the requirements be updated?

Whenever research, prototypes, testing or quotes change what you know. Record what changed and why, so decisions can be traced back to evidence.
