Building a hardware business case.

A credible hardware business case connects market reach, conversion, unit economics, development spend, and payback timing. It should make the opportunity easier to challenge, not easier to oversell.

Quick answer

A hardware business case shows whether enough reachable buyers will pay a price that recovers development spend in an acceptable time.

Payback (years) = upfront investment / (annual gross profit - annual operating cost)

Build it from reachable market, realistic conversion, unit price and gross margin, then compare the profit that produces with the development, tooling and operating cost it has to carry. If payback only looks acceptable when every assumption is optimistic, the case is not yet robust.

Early business cases go wrong when they confuse the total market with the reachable market. Hardware businesses do not monetise market size directly. They monetise the portion they can actually reach, convert, and serve.

A good business case is not a sales document. It is a structured argument that the team, an investor or a board can challenge line by line. Its job is to make the important assumptions visible, show which ones the outcome depends on most, and point to the evidence that would make them stronger.

Decision test: if the business case only works when market share, conversion, and pricing all lean optimistic at the same time, the opportunity is not yet robust enough.

What belongs in the first-pass model

  • Addressable market and the smaller reachable segment: who could buy, and who you can realistically get in front of with your channels and budget.
  • Conversion: the share of that reachable audience that becomes actual unit sales each year.
  • Unit price and gross-profit quality: the net price you receive after channel deductions, minus landed unit cost.
  • Development and tooling spend: design, prototypes, tooling, certification and launch work that must be recovered.
  • Annual operating cost: people, marketing, support, warehousing and overhead that the product has to carry every year.

Keep the first pass small. Five or six well-argued inputs are more useful than a fifty-tab spreadsheet that nobody can explain. Detail can be added once the shape of the opportunity has survived a first challenge.

Addressable market versus reachable market

The addressable market is everyone who could plausibly need the product. The reachable market is the part you can actually access in the next two or three years, given your channels, geography, sales capacity, certification and price point. For most early hardware businesses the reachable segment is a small fraction of the headline figure.

That is not a weakness. A narrow, well-understood segment with a clear reason to buy is easier to convert than a vast, vague one. The risk lies in multiplying a large market by a small percentage and treating the result as demand. That number has no evidence behind it. Build demand upwards from the channels you will use and the conversion rates you can defend.

Why payback matters

A large market story can still hide a poor payback profile. If it takes too long for the product to recover development and operating spend, the risk profile remains weak even if the eventual upside looks attractive.

Payback also connects the business case to cash. A product that recovers its investment in two years needs much less funding, and carries far less risk, than one that needs six. Hardware product lives are finite, so a long payback can mean the product is superseded before it has earned its keep.

Worked example: a connected air-quality monitor

Illustrative figures only. A small team is planning a connected air-quality monitor for small offices. They assume an addressable market of 400,000 organisations, of which 10% (40,000) are reachable through their chosen channels. They expect 6% of that reachable group to buy each year, giving 2,400 units.

The net price received is GBP180 and landed unit cost is GBP81, so gross margin is 55% and gross profit is GBP99 per unit. Annual gross profit is 2,400 x GBP99 = GBP237,600. The product carries GBP140,000 of annual operating cost, leaving GBP97,600 a year towards the GBP210,000 upfront investment in development, tooling and certification. Payback is GBP210,000 / GBP97,600, or roughly 26 months.

That looks reasonable, until conversion is tested. The same model with conversion moved up and down tells a very different story.

Scenario Annual conversion Units per year Profit after operating cost Payback
Conservative 4% 1,600 GBP18,400 Over 11 years
Expected 6% 2,400 GBP97,600 About 26 months
Optimistic 8% 3,200 GBP176,800 About 14 months

The lesson is not that the product is good or bad. It is that conversion is the assumption that decides the case, so it is where the team should spend its next round of evidence-gathering: pilot customers, pre-orders, distributor conversations or a small paid test.

A useful business case tells you which assumption to test next. A weak one only tells you the answer you hoped for.

Common mistakes in hardware business cases

Top-down demand. Taking a percentage of a large market instead of building demand from channels, conversion and sales capacity.
Using retail price as revenue. Revenue should be what the business actually receives after VAT, retailer margin or marketplace fees, not the price on the shelf.
Gross margin based on factory price. Unit cost should be landed cost, including freight, duty, packaging and an allowance for scrap and returns.
Forgetting the operating cost. A product can have healthy unit economics and still fail to pay back if support, marketing and overhead are left out.
One scenario only. A single forecast hides fragility. Always show conservative, expected and optimistic cases side by side.

Practical next steps

  1. Write down the reachable segment and the channels that reach it, in one sentence each.
  2. Estimate net price per channel and landed unit cost, even if the cost is a range.
  3. List the upfront investment as cost blocks rather than a single figure.
  4. Run three conversion scenarios and note which one the business can survive.
  5. Identify the single assumption that moves payback most, and plan how to test it before committing to tooling.

FAQ

What should a hardware business case include?

At minimum: the reachable market, expected conversion into unit sales, net price after channel costs, landed unit cost and gross margin, upfront development and tooling spend, annual operating cost, and the resulting payback period. It should also show conservative, expected and optimistic scenarios so readers can see how fragile the outcome is.

What is the difference between addressable and reachable market?

The addressable market is everyone who could plausibly need the product. The reachable market is the smaller group you can realistically access in the next few years with your channels, geography, budget and certification. Business cases should be built on the reachable market.

How long should payback be for a hardware product?

There is no universal figure, but shorter is safer because hardware has upfront tooling and certification costs and a finite product life. Many early teams look for payback within two to three years of launch. If payback stretches beyond the expected life of the product, the case needs rethinking.

How accurate does an early business case need to be?

It does not need to be precise, but it does need to be honest and explainable. Ranges are fine at the start. What matters is that each assumption has a stated source and that the team knows which assumption moves the result most.

What gross margin should a hardware business case assume?

Use the margin your actual unit cost and channel prices produce, not a target. As a rough guide, many consumer hardware businesses aim for gross margins of around 40 to 60 per cent on direct sales, and less on wholesale. If the case only works at a margin well above that range, check the cost and price assumptions.
