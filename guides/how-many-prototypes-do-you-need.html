How many prototypes do you need? Planning prototype rounds

Why the number of prototypes depends on the questions you need to answer, the typical rounds from rough models to pre-production samples, what makes a product need more, and how to keep prototyping fast and affordable.

Quick answer

There is no fixed number: plan prototypes by the questions you need to answer, and build each one to answer a specific question as cheaply and quickly as possible.

Most physical products go through several rounds: rough models to explore ideas, looks-like and works-like prototypes to test appearance and function, an integrated prototype that combines them, and pre-production samples made with production processes. Simple products may need few rounds; products with electronics, safety requirements or tight fits usually need more (see electronics in a physical product). Budget for at least one round more than you expect, because testing nearly always finds something.

Plan by question, not by count

Each prototype should answer something you do not yet know: does the mechanism work, is it comfortable, will users understand it, does it fit together, will it survive a drop? List the questions first, then group them into prototype rounds. The Prototype Strategy Tool helps match questions to prototype types, and usability testing a prototype explains how to answer the "will users understand it?" questions in each round.

Typical prototype rounds

Round Answers Typical methods
Sketch and rough models Size, form, basic idea, early user reactions Card, foam, quick 3D prints, existing parts
Works-like Does the mechanism, electronics or function work? 3D prints, machined parts, development boards, test rigs
Looks-like Appearance, feel, size and user appeal Finished 3D prints or machined models, painted and assembled
Integrated (looks-and-works-like) Do function and form fit together in one product? Higher-quality prints, machining, urethane casting, custom electronics
Pre-production samples Can it be made, assembled and tested at volume, and does it pass certification? Production tooling and processes, pilot batches

Each round may involve several iterations. See prototyping vs production for the methods in more detail, and looks-like vs works-like prototypes for how to brief and combine those two streams. Where a round uses 3D printing, choosing a 3D printing process helps match filament, resin or powder printing to the question.

What drives the number of rounds

  • Complexity: moving parts, electronics, software and fluids each add questions.
  • Risk: products that are safety-critical, worn on the body or used by children need more testing.
  • Novelty: a new mechanism or use needs more learning than a variation on a known product.
  • Precision: tight fits, seals and snap fits often need iterations in the production material.
  • Certification: formal testing may need units made exactly like production.
  • Evidence needed: investors, crowdfunding or retailers may need a convincing looks-like model earlier.
Practical rule: test the riskiest question first, with the cheapest prototype that can answer it. A rough model that kills a bad idea in a week is worth more than a beautiful model of the wrong product.

Budget and time

Prototype costs rise sharply through the rounds: rough models are cheap, integrated prototypes and pre-production samples cost far more, and changes after tooling are the most expensive. Plan time for testing and changes between rounds, not just building. Ending each round with a short design review turns what the prototype showed into recorded decisions before the next one is built. Add prototype rounds to your plan with the Timeline Planner and budget them in the Startup Cost Planner.

Common mistakes

Polishing too early. High-quality models of an untested idea cost time and money and make change feel harder.
Combining everything too soon. An integrated prototype that fails is harder to diagnose than separate works-like and looks-like tests.
Building without a test plan. A prototype without defined tests answers nothing; plan them with the Product Test Plan Builder.
Budgeting for one perfect round. Testing almost always leads to changes; plan for iterations.

FAQ

How many prototypes does a product need?

There is no fixed number. Most physical products go through several rounds, from rough models to works-like, looks-like, integrated and pre-production samples, each with its own iterations. Complexity, risk, novelty and certification drive how many.

What is the difference between looks-like and works-like prototypes?

A looks-like prototype shows appearance, size and feel without necessarily working. A works-like prototype proves the function, mechanism or electronics without necessarily looking right. They are often built separately before being combined.

Which prototype should I build first?

The cheapest one that answers your riskiest question. If the main risk is whether the mechanism works, start with a works-like rig; if it is whether people want it, a rough model or looks-like prototype may come first.

When should I stop prototyping?

When the integrated design meets its requirements, passes its tests and has been checked with production processes in pre-production samples. Moving to tooling earlier risks expensive changes.

How do I keep prototyping costs down?

Test one question at a time with the simplest prototype that can answer it, use quick and cheap methods early, avoid polishing untested ideas, and plan tests before building.
