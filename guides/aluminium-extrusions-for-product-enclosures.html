Aluminium extrusions for product enclosures: profiles, end caps and finishing

A plain-English guide to building a product enclosure from extruded aluminium: how extrusion works, why it suits electronics housings, when an off-the-shelf profile is enough and when a custom die is worth it, how to design a profile an extruder can make, and how end caps, machining and finishes complete the box.

Quick answer

An extruded enclosure is a length of aluminium profile, cut to size, machined for connectors and closed with end caps. It gives a tough metal housing for a modest die cost.

Start with an off-the-shelf enclosure profile if one fits; move to a custom profile when you need your own shape, PCB slots or screw ports built in. Design the profile with balanced walls, generous radii and simple hollows, and expect looser tolerances than machining. Seal the open ends with gasketed end caps screwed into the profile, and anodise the result.

What extrusion is

Extrusion works much like squeezing toothpaste through a shaped nozzle. A cylinder of aluminium, called a billet, is heated until it is soft but still solid, then a press pushes it through a steel die with the profile's shape cut through it. What comes out is a long length with the same cross-section all the way along. It is cooled, stretched straight, usually aged to its final strength, and cut to the lengths you need.

The key limit follows from this: an extrusion can only have features that run the full length of the part. Anything that changes along the length, such as a connector hole, a mounting slot or a cut-out, has to be added afterwards.

Why extrusion suits enclosures

Many electronics products are a circuit board in a box, and a constant-section tube open at both ends is exactly what extrusion makes well.

  • Low tooling cost. An extrusion die is a relatively simple steel disc, so it typically costs a fraction of a die-casting die or an injection mould for a part of similar size. Manufacturing processes compared sets it alongside the alternatives in relative terms.
  • Many lengths from one die. The same profile can be cut short for one product and long for another, so a product family can share one die.
  • A good surface. Outside faces come out clean and consistent, ready for blasting or brushing.
  • Heat spreading. Aluminium conducts heat well, so the enclosure itself can carry heat away from hot components, which matters most in sealed products. Managing heat in a product enclosure explains why the housing is often the best heat sink.
  • Anodising. Extrusion alloys generally anodise well, giving a hard, even finish in clear or dyed colours. Surface finishes for plastic and metal parts covers anodising and its alternatives.

The trade-off is shape freedom. An extruded box is a straight-sided tube; if the product needs curved, sculpted or tapered forms, die casting, moulding or CNC machining will suit it better.

Standard or custom profiles

Several suppliers sell ready-made enclosure extrusions: rectangular and oval tubes with PCB slots, screw ports and matching end plates, often in a range of widths and lengths. A custom profile means paying for your own die, which you then normally own or have reserved for your use.

Route Choose it when Watch out for
Off-the-shelf enclosure profile Prototypes, low volumes, or products where a standard shape is acceptable; you need parts quickly with no tooling Board size and connector positions must fit the catalogue sizes; the product looks like others built from the same range
Custom profile You need your own shape, branding, fin pattern or internal features; volumes justify a die; the product family will use one section in several lengths Die lead time and a sample round; extruders often set a minimum run, so ask about it early

A common path is to prototype in a standard profile, then commission a custom one once the board outline and look are stable.

Designing the profile

Limits depend on the alloy, profile size and the extruder's presses. Treat these points as typical guidance and ask your extruder to review the profile before the die is made.

Feature Typical guidance Why
Wall thickness Keep walls as even as possible around the profile Metal flows through the die fastest where the opening is widest; balanced walls fill evenly and stay straight
Thin next to thick Avoid very thin walls joined to thick sections; blend changes gradually Uneven flow distorts the profile, and thin fins next to thick walls are hard to hold in shape and tolerance
Corners Round internal and external corners generously Radii help the metal flow, strengthen the die and reduce stress; sharp corners wear and crack dies
Hollows Keep hollow sections simple and as few as the design allows Each fully enclosed hollow needs a more complex die, which costs more and runs slower
Screw ports Build ports into the profile, usually in the corners, sized for the screw the extruder and fastener supplier agree End caps can then be screwed straight into the extrusion, with no drilling or inserts
PCB slots Add pairs of rails to slide the board in; allow clearance for board thickness variation and the finish The slots locate the board without extra brackets
Tolerances Expect looser tolerances, flatness and straightness than machining; machine features that need precision The profile cools and is stretched straight after leaving the die, so it moves more than a machined part

Hollow, semi-hollow and solid profiles. A solid profile has no enclosed spaces, like a flat bar or an angle. A hollow profile fully encloses at least one space, like a tube; the die needs a mandrel supported on bridges, and the metal splits around them and welds back together inside the die. A semi-hollow profile nearly encloses a space through a narrow gap, like a C-shaped screw port, and can be harder to make than it looks, because the thin tongue of die steel forming the gap is weak. Discuss screw port shapes with the extruder.

Practical rule: send the extruder a 2D section with critical dimensions and cosmetic faces marked, not just a 3D model, and ask which features they would change to make the die more reliable.

Use your extruder's published tolerances on the profile drawing and keep tight ones for machined features, as tolerances and engineering drawings explains.

End caps and sealing

The open ends carry the connectors, lights and the seal, so they take most of the design work.

  • Machined end plates in aluminium suit low volumes, need no tooling, are easy to change and anodise to match the body.
  • Moulded end caps in plastic make sense at higher volumes, can include light pipes, cable glands and gasket grooves, and avoid machining every plate. They need a mould, so they usually come later.
  • Fixing. Screws pass through the end plate into the screw ports. Use thread-forming screws where the fastener supplier agrees they suit the port, or tap the ports, and consider how often the joint will be reopened.
  • Sealing. A die-cut or moulded gasket between the end cap and the profile's end face, compressed by the screws, seals the joint. The cut end must be square and clean for the gasket to work. Sealing and waterproofing an enclosure explains gasket compression and how to seal cables and connectors.

Cutting, machining and deburring

Most extruded enclosures need some secondary operations, which many extruders or their partners can do:

  • Cutting to length, with the cut ends kept square and flat so the end caps seal. Some designs face the ends by machining for a better sealing surface.
  • CNC machining for anything that varies along the length: connector holes, display windows, mounting slots, vents and countersinks. Keep these features simple and reachable, as in the CNC machining design rules, and group them on as few faces as possible to reduce set-ups.
  • Deburring of cut ends and machined edges, so there are no sharp edges for users and no burrs to cut gaskets or cables.
  • Finishing, usually bead blasting and anodising after machining, with bare areas masked where the case needs an electrical contact.

Alloy and temper

Most extrusions are made from 6000-series alloys, which flow well through a die and respond to heat treatment. As choosing metals for a product notes, 6063 is a common choice for extrusion, and 6060 is widely used for similar work; both give a good surface for anodising. Where the profile must carry more load, extruders often offer 6082, which is stronger but generally harder to extrude in thin, detailed sections and may not anodise to quite the same appearance.

Specify the temper as well as the alloy: the same alloy can be soft or fully heat-treated, and the strength differs considerably. Ask your extruder which alloy and temper they recommend for your profile and finish, and check the datasheet values they work to.

Worked example: a sensor enclosure

Illustrative example. A small company makes an environmental sensor for plant rooms. Its first units used an off-the-shelf enclosure extrusion, but the board was a poor fit, a bracket was needed to hold it, and the housing looked generic. With the electronics stable and a product family planned in two lengths, the team commissions a custom profile.

Feature Designed into the profile, or added afterwards?
Outer shape with shallow fins on the top face for heat spreading and a recognisable look Designed into the profile
PCB slots sized to the board, replacing the bracket Designed into the profile
Four screw ports in the corners for the end caps Designed into the profile, shape agreed with the extruder
A slot along the base for a wall-mounting bracket Designed into the profile
Two lengths for the two product versions Cut to length from the same profile
Square, clean end faces for the gaskets Machined after cutting
Vent hole for a breathable membrane, and a window for a status light Machined after cutting
End caps with a cable gland, a connector cut-out and a gasket groove Machined from aluminium plate for the first batches; a moulded cap is considered once volumes are known
Deburring, bead blasting and clear anodising, with a masked earth contact inside Finishing after machining

Before ordering the die, the team asks two extruders to review the section and quote the die, minimum run and machining, then builds the first samples into complete units for seal and temperature tests. This example gives no prices on purpose: get real quotes for your own profile.

Common mistakes

Designing the profile like a machined part. Thin fins beside thick walls, sharp corners and tight tolerances everywhere make a die that is hard to run. Let the extruder review the section.
Forgetting what cannot be extruded. Holes, cut-outs and anything that changes along the length need machining. Count those operations in the cost, not just the extrusion.
Leaving the end caps until last. The ends carry the connectors, seal and fixings, and the screw ports depend on them. Design them alongside the profile.
Too little clearance in the PCB slots. Board thickness varies and anodising adds material. Leave clearance so the board slides in without forcing.
Commissioning a custom die too early. If the board outline or the look is still changing, prototype in a standard profile first.

FAQ

Is an extrusion die expensive?

Compared with a die-casting die or an injection mould for a similar part, an extrusion die is usually much cheaper, because it is a relatively simple steel disc. Cost rises with profile size, complexity and the number of hollows, so ask extruders for quotes on your own section.

Can I start with a standard enclosure extrusion and change later?

Yes, and it is often the sensible route. Prototype and sell early units in a standard profile, then move to a custom profile once the board outline, connectors and look are stable. Keep the end caps and board design easy to adapt.

Which aluminium alloy is used for extruded enclosures?

6063 and 6060 are commonly used, because they extrude well and anodise to a good finish. 6082 is often offered where more strength is needed. Ask your extruder which alloy and temper they recommend for your profile and finish.

Can an extruded enclosure be waterproof?

Yes. The tube itself has no joints along its length, so sealing comes down to the two ends: gaskets compressed between the end caps and square, clean end faces, plus sealed cable glands and connectors. Test the complete unit, not just the parts.

What has to be machined after extrusion?

Anything that is not constant along the length: connector holes, windows, vents, mounting holes and sometimes the end faces. Cutting to length and deburring are always needed, and finishing usually follows machining.
