Planning a pilot production run

What a pilot run is for, where it sits between first-article approval and volume production, how large to make it, what to plan and measure, how to run it with a supplier you cannot visit every day, and how to decide whether you are ready to ramp up.

Quick answer

A pilot run is a small batch built on the real production line, at production rate, to prove the process rather than the design, before you commit to volume.

It comes after design freeze and first-article approval. Plan it like an experiment: agree in advance what you will measure, including yield, defect types, assembly time, test results and how packed units survive transit, and what result counts as a pass. Then decide go, fix and repeat, or stop, and feed what you learned into your cost, quotes and cash plan before placing the volume order.

What a pilot run is for

By the time you reach a pilot run, the design should already be proven and the first parts from production tools checked against the drawings. What is not yet proven is whether the whole system can make the product repeatedly, at the rate and quality you need. That system includes:

  • The process: settings that hold steady over hours, not just for a handful of careful samples.
  • The tooling and fixtures: tools that run at their intended cycle, and the jigs, fixtures and test stations that assembly depends on.
  • The assembly line: operators who have not built your product before, working from written instructions at normal speed.
  • Packaging and labelling: the real box, inserts, labels and documents, packed the way production will pack them.
  • The supply chain: bought-in parts arriving from production suppliers, in production quantities, on time.

Electronics teams often call this stage production validation, or PVT. The question is the same: if we place the volume order tomorrow, will what arrives be what we approved?

Where it sits in the journey

A pilot run follows two earlier gates. First, design freeze: the design has met its requirements, its tests are complete and further changes go through change control. The testing and validation plans guide covers the evidence you need, and Am I Ready to Commit to Tooling? checks whether you are ready to cut tools at all.

Second, first-article approval: parts from production tools and processes are measured and checked in full, and golden samples are signed off. Quality control and first-article inspection explains how. A first article proves that the process can make a good part; a pilot proves that it does, repeatedly, when the line is running normally.

The pilot is also the bridge into the wider staged approach described in the risk-progressive manufacturing strategy: it supplies the yield, defect and process evidence that justifies the next, larger commitment.

How large should it be?

There is no standard number. The batch needs to be large enough for the line to run as it will in production, long enough to settle and show drift, and for occasional problems to have a fair chance of appearing. A handful of units built slowly by the best technician proves very little.

But every pilot unit is built before the process is proven, so a very large pilot carries the risk it was meant to reduce. A sensible size usually follows from the process: a shift or a few hours of continuous assembly, a full cycle of any batch process such as painting, and enough units for the tests and samples you have planned. Agree the quantity with your supplier, and what happens to units that fail.

What to plan and measure

Write a short pilot plan before the run and share it with the supplier. It should name what will be built, how, and what will be recorded:

  • Work instructions. Illustrated steps for each station, written for operators new to the product. Where they hesitate or improvise, the instructions are unclear. Assembly instructions and work instructions for production explains what each step should contain.
  • Fixtures and test stations. Check that jigs hold parts repeatably and end-of-line tests catch faults without rejecting good units. Record each unit's results, not just pass or fail.
  • Yield and defect types. Count units that pass first time, need rework or are scrapped, and record each defect by type and station. The pattern matters more than the total: one recurring defect usually has one fixable cause.
  • Cycle and assembly time. Time each station and compare it with the estimate your costing assumed. The assembly time guide and the Assembly Time Estimator give the baseline to compare against. Note the bottleneck station, because it sets the rate of the whole line.
  • Packaging and transit. Pack units exactly as production will, then ship or drop-test them and inspect what arrives. Packaging design for shipping covers the checks.
  • Labelling and documents. Check rating labels, barcodes, serial numbers, warnings and instructions against your specification and required markings.
  • Supply chain. Note any bought-in part that arrived late, short, in the wrong revision or with quality problems.

Who attends, and running it with a remote supplier

On the supplier side, the process engineer, line leader and quality lead should own the run. On your side, someone who knows the design should be present: ideally its engineer, or a trusted representative or inspection company if you cannot travel. Their job is to observe, record and answer questions, not to fix problems by hand.

With a distant supplier, agree the plan, quantity and acceptance criteria in writing before the run. Ask for photos or video of each station, a daily summary of yield and defects, the test data and measured times, and some finished, packed units sent to you. Using the pilot units

Pilot units are the first that truly represent production, which makes them valuable:

  • Testing. Repeat the key tests from your validation plan on production-made units, especially durability, drop, temperature and anything sensitive to process settings.
  • Certification samples, where appropriate. Test houses generally want samples that represent what you will sell, so pilot units can be a good source. Confirm with your test house what they need and when; product certification basics outlines how conformity is shown.
  • Early customers. A few units with friendly customers can reveal set-up and instruction problems that factory tests miss. Tell them these are early units, and only sell units that passed the same checks as production.
  • Reference samples. Keep a few as reference units for comparing future batches.

Go/no-go criteria and what happens if it fails

Agree the criteria with the supplier before the run, so the decision is about evidence rather than opinion. Typical criteria: first-pass yield at or above an agreed level; no safety or critical-to-quality defects; every recurring defect understood and fixable; assembly time close enough to the estimate to support your costing; packed units surviving transit checks; correct labelling; and bought-in parts available in production quantities.

The outcome is usually one of three:

  • Go: the criteria are met, perhaps with minor actions that can be closed during ramp-up.
  • Fix and repeat: the causes are understood and fixable, such as a fixture, an instruction or a process setting, but the fix must be proven, often with a smaller, focused re-run.
  • Stop: a problem points back to the design or tooling. Treat it as a controlled design change, with new first articles where parts change, rather than patching it on the line.

Agree in advance who pays for a repeat run: often the supplier for process faults, and you for design or specification issues.

Feeding the results into cost and plan

The pilot replaces assumptions with measurements. Update your unit cost with measured assembly time, real yield and scrap, and actual packaging in the Product Cost Estimator. If the numbers have moved, revisit your quotes using getting and comparing manufacturing quotes, since a supplier's price was based on their own assumptions about time and yield.

Then check timing and cash. A repeat run delays first stock, and the volume order may carry a minimum quantity and a deposit due well before sales arrive. Minimum order quantities and cash shows how to plan for that.

Worked example: a small consumer product made overseas

Illustrative and qualitative. A start-up's small battery-powered kitchen gadget has two moulded housing parts, a bought-in circuit board, a battery, a button and a printed retail box. The supplier is overseas, and first articles were approved from video and samples.

The team and supplier agree a written plan: roughly a shift's output, yield recorded by defect type, every station timed, every unit tested, and a carton of packed units sent by the normal freight route. The criteria are no safety defects, recurring defects understood, times close to the estimate and packed units arriving undamaged. An inspection company attends for the team.

What the pilot finds:

  • A recurring button defect. Several buttons stick, because a fixture lets the housing close slightly off-centre. The supplier modifies the fixture; the design does not change.
  • Assembly slower than estimated. Operators struggle to route a cable around a boss. Clearer instructions and a pre-formed cable bring the time down, though not fully to the estimate, so the team updates its costing.
  • Transit damage. In the shipped carton, some retail boxes are crushed at the corners. A stronger shipping carton and a change of pallet pattern solve it, and the change is checked with a drop test.
  • A late bought-in part. The battery arrives late from its supplier. The team agrees a buffer stock and a second approved source.

The decision is fix and repeat: a short re-run proves the fixture, cable and carton changes before the volume order is placed. A few pilot units go to the test house, and a few to friendly early customers, who find the quick-start card unclear; it is reprinted before launch.

Common mistakes

Treating the pilot as a sales batch. When pilot units are already promised to customers, the pressure is to ship them whatever the results show. Plan the pilot to learn, and size and promise units accordingly.
No agreed acceptance criteria. Without criteria written down beforehand, every result can be argued either way, and the volume order goes ahead by default.
Design changes slipped in during the pilot. A "small tweak" on the line means the pilot no longer represents what was approved, and the change has not been tested. Route any change through change control and re-prove it.
Skipping packaging and transit checks. A product that leaves the factory perfect can arrive damaged. Pack and ship pilot units the way production will be packed and shipped.

Decision rules

  • Prove the process, not the design: freeze the design and approve first articles before the pilot.
  • Write the plan and criteria first, and agree them with the supplier.
  • Run at production rate with normal operators, fixtures, packaging and suppliers.
  • Record yield by defect type and time by station, then fix causes, not symptoms.
  • Update cost, quotes and cash from the measured results before placing the volume order.

FAQ

What is a pilot production run?

A small batch built on the real production line, with production tools, fixtures, operators, packaging and suppliers, at normal rate. Its purpose is to prove the manufacturing process before committing to volume, not to prove the design.

How is a pilot run different from first-article inspection?

First-article inspection checks the first parts from production tools in full, to show the process can make a correct part. A pilot run builds complete, packed products at production rate, to show the process does so repeatedly.

How many units should a pilot run be?

There is no standard number. Make it large enough for the line to run normally for a sustained period and for occasional problems to show, and enough to cover planned tests and samples, but not so large that a process problem becomes expensive.

Can I sell units from a pilot run?

Units that pass the same checks as production can be sold, often to friendly early customers. Do not plan the pilot as a sales batch, though, because the pressure to ship can override what the results are telling you.

What happens if the pilot run fails?

Find the cause of each problem. Process, fixture and instruction problems are fixed and proven with a focused re-run; design or tooling problems go through change control, with new first articles where parts change, before you try again.
