Must-have documents before talking to a manufacturer

Manufacturer conversations go badly when the team brings a vague idea, a half-defined file pack, and no shared view of what is confidential. The fastest route to better feedback is better preparation.

Quick answer

An NDA is useful, but it is not the only document that matters.

Useful supplier pack = NDA + product scope + drawings + BOM + volumes and target cost + prototype evidence + decision questions

Before talking to a manufacturer, prepare a short scope note, current 3D CAD and drawings, a bill of materials, realistic volumes and a target cost, plus any prototype evidence and the specific questions you need answered. Suppliers respond better when the technical brief is clear, the scope is explicit, and the commercial conversation is framed properly from the start.

A manufacturer can only quote what it can understand. A vague enquiry tends to produce a vague quote, a long list of questions, or no reply at all, because the supplier cannot judge whether the work suits them. A clear, well-organised pack signals that the team is serious and makes quotes from different suppliers easier to compare.

The core documents most teams need first

  1. NDA: sets the confidentiality baseline if the discussion is sensitive.
  2. Product scope note: explains what the product is, what stage it is at, and what kind of help is being requested.
  3. Drawings or 3D files: enough geometry to discuss manufacturability meaningfully.
  4. BOM or major component list: shows the parts and bought-in items likely to matter.
  5. Prototype evidence: photos, test results, or lessons from existing builds.
  6. Open questions: what you actually want the supplier to comment on.

What to send at each stage of the conversation

Not every document belongs in the first email. A staged approach protects sensitive information and keeps the conversation focused.

  • First contact: scope note, an image or overview drawing, rough volumes, and the NDA if needed.
  • Feasibility or DFM review: 3D CAD, a draft BOM, prototype evidence and your question list.
  • Formal quotation: fully toleranced drawings, material and finish specifications, volumes and delivery expectations.
  • Pre-production: inspection criteria, approved samples, packaging and labelling details.
Do not send everything by default. Send the right amount for the conversation you need, not every file you have.
Revision control matters. Suppliers lose confidence when they cannot tell which file is current.
Asking better questions improves quotes. If you need feedback on tooling risk, tolerances, or yield, ask that directly.

Illustrative example: a moulded sensor enclosure

Imagine a startup that needs a two-part injection-moulded enclosure for an outdoor wall-mounted sensor. A weak enquiry says “please quote for our enclosure” and attaches a single native CAD file. A stronger pack, sent after an NDA, includes a one-page scope note, STEP files for both parts, a drawing showing the critical dimensions around the seal groove and snap fits, a preferred material with UV stabilisation, the texture finish, a first order of a few thousand units with expected growth, and a target part cost range.

It closes with three questions: are the draft angles and wall thicknesses suitable for moulding, would a family tool make sense for both parts, and what tool life should the team specify for the expected volumes. The figures and choices here are illustrative, but the effect is typical: suppliers can quote on the same basis, and their DFM comments arrive before tooling, not after.

Where the NDA fits

An NDA is usually about setting expectations, not about making a weak brief safe. If the product definition is still vague, the real problem is often not legal. It is that the supplier cannot respond properly because the pack does not yet make sense.

If your main concern is protecting sensitive information before the conversation starts, read How to protect product IP before talking to manufacturers. It covers staged disclosure, file control, and the practical limits of NDAs on their own.

Common mistakes when approaching manufacturers

Leaving out volumes. Without quantities, a supplier cannot choose between processes or size tooling, so the quote becomes guesswork.
Tolerancing everything tightly. Tight tolerances on non-critical features raise cost. Mark only what genuinely matters to function or fit.
Sending native CAD only. Not every supplier uses the same software. A STEP file and a PDF drawing are the safer default.
Reusing file names across revisions. Add a revision letter to every file name and drawing title block. Bills of materials and revision control sets out a simple scheme for part numbers, revisions and changes.
Asking only for the best price. Price without context invites shortcuts. Ask about lead time, tool ownership, tool life and what is excluded.

The quality of a quote rarely exceeds the quality of the brief that requested it.

Practical next steps

  1. Draft a one-page scope note and decide what stays confidential until a later stage.
  2. Export current parts as STEP files and create drawings for critical parts, with revision letters; tolerances and engineering drawings covers what each drawing should show.
  3. Build a simple BOM and a first estimate of unit cost; the guide on how to estimate manufacturing cost can help.
  4. Write down the volumes you can defend and the questions you need answered.
  5. Prepare an NDA with the NDA Generator if the discussion is sensitive.

FAQ

Do I need an NDA before talking to a manufacturer?

If you are sharing unpublished designs or sensitive technical detail, an NDA is a sensible baseline and most established suppliers will sign a reasonable one. It sets expectations but does not replace careful, staged disclosure. For a general first enquiry with no sensitive detail, it is often not needed yet.

What file format should I send to a manufacturer?

STEP is the most widely accepted neutral 3D format for machined and moulded parts, with a PDF drawing for dimensions, tolerances and finishes. STL files suit 3D printing but lose the precise geometry most other processes need. Ask the supplier if they prefer native files.

What should a request for quotation (RFQ) include?

An RFQ should include the part files and drawings, material and finish, quantities and batch sizes, required delivery dates, quality expectations and the specific items you want priced, such as tooling, samples and unit cost. The clearer the RFQ, the easier it is to compare quotes fairly.

What is a bill of materials?

A bill of materials, or BOM, is a structured list of every part, component and material in a product, with the quantity used per unit. It usually shows whether each item is made or bought and who supplies it. Manufacturers use it to quote assembly and sourcing, and teams use it to estimate unit cost.

How much detail does a manufacturer need to give a quote?

For a budgetary quote, 3D files, material, finish and realistic volumes are often enough. For a firm quote, they also need toleranced drawings, quality requirements and delivery expectations. If a detail is missing, the supplier will either ask, assume, or add margin to cover the uncertainty.
