Industrial design vs engineering design: who does what

What industrial designers, product and mechanical design engineers, electronics and firmware engineers, and manufacturers each typically focus on, where the roles overlap, who you need at each stage of development, how to brief them so hand-offs do not fail, and what to ask when choosing help.

Quick answer

Industrial design shapes what a product is like to see, hold and use; engineering design makes it work reliably and makes it possible to manufacture.

Industrial designers usually lead on form, ergonomics, user experience, colour, material and finish (CMF) and brand. Product and mechanical design engineers lead on architecture, mechanisms, materials, tolerances, design for manufacture and production CAD, with electronics and firmware engineers alongside where the product needs them. The roles overlap heavily, many people work across both, and most products need both kinds of thinking from the start, in different proportions at each stage.

What industrial designers focus on

Industrial design is about the relationship between the product and the person using it. An industrial designer typically asks: what should this look and feel like, how will people understand it, and does it fit the brand and the setting it lives in?

  • Form and proportion: the overall shape, how parts read together and how the product sits on a shelf, a desk or in a hand.
  • User experience and interaction: how people pick it up, start it, understand controls and feedback, and recover from mistakes.
  • Ergonomics: grip, reach, weight balance, forces and comfort across the range of intended users.
  • CMF (colour, material and finish): the visible materials, colours, textures and surface finishes, and how they feel and age.
  • Brand and product character: making the product recognisably part of a family and right for its price point and market.

Typical outputs are sketches, mood and CMF boards, foam and looks-like models, renders and surface CAD that defines the visible shape.

What product and mechanical design engineers focus on

Engineering design is about making the product work, keeping it working and making it buildable at a sensible cost. A product or mechanical design engineer typically asks: how is it arranged inside, what holds it together, what will break, and how will it be made and assembled?

  • Architecture: how the product is divided into parts and sub-assemblies, where components sit and how they connect.
  • Mechanisms: hinges, latches, buttons, springs, seals and anything that moves or takes load.
  • Materials: choosing grades for strength, stiffness, temperature, chemical resistance and cost, often from the shortlist the CMF work suggests.
  • Design for manufacture and assembly (DFM/DFA): shaping parts for the chosen process, such as draft and wall thickness for injection moulding, and reducing parts, fasteners and assembly steps.
  • Tolerances: deciding how much each dimension may vary and checking parts still fit and function; see tolerances and engineering drawings.
  • CAD for manufacture: solid models, drawings and data packs that suppliers can quote and make from.

Typical outputs are works-like prototypes, detailed CAD, engineering drawings, a bill of materials, calculations and test results.

Electronics and firmware engineers

If the product has a circuit board, battery, sensor, motor or radio, electronics engineers design the circuits and board layout, and firmware engineers write the software that runs on it. Their work constrains the enclosure as much as the enclosure constrains them: board size and shape, battery space, heat, antenna placement and test access all need agreeing with the mechanical and industrial design early. Electronics in a physical product covers what to plan and what to hand over to an electronics partner.

Manufacturers and toolmakers

Manufacturers, toolmakers and other suppliers know their own processes in depth: what their machines and tools can hold, where defects tend to appear and what drives their cost. Their feedback on a design is valuable, especially before tooling. But a factory's job is usually to make what it is given. It may suggest changes that suit its process, which are not always the changes that suit your product, so someone on your side needs to judge them against your requirements. Where the roles overlap

The boundary is blurred in practice. Ergonomics, materials, finishes, parting lines and the position of every button affect both appearance and engineering, so the best results come when both kinds of thinking happen together rather than in sequence. Many industrial designers are comfortable with mechanisms and manufacturing, and many engineers have a strong eye for form and use. Job titles vary between companies and countries, so ask what someone actually does rather than relying on the title.

"Product design engineer" is a common name for someone who works across the two. Product design often focuses on form, user experience and appearance; product design engineering also covers how the product works and how it will be made: mechanical architecture, materials, tolerances, assembly, manufacturing route and cost. Who you need at each stage

Every discipline has some role throughout, but who leads tends to shift as the product moves from idea to production.

Stage Main question Skills usually most needed
Opportunity and concept What should we build, for whom, and could it work? Industrial design for direction and use; engineering for feasibility, rough architecture and early cost
Prototypes Does it work, can people use it, and does it look right? Separate or combined looks-like and works-like builds; engineering and electronics leading, industrial design refining
Detailed design and production Can it be made consistently, at the target cost? Engineering for DFM, tolerances and data packs; manufacturer and toolmaker input; industrial design signing off CMF and appearance

Briefing, and how hand-offs go wrong

Most problems between disciplines are not about skill but about hand-offs: one person finishes a stage and passes it on, and the next discovers that something important was never agreed. Common failures include:

  • A beautiful shape that leaves no room for the battery, board or fixings, or cannot be moulded without costly tooling.
  • An engineered layout that changes the proportions or controls the users liked in testing, without anyone checking.
  • CMF choices that look right on a render but have no matching production finish or material grade.
  • CAD sent to a supplier without drawings, tolerances, materials or quantities, so quotes and parts vary.

To avoid them:

  1. Share one set of requirements. A product requirements document gives every discipline the same targets for use, appearance, function, cost and compliance.
  2. Overlap, do not relay. Bring engineering into concept reviews and industrial design into engineering reviews, even briefly.
  3. Agree who owns each decision, such as the visible surfaces, the internal layout and the final material, and how changes are approved.
  4. Hand over complete information, including CAD revisions, decisions made and open questions. Must-have documents before talking to a manufacturer lists what suppliers need.
Practical rule: if a change in one discipline would surprise someone in another, it needs a review, not just an updated file.

Questions to ask when choosing help

Whether you are looking at an industrial design studio, an engineering consultancy, a freelancer or someone who spans both, these questions help show what you would actually get:

  • Which parts of the work will you do yourselves, and which would you expect another discipline or supplier to do?
  • Can you show examples of work taken through to production, not just concepts or renders?
  • How do you work with electronics engineers and manufacturers, and who manages those hand-offs?
  • What will you hand over at the end of each stage, and in what file formats?
  • Who will actually do the work, and who makes design decisions day to day?

Worked example: a consumer gadget

Illustrative example. A founder has an idea for a small, rechargeable desk gadget that sits in view and is handled several times a day. It has a button, a light and a simple mechanism. Appearance matters because it will sell partly on how it looks in photographs.

Stage Skills the founder needs Why
Concept Industrial design to explore form, size and interaction; a short engineering feasibility check of the mechanism, battery and likely processes So the favoured concept is one that can work and be made, not just one that looks good on paper
Prototype Mechanical engineering for architecture and the mechanism; an electronics partner for the board, battery and charging; industrial design refining looks-like models and testing them with users Function and handling are tested in parallel, then combined in an integrated prototype
Production Engineering for DFM, tolerances and drawings; manufacturer and toolmaker reviews; industrial design approving colour and finish samples So the parts that come off the tool match the design that was tested and the look that was promised

The founder could hire each skill separately, or work with someone who spans industrial design and engineering and brings in electronics and manufacturing partners as needed. Either way, one shared requirements document and regular joint reviews keep the disciplines pointing at the same product.

Common mistakes

Polishing looks before the architecture works. Detailed renders and CMF work on a shape that cannot fit the internals end up being redone. Prove the layout roughly before refining the surface.
Engineering a product nobody wants to use. A product can be robust and cheap to make yet awkward to hold, confusing to start or unappealing on the shelf. Keep users and appearance in the reviews throughout.
Hand-offs without shared requirements. Each discipline then optimises for its own idea of success, and the conflicts surface late, when changes are most expensive.
Assuming the factory will "sort out" design for manufacture. A manufacturer may adjust parts to suit its process, but not necessarily your product, cost target or appearance. DFM decisions need an owner on your side before tooling.

Decision rules

  • If people will choose the product partly on how it looks or feels, involve industrial design from the start, not as a finishing step.
  • If the product has moving parts, loads, seals or tight internal space, bring engineering in at concept, not after the shape is fixed.
  • If it contains electronics, agree board size, battery and antenna space with the mechanical layout before the form is frozen.
  • If two or more parties are involved, write down the requirements and who owns which decisions before work starts.
  • Before tooling, have an engineer on your side review DFM, tolerances and the manufacturer's suggested changes.

FAQ

What is the difference between industrial design and engineering design?

Industrial design focuses on how a product looks, feels and is used: form, ergonomics, interaction, colour, material and finish, and brand. Engineering design focuses on how it works and is made: architecture, mechanisms, materials, tolerances and design for manufacture. Most products need both, and the work overlaps.

Do I need an industrial designer and an engineer?

Most physical products need both kinds of skill, but not always two people. Some designers and engineers work across both, and the balance depends on the product: appearance-led consumer products lean on industrial design, while mechanism-heavy or technical products lean on engineering.

What is a product design engineer?

A product design engineer usually works across industrial design and engineering: considering how a product is used and how it looks, while also designing the mechanical architecture, materials, tolerances and assembly so that it can be manufactured. The exact scope varies between people and companies.

Who should I hire first?

Start with whoever addresses your biggest unknown. If the risk is whether people want and can use it, start with industrial design and user testing; if the risk is whether it can work or be made at the target cost, start with engineering. Bring the other in early rather than at the end.

Can the manufacturer do the engineering design?

Some manufacturers offer design services, and their process knowledge is valuable. But their priorities are not always yours, and you may have less control over the design and its ownership. Keep requirements, key decisions and design files under your own control, and have suggested changes reviewed against your requirements.
