How engineers choose between concepts.

Good concept selection is not about defending a favourite idea. It is about comparing options against the same criteria, surfacing the real trade-offs, and deciding what needs to be de-risked next.

Quick answer

Engineers choose between concepts by scoring each option against the same agreed, weighted criteria, then testing the assumptions behind the leader.

Concept decision = agreed criteria + honest weights + evidence-based scores + a plan to de-risk the leading option

A Pugh matrix or weighted scoring matrix makes the trade-offs visible. It does not make the decision for you: its real output is a shortlist and a clear view of what still needs to be proven before more design effort is committed.

Teams rarely struggle because they have no ideas. They struggle because several ideas remain plausible at the same time and each stakeholder is weighting success differently. The founder may be thinking about the customer, the engineer about technical risk, and the investor about unit cost. That is when a structured concept-selection matrix becomes useful.

Better question: not “which concept wins?” but “which concept is strongest under the criteria we actually agree matter, and what evidence would change that answer?”

How concept selection works in practice

Most engineering teams use two passes. The first is concept screening, often done with a Pugh matrix: one concept, or an existing product, is chosen as the reference (the datum), and every other concept is marked better (+), the same (0) or worse (−) against each criterion. Screening is quick and is good at removing weak options and spotting strengths that could be combined.

The second pass is concept scoring. The remaining two to four concepts are rated on a scale, typically 1 to 5, and each criterion carries a weight that reflects its importance to the project. Multiplying scores by weights gives a total that is easier to discuss, as long as everyone remembers that the precision is only as good as the inputs.

What a strong decision matrix includes

  • stable criteria used across every concept, agreed before anyone scores
  • weights that reflect the real project priorities and add up to 100%
  • scores grounded in evidence such as tests, quotes, user sessions or calculations, not just preference
  • a note of the confidence behind each score, so weak evidence is visible
  • a follow-up view of what the matrix does not yet know

Typical criteria for a physical product

Criterion What it asks Evidence that supports a score
User value How well does the concept solve the problem the customer actually has? User interviews, rough prototype trials, observed workarounds
Technical feasibility Can it be made to work reliably with known technology? Calculations, rig tests, supplier data sheets
Manufacturability Can it be made repeatably with sensible processes and tolerances? Early DFM review, supplier conversations, part count
Unit cost Does the likely cost leave room for the target selling price? Rough BOM, process estimates, indicative quotes
Development risk How many unknowns stand between the concept and a working product? Count of unproven functions, dependence on new suppliers or technology
Compliance What standards or approvals will it need? Initial regulatory screen for safety, EMC, radio or food contact
Lifetime and repair Will it last, and can it be serviced when parts wear? Wear-part analysis, fastening strategy, battery access

Five to seven criteria is usually enough. More than that and the weights become thin, the scoring slows down, and criteria start to overlap.

Worked example: three concepts for a rechargeable bike light (illustrative)

Imagine a startup with three concepts for a clip-on rechargeable rear bike light. Concept A is a sealed one-piece moulded housing with a built-in battery. Concept B uses a two-part housing with a user-replaceable battery. Concept C puts a bought-in LED and battery module inside a simple custom shell. The figures below are illustrative, not data from a real project.

Criterion (weight) A: sealed housing B: replaceable battery C: bought-in module
User value (30%) 4 4 3
Manufacturability (20%) 4 3 4
Unit cost (20%) 4 3 5
Development risk (15%) 3 3 5
Lifetime and repair (15%) 2 5 2
Weighted total 3.55 3.60 3.75

On paper Concept C leads. But the totals sit within 0.2 of each other, which is well inside the uncertainty of the scores. A quick sensitivity check makes the point: raise user value to 40% and drop unit cost to 10%, and Concept B moves to the top with 3.70 while A and C tie on 3.55. The matrix is telling the team that the decision hinges on how much customers value brightness, mounting and battery life against price.

One concept might lead on customer value but lag on manufacturability. Another may be technically safer but commercially weaker. The right response is often to identify the best de-risking experiment, not to declare the discussion over. Here, that might be a short user test with rough prototypes of B and C, plus an indicative quote for the bought-in module at the expected volumes.

Common concept-selection mistakes

Changing the criteria mid-discussion. That usually means the team is trying to defend a preferred solution after the fact.
Weighting everything highly. If every criterion is critical, none of them are helping make the decision.
Scoring as a group from the start. The loudest voice anchors everyone else. Score individually first, then discuss the differences.
Double counting. Criteria such as “part count” and “assembly cost” often measure the same thing and quietly inflate one concept.
Ignoring hybrids. The best answer is often a combination, such as the low-risk electronics of one concept inside the better housing of another.
Treating the output as final truth. The matrix should guide the next test, not replace engineering judgement.

A concept matrix is most useful when it shows the team where it disagrees. The disagreement is usually where the next experiment belongs.

What to do after the matrix

  1. Name the leading concept and a credible runner-up, so there is a fallback if testing goes badly.
  2. List the assumptions behind the leader’s highest scores, starting with the ones that carry the most weight.
  3. Design the cheapest test that could prove each assumption wrong: a rough prototype, a calculation, a supplier quote or a user session.
  4. Set a date to rescore with the new evidence, using the same criteria and weights.
  5. Record the decision and the reasoning, so later design changes can be traced back to it.

If the problem itself is still uncertain, step back before scoring concepts. The guide on how to validate a product idea covers the evidence to collect first.

FAQ

What is a Pugh matrix in product design?

A Pugh matrix compares several design concepts against a reference concept, called the datum, across a fixed set of criteria. Each concept is marked better, the same or worse than the datum for each criterion. It is a fast way to screen out weak options and spot strengths worth combining before more detailed scoring.

How many concepts should you compare in a selection matrix?

Screening can handle a wider set, but detailed weighted scoring works best with around two to four serious concepts. Beyond that, the scoring takes longer than the insight it produces. Use quick screening to cut the list down first.

How do you choose weights for concept selection criteria?

Agree the weights before scoring, based on what the project genuinely needs to succeed, and make sure they add up to 100%. A useful check is to ask which criterion you would sacrifice first if forced. Then test whether reasonable changes to the weights change the winner.

What is the difference between concept screening and concept scoring?

Screening uses simple better, same or worse judgements against a reference to narrow a long list quickly. Scoring uses weighted numerical ratings on a short list to compare finalists in more detail. Most teams screen first and score second.

What should you do if two concepts score almost the same?

Treat a close result as a sign that the evidence is not yet strong enough to separate them. Identify the criteria driving the difference and run a quick test, prototype or supplier enquiry to firm up those scores. Look for a hybrid that keeps the strengths of both.
