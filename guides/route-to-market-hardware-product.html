How route to market shapes product design and cost.

A retail price on its own tells you very little. What matters is how much of that price survives the route to market, and how much disappears into warehousing, packaging, logistics, platform fees, marketplace charges, and retailer margin.

Quick answer

Route to market matters because it changes the COGS target the product must hit.

Target COGS = retained price - channel deductions - hidden selling costs - required margin

The same retail price can support very different factory costs depending on whether the product is sold DTC, through a marketplace, or through retail. Retailers commonly keep 30 to 50 per cent of the retail price, while direct sales keep more of the price but carry acquisition, fulfilment and returns costs, so the channel choice should shape the product brief from the start.

Founders and product teams often ask whether the product can sell for a certain RRP. The harder question is whether the product still works once the chosen route to market takes its share. That is where many products become commercially weak without anyone noticing early enough.

Core point: route to market is not a downstream sales decision. It directly changes the COGS target the product must hit.

The same RRP can support very different COGS

Route What you keep What usually gets missed
Direct to consumer Most of the retail price Site fees, payment charges, warehousing, pick and pack, customer service, returns, and paid acquisition
Marketplace Less than DTC, often more than wholesale Marketplace commission, promoted listings, fulfilment surcharges, and reduced control over pricing
Retailer The smallest share of the RRP Wholesale discount, account management, chargebacks, retail-ready packaging, and inbound logistics

Routes also change when you get paid

Margin is only half of the route decision. Each route also changes cash timing and stock commitment. Direct sales are usually paid at the point of order. Marketplaces typically pay out on a regular settlement cycle and may require stock to be held in their warehouses. Retailers commonly buy on credit terms of 30 to 90 days, may expect larger and earlier stock commitments, and can apply deductions for promotions or non-compliant deliveries. A route with acceptable margin can still strain cash, which is why route choice and the cash flow model should be reviewed together.

Why hidden costs matter so much

Hidden costs are usually not hidden because they are rare. They are hidden because they sit in different teams or budgets. Operations owns warehousing. Marketing owns customer acquisition. Ecommerce owns hosting and transaction fees. Sales owns retailer terms. The product still has to carry all of it.

Worked example: what happens when the route mix shifts

Illustrative figures only. A kitchen product has an RRP of GBP120 including VAT, so GBP100 net, and a landed COGS of GBP30. The business plans to sell 50% direct, 30% through a marketplace and 20% through a retailer. Fees and costs below are plausible assumptions, not quotes.

Contribution per unit = retained price - hidden selling costs - landed COGS
Route Retained price Hidden selling costs Contribution per unit
Direct to consumer GBP100 GBP36 (payment, fulfilment, returns, acquisition) GBP34
Marketplace GBP82 (15% commission on GBP120) GBP20 (fulfilment, advertising, returns) GBP32
Retailer GBP55 (45% retailer margin) GBP7 (retail packaging, inbound freight, promotional support) GBP18

At the planned 50/30/20 mix, blended contribution is about GBP30.20 per unit. Now suppose a large retail listing arrives and the mix becomes 25% direct, 15% marketplace and 60% retail. Blended contribution falls to about GBP24.10, a drop of roughly 20%, even though more units may be sold. To restore the original contribution at the new mix, landed COGS would need to fall by about GBP6.10, to roughly GBP23.90.

Notice also that direct sales only narrowly beat the marketplace here, because acquisition cost absorbs much of the advantage of keeping the full price. That is why the right answer depends on the real cost stack, not on channel mythology.

A retail listing can be a commercial success and a margin problem at the same time. Plan for both before it arrives.

Common route-to-market mistakes

Choosing the route for volume, then fixing the margin later. Once tooling and the bill of materials are fixed, there is little room left to recover a missing 20%.
Assuming direct sales are cheap. Direct sales keep more of the price, but paid acquisition, returns and customer service can absorb most of that gain.
Ignoring price consistency across channels. Retailers and marketplaces generally expect the direct price not to undercut them, which limits how differently each route can be priced.
Packaging designed for one channel only. Retail-ready packaging, marketplace requirements and direct-mail shipping often need different solutions. Retrofitting adds cost.
Overlooking payment terms. A route with good margin and slow payment can still starve the business of cash.

Questions worth asking before you lock the design

  1. If this product needs to work through retail, what wholesale price does that imply?
  2. What level of packaging, fulfilment, and account cost is really required for each route?
  3. Does the product architecture leave enough room for a sensible COGS at the target route?
  4. Would a channel shift force a redesign later because the product cost is too high?
  5. Can the business finance the stock and payment terms the route requires?

Use route-to-market thinking to guide engineering

Route-to-market choice should influence the brief early. If the likely route is retail, the product may need a much more aggressive cost-down strategy, fewer parts, simpler assembly, or a different specification. If the likely route is DTC, the product may be able to carry more value and margin, but only if acquisition and fulfilment do not absorb the gain.

FAQ

What is a route to market for a hardware product?

It is the path a product takes from the business to the end customer, such as selling direct through your own website, through an online marketplace, through retailers, or through distributors. Each route has different fees, costs, payment terms and stock requirements.

Is direct to consumer or retail better for a hardware startup?

Neither is better in general. Direct sales keep more of the price and give closer customer contact, but need marketing spend and fulfilment capability. Retail offers reach and credibility, but takes a large share of the price and often demands longer payment terms and more stock.

How much margin do retailers take?

Retailer margins commonly sit somewhere between 30 and 50 per cent of the retail price before VAT, depending on the category and the retailer. Promotional contributions and other deductions can add to that, so check the full terms rather than the headline margin.

How much do online marketplaces charge?

Most charge a commission on each sale, often in the region of 8 to 15 per cent depending on category, plus optional fulfilment, storage and advertising fees. Rates change, so check the current fee schedule for your category before setting a cost target.

Can I change route to market after launch?

Yes, but moving to a route that keeps less of the price can leave the product with too little margin. Setting the cost target from the most demanding route you realistically expect avoids being forced into a redesign later.
