Supply agreements with a manufacturer: what to cover

Why the relationship with your manufacturer belongs in writing, when to agree it, and the topics a supply agreement usually needs to address, explained in plain terms so you can prepare well and get more from a solicitor's time.

Quick answer

Agree the relationship in writing before you pay for tooling or place the first production order, and make sure it covers more than price.

Cover what "good" looks like (specification, approved samples, inspection, rejects), who owns the tools and data and how you get them back, how pricing, orders, delivery and payment work, how changes are controlled, and who is responsible for faults, compliance and insurance. This guide is general information, not legal advice: use it to prepare, then have a solicitor draft or review the agreement.

Why put it in writing, and when

Many small brands run first production on quotes and purchase orders alone. A purchase order says what you are buying this time, at what price and by when. It rarely says who owns the mould you paid for, what happens when a batch fails inspection, or how you move production elsewhere. Those questions become urgent only when something goes wrong, the worst time to negotiate them.

A supply agreement sets the rules for the whole relationship, so each purchase order only deals with quantities, dates and prices. It need not be adversarial: a clear agreement helps a good supplier too, because it removes guesswork.

The right moment is before you pay for tooling and before the first production order. Once a tool is paid for and your launch depends on the next delivery, you have far less room to ask. The main points are often agreed in outline while comparing manufacturing quotes, then written up once you have chosen the supplier.

Important: this guide is general information, not legal advice. Contract law and the right approach differ between countries and situations, especially with an overseas supplier. Have a solicitor draft or review the agreement, and use this guide to arrive knowing what you need.

Specification, samples and quality

Many disputes with a manufacturer come back to what was ordered. The agreement should point to a controlled specification: drawings with revision numbers, materials and grades, finishes and colours, cosmetic standards, packaging and labelling. It should also refer to approved samples, often called golden samples, signed off by both sides and kept as the physical reference for appearance and fit.

Then cover how quality is checked: first-article inspection before production, inspection during and after production, who carries it out (you, the supplier or an inspection company), the acceptance criteria, and what happens to rejects, including who pays for rework, replacement, scrap and freight on faulty parts. Quality control and first-article inspection explains the checks themselves.

Tooling: ownership, storage, maintenance and transfer

For moulded or cast parts, tooling is often the largest early payment and what ties you to a supplier. As comparing quotes notes, ownership is whatever the contract says: some suppliers transfer it once the tool is paid for, some keep it, and some tie it to an order volume. Write down:

  • Ownership: who owns the tool, and from when (on full payment, for example).
  • Identification and storage: that the tool is marked as yours, where it is kept, and that it will not be used for anyone else's parts.
  • Maintenance and life: who pays for routine maintenance, repairs and refurbishment, and what happens when the tool wears out. Injection moulding explained covers how tool steel and design affect tool life.
  • Insurance: who insures the tool while it is at the supplier's premises.
  • Transfer: how you can collect or move the tool, how much notice is needed, and whether any costs apply, such as packing or outstanding invoices.

IP, confidentiality and supplier design changes

An NDA protects information you share while talking to suppliers. It is a sensible start, and the NDA Generator can produce a draft to review, but an NDA is not a supply agreement. It usually says nothing about tooling, quality, orders or exit.

The supply agreement should confirm that your design, drawings and data remain yours, and say who owns anything created during the relationship, such as supplier design changes, tooling data, fixtures and test software. It should also say whether the supplier may change materials, processes, sub-suppliers or factory without your approval. How to protect product IP covers staged disclosure and ownership of supplier design work, and design registration vs patents explains the rights you may want to secure before sharing.

Pricing, orders, delivery and payment

  • Pricing, price reviews and currency. What the price includes, how long it holds, when it can be reviewed (for example, if material costs move), and the currency. Paying in a foreign currency leaves exchange-rate risk with you unless agreed otherwise.
  • Forecasts, firm orders and MOQs. Separate a forecast, which helps the supplier plan, from a firm order, which commits you to pay. Agree minimum order quantities, when orders become firm, and whether you pay for materials bought against a forecast. Minimum order quantities and cash shows why these commitments matter so much to a small business.
  • Lead times and delivery terms. Lead times for samples, first and repeat orders, and what happens when delivery is late. State the delivery term using the Incoterms rules with a named place, so it is clear where cost and risk pass to you. Landed cost: freight, duty and VAT explains what you still pay after that point.
  • Payment terms. Deposits, stage payments and when the balance is due, ideally linked to milestones you can verify, such as a passed inspection. Calculating hardware start-up cash flow helps you see when each payment hits your bank account.

Change control

Products change after launch: a fix, a cost reduction, a component going obsolete. The agreement should say how either side proposes a change, who approves it, how cost and timing are agreed, and when new samples or first articles are needed. Above all, the supplier should not change anything affecting form, fit, function or compliance without your written approval. After launch: returns, feedback and design changes covers how to run changes on your side.

Liability, warranty, insurance and compliance

If you sell under your own brand or import the product, you may be treated as its producer, as product liability basics explains. The agreement cannot remove those duties, but it can set out how responsibility is shared: the supplier's warranty on its work, what happens if a manufacturing defect leads to returns or a recall, any limits on liability, and what insurance each side holds. This is where a solicitor's and an insurance broker's input matters most.

Compliance responsibilities belong here too. Agree who provides material declarations, test reports, certificates for bought-in components and traceability records such as batch or date codes, and how long records are kept. The detail depends on your product and market; product certification basics gives a general overview of what conformity involves.

Ending the relationship

The ending is easy to forget while everyone is getting along. Cover how long the agreement lasts and how either side can end it, with what notice. Then cover the practical exit: open orders, paying for materials and finished stock, and above all returning your tools, fixtures, samples and files in working order, on a reasonable timetable. Without a route to get your tools back, a better supplier is of little use to you.

Worked example: tooling a moulded product overseas

Illustrative and qualitative. A small brand has chosen an overseas supplier for a two-part moulded product and is about to pay a tooling deposit. With limited time and legal budget, it decides what matters most before asking a solicitor to draft the agreement.

  • Tooling first. The tool is the largest early payment and hard to replace, so ownership, storage, maintenance and the right to collect it come top.
  • Specification and approved samples. The brand cannot visit often, so it relies on controlled drawings, signed golden samples and pre-shipment inspection, with a clear rule on who pays when a batch fails.
  • Change control. The supplier proposes helpful moulding tweaks. The brand welcomes them, but no material, colour or geometry change happens without written approval and new samples.
  • Orders and payment. The brand gives a forecast but keeps firm orders to what it can afford, and links payments to sample approval and passed inspection rather than paying everything up front.
  • Exit. It agrees how the tool, samples and files come back if it moves production, so the relationship can end cleanly if it needs to.

Price reviews, currency and liability still go into the agreement, but the brand spends its preparation time where a gap would be hardest to recover from.

Common mistakes

No written tooling ownership. Paying for a tool does not by itself make it yours to move. If it is not in writing, assume you cannot take it elsewhere.
Relying on an NDA alone. An NDA covers confidential information. It does not cover quality, tooling, ownership of new work, orders or exit.
Vague specifications. "As per sample" or an uncontrolled drawing leaves every quality argument open. Point to revision-controlled documents and signed samples.
No route to get tools and files back. Without agreed terms for returning tools and files, leaving a supplier can mean starting again.

Decision rules

  • Agree the terms before money is committed: before the tooling payment and the first production order.
  • Define "good" in documents and samples, not in conversations.
  • Treat tooling as an asset you need to own, find, maintain and move.
  • Separate forecasts from firm commitments, and link payments to verifiable milestones.
  • Plan the exit while relations are good, and have a solicitor draft or review the agreement.

FAQ

Do I need a supply agreement if I already have purchase orders?

A purchase order covers one order: what, how many, what price and when. A supply agreement sets the rules for the whole relationship, including quality, rejects, tooling ownership, changes, liability and how it ends, which purchase orders rarely address.

When should I agree a supply agreement?

Before you pay for tooling and before the first production order. Agree the main points in outline while comparing quotes, then write them up properly once you have chosen the supplier, while you still have room to negotiate.

Is an NDA enough when working with a manufacturer?

No. An NDA covers confidential information shared during discussions. It does not usually deal with specifications, quality, tooling, ownership of new work, pricing, orders or ending the relationship, which a supply agreement should cover.

Who owns the tooling I pay for?

Whoever the agreement says. Some suppliers transfer ownership once the tool is paid for, others keep it or tie it to an order volume. Put ownership, storage, maintenance, insurance and your right to move the tool in writing.

Can I write a supply agreement myself?

You can prepare for it by deciding what you need on each topic, which makes a solicitor's work quicker and better targeted. Contract law differs between countries and situations, so have a solicitor draft or review the agreement, especially with an overseas supplier.
