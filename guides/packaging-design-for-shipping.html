Packaging design for shipping: protecting products from factory to customer

How to design packaging that protects a product all the way from the factory to the customer: mapping the journey, designing the layers, fitting cartons and pallets, testing before production, and meeting sustainability and producer responsibilities.

Quick answer

Design packaging for the whole journey, from factory to customer, not just the shelf: protect the product, fit the shipping system, sell the product, and use as little material as possible.

Start early, because pack size affects freight cost, pallet and carton counts, and sometimes the product itself. Work out how the product will travel, which drops, vibration, compression and temperatures it will meet, and design inner protection and outer cartons to suit. Test with recognised transit tests before production, and check your packaging responsibilities, which in the UK include extended producer responsibility for many businesses.

What packaging has to do

Job What it means
Protect Survive drops, vibration, stacking, humidity and temperature on the way to the customer
Fit the system Work with carton, pallet and container sizes, parcel carriers and warehouse handling
Sell and inform Retail presence or a good unboxing, plus required labels, barcodes, safety and disposal information
Minimise waste and cost Right-sized, with as little and as simple material as the protection allows

Map the journey

List each stage: factory, container or air freight, warehouse, retailer or fulfilment centre, parcel carrier, customer. Each has its own hazards. Products sold by parcel direct to customers are handled individually and dropped more often than products shipped on pallets to shops, and online marketplaces and retailers may have their own packaging requirements. Ask them early; retail-ready packaging and barcodes covers what retailers commonly ask for.

Design the layers

  • Primary pack: the box the customer sees. It may be the shipping box too, for direct-to-customer products.
  • Inner protection: moulded pulp, folded card or foam inserts that hold the product away from the walls and stop it moving.
  • Shipper carton: holds several units for freight; its strength must suit how high cartons will be stacked.
  • Pallet: carton sizes that fill a pallet well reduce freight cost and damage.
Practical rule: decide the pack size before you finalise the product. A few millimetres can change how many units fit a carton or pallet, which affects every shipment's cost.

Test before production

Transit test procedures, such as those published by the International Safe Transit Association (ISTA), simulate drops, vibration and compression in a repeatable way. Test packaging samples with real products inside, and include it in your test plan. Fix weak points before ordering packaging in volume.

Sustainability and responsibilities

  • Right-size the pack and avoid void fill where you can.
  • Use materials that are widely recycled, and avoid mixing materials that cannot be separated.
  • Mark materials and disposal guidance clearly.
  • Check your obligations: in the UK, many businesses that supply packaging have extended producer responsibility for packaging duties, depending on size and role.

Packaging also adds to the product's footprint; see the Carbon Footprint Estimator.

Cost

Packaging carries tooling for inserts and print set-up, a unit cost and minimum order quantities of its own, and it changes freight cost through size and weight. Include it in the unit cost with the Product Cost Estimator and in landed cost with the Landed Cost Calculator.

Common mistakes

Leaving packaging until last. It can delay launch and leave an oversized, expensive pack.
Designing for the shelf only. Retail boxes that look good may not survive parcel delivery.
No transit testing. Damage found by customers costs far more than a test.
Ignoring pallet and carton fit. Poor fit means paying to ship air.

FAQ

When should packaging design start?

Early, alongside the product. Pack size affects how many units fit a carton and pallet, freight cost and sometimes the product design, and packaging has its own lead times, tooling and minimum orders.

How do I test packaging for shipping?

Use recognised transit test procedures, such as those published by ISTA, which simulate drops, vibration and compression. Test real products in production-representative packaging before ordering in volume.

Is direct-to-customer packaging different from retail packaging?

Usually. Parcels are handled individually and dropped more, so they need more protection, while retail packs are mainly shipped in cartons on pallets and need shelf presence. Some products need both.

What are my packaging responsibilities in the UK?

Many businesses that supply packaging have extended producer responsibility duties, depending on their size and role. Check the current official guidance on GOV.UK for what applies to you.

How can I make packaging more sustainable?

Right-size it, use less material, choose widely recycled materials, avoid mixing materials that cannot be separated, and label disposal clearly, while still protecting the product, because damage wastes far more.
