How to turn RRP into target COGS.

If you know the retail price the market can support, you can work backwards to the COGS the product needs. The mistake is stopping too early and forgetting how much of that price gets consumed before profit appears.

Quick answer

Target COGS is the amount left after VAT, route costs, hidden selling costs, and the margin the business needs to keep.

Target COGS = net selling price - channel deductions - selling costs - required margin

Start from the RRP, remove VAT, remove what the retailer or marketplace keeps, remove fulfilment, acquisition and returns costs, then remove the margin the business needs. What remains is the most the product can cost to make and land. Working backwards like this gives founders and engineers a clearer cost target than treating the retail price as if it belongs to the factory.

Product teams often set a target retail price and then ask engineering to make the cost work. That only becomes useful once the target COGS is derived properly. Retail price is not factory value. It has to absorb VAT, route-to-market fees, warehousing, packaging, logistics, selling costs, and the margin the business needs to keep.

Practical rule: target COGS is what is left after everyone else has been paid and the business still has a worthwhile margin.

Operational selling costWarehousing, pick and pack, secondary packaging, outbound logisticsThese costs rise with every unit sold
Commercial costSite and payment fees, paid acquisition, trade spend, account supportThese are often split across teams and missed in the product economics
Business marginThe profit needed to fund growth, overhead, and future product workIf nothing is left here, the product is not commercially healthy

A simple way to think about it

  1. Remove VAT if the RRP includes it. UK consumer prices normally do, and the standard rate is 20%, so divide by 1.2.
  2. Remove the route-to-market share you do not keep.
  3. Remove the hidden selling and fulfilment costs attached to that route.
  4. Remove the margin the business needs to keep.
  5. What remains is the maximum landed COGS the product can afford.

Margin and markup are not the same

Margin = (price - cost) / price. Markup = (price - cost) / cost.

A product that costs GBP50 and sells for GBP100 has a 50% margin but a 100% markup. Suppliers and retailers sometimes talk in different terms, so confirm which one is meant before agreeing a number. Confusing the two is one of the quickest ways to set a cost target that is far too generous.

Worked example: one RRP, three routes

Illustrative figures only. A home product has an RRP of GBP180 including VAT, so the net retail price is GBP150. The business wants to keep 30% of the revenue it actually receives as margin to cover overhead and future development. Fees and costs below are plausible assumptions, not quotes. The marketplace commission here is charged on the VAT-inclusive price; the Target COGS tool applies its fee to the price before VAT, which leaves slightly more.

Step Direct to consumer Marketplace Retailer
Net of VAT GBP150.00 GBP150.00 GBP150.00
Channel take None 15% commission on GBP180: GBP27.00 45% retailer margin: GBP67.50
Revenue to the business GBP150.00 GBP123.00 GBP82.50
Selling costs GBP43.00 (payment fees, fulfilment, returns, acquisition) GBP27.50 (fulfilment, advertising, returns) GBP8.13 (retail-ready packaging, inbound freight, promotional support)
Required margin at 30% GBP45.00 GBP36.90 GBP24.75
Target landed COGS GBP62.00 GBP58.60 GBP49.62

The same GBP180 product can afford about GBP62 of landed cost when sold direct, but under GBP50 through retail. If the product is engineered to the direct-sales target and later needs to go into retail, it may need cost-down work or redesign because the original cost target was based on the wrong commercial model. Note too that direct sales only support the higher figure if acquisition cost stays under control. If paid acquisition doubles, the direct target falls sharply.

Design to the cost target of the most demanding route you realistically expect to use, not the most generous one.

Why this matters for design teams

  • It sets a clearer brief for cost-down and design-for-manufacture work.
  • It shows whether the route-to-market idea and the product architecture fit each other.
  • It helps avoid late commercial surprises that force rushed redesign.
  • It gives founders and engineers a shared number to design toward.

A target COGS can then be split into a budget for each major sub-assembly: enclosure, electronics, packaging, assembly labour. That turns a commercial number into engineering decisions about part count, materials, finishes and supplier choice. The guide to estimating manufacturing cost shows how to build the bottom-up figure to compare against it.

Common mistakes when working back from RRP

Forgetting VAT. Treating a VAT-inclusive RRP as revenue overstates what the business receives by a sixth at the standard rate.
Comparing against ex-works price. Target COGS should be compared with landed cost: factory price plus freight, insurance, duty and packaging.
Confusing margin and markup. A 50% markup is only a 33% margin. Mixing them up can hide a large gap.
Leaving out acquisition cost for direct sales. Direct sales keep more of the price, but advertising and returns can absorb much of that advantage.
Setting the target once. Fees, freight and channel terms change. Revisit the target at each major design review.

Practical next steps

  1. Agree the realistic RRP range with evidence from comparable products.
  2. Decide which routes to market are likely in the first two years, and which is the most demanding.
  3. Work back to target landed COGS for each route using the Target COGS by Route to Market tool.
  4. Compare the target with a bottom-up estimate from the Product Cost Estimator.
  5. If the gap is large, decide early whether to change the specification, the price or the route. The guide to how route to market shapes product design and cost covers that choice in more depth.

FAQ

How do you calculate target COGS from RRP?

Remove VAT from the RRP, then subtract the channel's share, the selling and fulfilment costs for that route, and the margin the business needs to keep. The remainder is the maximum landed cost the product can carry. Repeat it for each route you expect to use.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. Markup is profit as a percentage of cost. A product that costs GBP50 and sells for GBP100 has a 50% margin and a 100% markup.

What percentage of RRP should COGS be?

It depends on the route, category and selling costs, so there is no single answer. A common rule of thumb for consumer products sold through retail is an RRP of roughly three to five times landed cost. Direct sales can support a higher share, provided acquisition and fulfilment costs are included.

Should target COGS include freight and duty?

Yes. Compare the target with landed cost, which includes the factory price plus freight, insurance, import duty and packaging. Comparing against the ex-works factory price makes the product look cheaper than it really is.

Does RRP include VAT in the UK?

Prices shown to UK consumers normally include VAT, which is 20% at the standard rate. Some products qualify for reduced or zero rates, so check the rate for your category before working back to a cost target.
