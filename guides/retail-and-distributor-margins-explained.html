Retail and distributor margins explained: from shelf price to what you receive

How margins work when a physical product is sold through retailers and distributors: the difference between margin and markup, how to work back from the shelf price, and the terms beyond the headline margin that affect what you actually keep.

Quick answer

Each business in the chain takes its margin as a share of its own selling price, so work backwards from the shelf price to see what you receive.

Margin = (selling price − buying price) ÷ selling price Markup = (selling price − buying price) ÷ buying price Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

Retailers and distributors usually talk in margin, while many founders think in markup, and confusing the two is a common source of pricing mistakes. Start from the shelf price, remove VAT, then remove each party's margin in turn to find what you will be paid. Then check that covers your landed cost and your own margin, plus any promotions, marketing contributions and payment terms the buyer asks for.

Margin and markup

Margin Equivalent markup Selling price if the buying price is £10
20% 25% £12.50
25% 33.3% £13.33
40% 66.7% £16.67
50% 100% £20.00
60% 150% £25.00

When a buyer asks for "a 50% margin", they mean half of their selling price, which is double your price to them, not 50% more.

The chain from factory to shelf

Stage What happens
Landed cost What each unit costs you once made, packed, shipped, duty-paid and in your warehouse; see landed cost
Your margin Must cover overheads, development, marketing, returns and profit
Distributor (if used) Buys from you, stocks and sells to retailers; takes a margin for logistics, sales reach and credit risk
Retailer Buys from you or the distributor and sets the shelf price; takes a margin for space, staff, marketing and risk
VAT Added to the shelf price for consumers; see current VAT rates

Margins vary widely by category, retailer and the services each party provides, so ask buyers and distributors directly rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

Illustrative figures only. A product sells at £39.99 including 20% VAT, so £33.33 before VAT. A retailer taking a 40% margin pays £20.00. A distributor taking a 25% margin pays the brand £15.00. With a landed cost of £8.00, the brand keeps £7.00, a 46.7% margin, from which overheads, marketing and returns must be paid. Selling direct to the retailer instead, the brand would receive £20.00. The retailer's 40% margin is a 66.7% markup, and the distributor's 25% is a 33.3% markup.

Practical rule: build your price from the shelf down, not from your cost up. If the shelf price customers will pay cannot support every margin in the chain, change the cost, the route or the product.

Beyond the headline margin

  • Payment terms: buyers often pay weeks after delivery, which affects your cash; model it with the Cash Flow Projection.
  • Promotions: discounts are frequently funded partly or wholly by the supplier.
  • Marketing contributions: some retailers ask for fees or co-funded marketing for listings or displays.
  • Returns and damages: agree who pays for faulty, damaged or unsold stock.
  • Sale or return: occasionally offered to new brands, but you carry the stock risk until it sells.
  • Settlement discounts: a small discount for early payment can help cash flow.

Agree these in writing; they can take more of the price than the headline margin suggests. Retailers also set packaging, barcode and outer case requirements of their own; see retail-ready packaging and barcodes.

Common mistakes

Confusing margin and markup. A "40% markup" leaves far less room than a buyer's "40% margin" requires.
Forgetting VAT. Margins are worked on prices before VAT.
Pricing direct sales below retail. Undercutting your retailers damages the relationship; see how to price a physical product.
Ignoring the extras. Promotions, contributions and terms can cut your real margin significantly.

FAQ

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a share of the selling price; markup is profit as a share of the buying price. A 50% margin equals a 100% markup. Retailers and distributors usually quote margin.

How do I convert margin to markup?

Markup equals margin divided by one minus the margin. For example, a 40% margin is 0.4 ÷ 0.6, a 66.7% markup.

What margin do retailers expect?

It varies widely by category, retailer and the services involved, so ask buyers directly. Work backwards from the shelf price to check that every margin in the chain still leaves you enough.

Do I need a distributor?

Not always. Distributors add reach, logistics and credit management in return for a margin. Selling direct to retailers keeps more of the price but means more sales and admin work for you.

Are margins calculated before or after VAT?

Before VAT. Remove VAT from the shelf price first, then work back through each party's margin.
