Freedom to operate explained: checking you can make and sell your product

An overview of freedom to operate: how it differs from protecting your own idea, which rights can block a product, how to make an early informal check, when to get a formal opinion and what to do if you find a problem.

Quick answer

Freedom to operate (FTO) asks whether you can make and sell your product without infringing someone else's rights, in the countries where you will make and sell it.

It is a different question from whether you can protect your own idea. Your product could be patentable and still infringe an older patent. An early, informal search helps you spot obvious problems and design around them; a formal FTO opinion from a patent attorney is worth considering before major investment such as tooling or launch. This guide is an overview, not legal advice.

Freedom to operate vs patentability

Patentability Freedom to operate
Question Is my invention new and inventive enough to protect? Can I make and sell this without infringing others' rights?
Looks at Everything published before your filing date, anywhere Rights that are in force, or may be granted, where you make or sell
Focus What is new about your idea Every feature of your actual product

What can block you

  • Patents in force in the countries where you make, import or sell, and published applications that may still be granted.
  • Registered designs covering the appearance of similar products.
  • Unregistered design rights and copyright, where you have copied another product.
  • Trade marks, for your product name and branding; see trade marks and product names.

Rights are territorial and time-limited: an expired patent, or one not in force in your markets, does not block you there. See design registration vs patents.

An early, informal check

  1. Break your product into its key features: mechanism, functions, shape, materials and methods.
  2. Search patents and published applications for those features, using the official patent search routes and international databases they link to.
  3. Search registered designs for similar-looking products.
  4. Note competitors' patents marked on their products and packaging.
  5. Record what you find and when, and flag anything close for professional review.
Practical rule: search early, while the design is still easy to change. Designing around a problem patent at concept stage is cheap; after tooling it is expensive.

When to get a formal opinion

Reading patent claims and judging infringement is specialist work, and an informal search cannot give certainty. Consider a formal FTO review by a registered patent attorney before committing to tooling, a large production order, a crowdfunding launch, or investment, and when entering a new market. Investors and retailers may ask what checks you have made.

If you find a problem

  • Check it properly: is it in force where you sell, and do its claims really cover your product?
  • Design around it: change the feature so the claims no longer apply.
  • License it from the owner.
  • Get advice on whether it could be challenged.

Common mistakes

Assuming your own patent gives freedom. Owning a patent does not stop you infringing someone else's.
Searching only for your main idea. Infringement can come from any feature of the product.
Leaving it until launch. Problems found late are the most expensive to fix.
Forgetting manufacturing countries. Making a product somewhere can infringe rights there, even if you sell elsewhere.

FAQ

What is freedom to operate?

Whether you can make, use and sell a product in particular countries without infringing someone else's patents, designs or other rights that are in force there.

Is freedom to operate the same as patentability?

No. Patentability asks whether your invention is new enough to protect; freedom to operate asks whether your product infringes others' rights. A product can be patentable and still infringe an older patent.

When should I check freedom to operate?

Start with an informal search early, while the design can still change, and consider a formal review by a patent attorney before major commitments such as tooling, launch, crowdfunding or investment.

Can I do a freedom to operate search myself?

You can do an informal search of patents and registered designs to spot obvious issues. Judging whether claims actually cover your product is specialist work, so get professional advice on anything close.

What if a patent covers my product?

Check it is in force where you make and sell and that its claims really apply, then consider designing around it, licensing it, or taking advice on whether it could be challenged.
