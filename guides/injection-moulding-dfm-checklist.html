DFM checklist for injection-moulded parts: walls, draft, ribs, bosses and undercuts

Use this checklist on your CAD model before asking for tooling quotes. It covers the features that most often cause sink marks, warping, sticking parts and expensive tooling, with typical starting values and the reason behind each rule.

Quick answer

Keep walls even, add draft, size ribs and bosses to the wall, round the corners, avoid undercuts, and agree cosmetic faces with the moulder.

Most injection-moulding problems, such as sink marks, warping, parts sticking in the tool and expensive side actions, come from a short list of design features. Checking those features before the tool is cut is one of the cheapest ways to reduce tooling cost, cycle time and rejected parts. The figures below are widely used starting points; the material datasheet and your moulder's design-for-manufacture review have the final say.

Why design for manufacture matters most in moulding

An injection-mould tool is machined from aluminium or steel to the exact shape of the part. Once it is cut, adding material to the part is usually easy (the toolmaker removes steel), but removing material from the part can mean welding and re-machining the tool, or making a new insert. Features that need moving tool parts, such as undercuts, add cost to every tool and complexity to every cycle. That is why the checks below are worth doing on the CAD model, not on the first samples. The checklist

Feature Typical starting point Why it matters
Wall thickness As uniform as possible; commonly around 1 to 4 mm for many small and medium parts, depending on material and size; blend changes gradually rather than in steps Thick and uneven sections cool unevenly, causing sink marks, voids, warping and long cycle times
Draft On every face parallel to the direction the tool opens: commonly at least 0.5 to 1 degree, more for deep features and textured surfaces Without draft, parts drag and stick on ejection, scuffing cosmetic faces
Ribs Thickness about 40 to 60 per cent of the adjoining wall; height up to about 3 times the wall; spaced at least about 2 wall thicknesses apart; drafted Ribs add stiffness without thick sections; too thick and they sink the opposite face
Bosses Outside diameter around twice the hole; boss wall thinner than the main wall; supported by gusset ribs or joined to nearby walls; cored from below Solid bosses cause sink and voids; unsupported bosses crack when screws are driven
Corners Inside radius at least about half the wall; outside radius about inside radius plus the wall Sharp corners concentrate stress, restrict flow and are harder to fill and eject
Undercuts Avoid where possible; otherwise use side actions, lifters, flexible bump-offs, or a pass-through core redesign Anything that stops the part pulling straight out needs moving tool parts, which add cost and maintenance
Holes and slots Prefer through holes; keep blind holes shallow relative to their diameter; keep holes away from edges Long slender core pins deflect and break; holes near edges weaken the part
Text, logos and texture Raised lettering on the part (engraved into the tool) is easier to make and change; add extra draft for texture (see surface finishes) Engraving can be added to a tool later; texture without enough draft scrapes on ejection
Gates, ejector pins, parting line Agree their positions with the moulder, away from cosmetic faces All leave visible marks; moving them after the tool is cut is costly
Tolerances and flatness Tight tolerances only where the function needs them; add ribs or curvature to large flat areas Plastics shrink and can warp; unnecessary tight tolerances raise tool and inspection cost (check critical fits with the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator)

Walls and draft

Even wall thickness is the most important single rule. The plastic in a thick section stays molten longest, and as it cools it shrinks and pulls the surface inwards (a sink mark) or leaves an internal void. The fix is almost always to core the thick feature out so that it is made of walls close to the nominal thickness.

Draft is the slight taper on faces that run in the direction the tool opens. It lets the part release cleanly. The amount needed rises with depth and surface texture: a textured cosmetic surface can need several degrees, and the texture supplier will usually specify a minimum. Add draft in CAD from the start; retrofitting it changes every dimension that depends on those faces.

Ribs and bosses

Ribs and bosses are how moulded parts get stiffness and fixing points without thick walls. Their proportions are a balance: thick enough to fill and to do their job, thin enough not to cause sink marks on the visible face opposite them.

For screw bosses, follow the screw manufacturer's recommendations for hole size in your chosen plastic: thread-forming screws for plastics are designed for specific hole diameters, and the right hole matters more for joint strength than the boss's outside size. Heat-set or moulded-in inserts are an alternative where the joint is opened often; joining methods compared sets screws and inserts against snap fits, welding and adhesives.

Practical rule: if a feature needs a side action, ask whether it can be redesigned. A snap-fit hook can often be moulded straight-pull by putting a hole in the wall behind it, so a core passes through and forms the hook's underside.

Worked example: reviewing an enclosure lid

Illustrative example. A start-up sends a moulder its CAD for a two-part plastic enclosure. The lid has 4 mm walls, four solid screw bosses 9 mm across, no draft on its 25 mm side walls, sharp internal corners, and two side-facing clips that would each need a side action.

Issue found Change made Effect
Walls thicker than the part needs Reduced to a uniform 2.5 mm, with three internal ribs for stiffness Less material, shorter cooling time, no sink marks
Solid screw bosses Cored bosses joined to the side walls with gussets No sink on the outer face; stronger under screw torque
No draft on side walls 1 degree on smooth faces, more on the textured top Clean ejection, no drag marks
Sharp internal corners Radii added, sized to the wall thickness Better flow, lower stress at the corners
Two clips needing side actions Redesigned as straight-pull snaps with pass-through cores Simpler, cheaper tool with fewer moving parts

None of these changes alters what the lid does, but together they make the tool simpler, the cycle shorter and the parts more consistent. That is typical of a good DFM review.

Common mistakes

Designing as if the part will be machined. Solid blocks and sharp internal corners that are easy to machine make poor moulded parts.
Adding draft at the end. It ripples through every mating dimension; design it in from the start.
Using one wall thickness rule for every plastic. Materials flow and shrink differently; check the datasheet and ask the moulder.
Ignoring where the tool will leave marks. Gate, ejector and parting-line marks end up on the cosmetic face unless they are agreed.
Skipping the moulder's DFM report. Most toolmakers provide one, often with a mould-flow simulation; read it before approving the tool.

FAQ

What wall thickness should an injection-moulded part have?

As uniform as possible, at the thinnest value that meets the strength and filling requirements. Many small and medium parts fall somewhere around 1 to 4 mm, but the right figure depends on the material, the part size and the flow length, so check the material datasheet and ask your moulder.

How much draft angle does an injection-moulded part need?

A common starting point is at least 0.5 to 1 degree on faces parallel to the direction the tool opens, with more for deep features and textured surfaces. Textured surfaces can need several degrees; the texture supplier will usually state a minimum.

How thick should ribs be on a plastic part?

Typically about 40 to 60 per cent of the wall they join, up to about three times the wall thickness tall, with draft and a small radius at the base. Thicker ribs tend to cause sink marks on the opposite face.

What is an undercut in injection moulding?

Any feature that stops the part being pulled straight out of the tool, such as a side hole, a clip or an internal groove. Undercuts need side actions, lifters or flexible ejection, which add cost, so they are worth designing out where possible.

What causes sink marks on moulded parts?

Thick sections, such as solid bosses or heavy ribs, cool more slowly than the surrounding wall and shrink as they do, pulling the surface inwards. Keeping walls even and coring out thick features is the usual cure.
