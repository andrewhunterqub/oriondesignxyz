Choosing lower-carbon materials: less material, better material, longer life

A practical approach to lower-carbon material choices: compare parts that do the same job, reduce mass first, use recycled content and better sources, and check durability and end of life so savings are real.

Quick answer

The lowest-carbon material is usually the one that does the job with the least mass, lasts the longest and can be recovered, not simply the one with the lowest figure per kilogram.

Compare materials per part, not per kilogram: a stiffer or stronger material may need much less of it. Use less material through better geometry, prefer recycled content where performance allows, consider where and how the material is made, and check durability, because a part that fails early wastes everything that went into it. Use consistent data sources and treat early figures as estimates.

Start with the part, not the material

A material's footprint per kilogram is only the starting point. What matters is the footprint of the part that meets the requirement. A higher-footprint material can win if it allows a much lighter or longer-lasting part. Define the requirement first: load, stiffness, temperature, environment, lifetime. See choosing materials for plastic products.

Five levers

Lever In practice
Use less material Ribs instead of thick walls, hollow sections, optimised geometry, fewer parts
Recycled content Recycled metals and plastics often have lower footprints than virgin material; check grades meet the requirement and supply is consistent
Production source The energy used to make a material, and where it is made, can change its footprint substantially; ask suppliers for data
Durability A part that lasts twice as long can justify a higher initial footprint; see design for repair
End of life Materials that are widely recycled, easy to separate and clearly marked keep their value
Practical rule: reduce mass first, then improve the material. Removing material removes its footprint entirely, whatever it was made from.

Watch for trade-offs

  • Bio-based is not automatically lower-carbon. Farming, processing and end of life all count, and some bio-based plastics are hard to recycle.
  • Lighter can mean shorter-lived. Thin walls and weaker materials can fail sooner.
  • Mixed materials may perform well but be impossible to separate for recycling.
  • Transport matters most for heavy, bulky products moved long distances, but is often smaller than material and manufacturing impacts.
  • Other impacts such as water use or toxicity may rise when carbon falls; see product carbon footprint vs LCA.

Getting reliable data

Footprint figures for materials vary between databases, regions and production routes. Use one consistent source for comparisons, ask suppliers for environmental product declarations or footprint data where available, and present early results as ranges. The Carbon Footprint Estimator helps compare options at design stage.

Common mistakes

Comparing per kilogram. Compare parts that meet the same requirement.
Mixing data sources. Differences between databases can be larger than the difference you are measuring.
Assuming "natural" means low impact. Check the evidence for each material.
Ignoring lifetime. The greenest part is the one that does not need replacing.

FAQ

How do I choose a lower-carbon material?

Compare the footprint of parts that meet the same requirement rather than footprints per kilogram, reduce mass first, prefer recycled content where performance allows, ask suppliers for data, and check durability and end of life.

Are recycled materials always lower-carbon?

Recycled metals and plastics often have lower footprints than virgin material, but it depends on the material, the recycling process and the data source. Check the grade meets your requirements.

Are bio-based plastics better for the climate?

Not automatically. Farming, processing and end of life all count, and some bio-based plastics are hard to recycle in existing systems. Compare them on evidence like any other material.

Does reducing weight reduce carbon footprint?

Usually, because less material is made and transported, as long as the lighter part still lasts as long. A part that fails early can undo the saving.

Where can I find carbon data for materials?

Use a consistent database for comparisons and ask suppliers for environmental product declarations or footprint data. Figures vary by source and region, so present early estimates as ranges.
