Choosing metals for a product: common alloys, trade-offs and how to decide

How to choose a metal for a physical product: what drives the decision, when to use aluminium, mild steel, stainless steel, brass, zinc or titanium, how the manufacturing process narrows the field, and how to avoid corrosion and finishing problems.

Quick answer

Choose the metal and the process together, starting from what the part must do: load, weight, environment, finish, volume and cost.

For most product parts the shortlist is short. Aluminium alloys (6082 or 6061 for machined parts, 5052 for bent sheet) when weight and appearance matter; mild steel with a protective coating when cost and stiffness matter most; 304 stainless for wet or food-contact use indoors, and 316 near salt water or chlorides; brass for small machined fittings; zinc die-casting alloys for detailed parts at volume. Check the datasheet for the specific alloy and temper, and keep dissimilar metals apart where they can get wet.

What drives the choice

Write down what the part must cope with before looking at alloys. Six questions settle most decisions:

  • Strength and stiffness for the weight. Steel is stiffer than aluminium and usually stronger, but aluminium is roughly a third of the density of steel and about a third as stiff. For a part that must be light, aluminium usually wins; for a part that must be stiff in a small space, steel often does.
  • Corrosion. Where will the part live: indoors, outdoors, in a kitchen, near the sea, in contact with sweat or cleaning chemicals? This decides whether you need stainless steel, a coating, or neither.
  • Process. Each process suits particular alloys. A metal that machines beautifully may crack when bent, and few alloys suit die casting.
  • Finish. Some finishes only work on some metals. Anodising, for example, is mainly an aluminium process.
  • Cost. Raw material price matters, but for small parts the cost of machining, forming and finishing usually matters more.
  • Recyclability. Steel and aluminium are among the most widely recycled materials, and recycled aluminium needs only a small fraction of the energy of new metal. Using one metal throughout, and avoiding permanent joints between different metals, keeps that value at end of life.

Aluminium: which alloy?

For machined parts, 6082 (common in the UK and Europe) and 6061 (common in North America) are the usual general-purpose choices. They are heat-treated to a hard temper, machine cleanly and anodise well. For bent sheet parts, 5052 is the usual choice: it bends tightly without cracking and resists corrosion well, including in damp and marine air. 6061 or 6082 in a hard temper tends to crack on tight bends, so do not specify it for sheet work without checking with the fabricator. Extruded profiles usually use alloys from the same 6000 series chosen for extrusion, such as 6063. Very high-strength alloys such as 7075 exist, but cost more and are rarely needed for consumer products.

Stainless steel: 304 or 316?

304 is the general-purpose stainless steel used for kitchen equipment, sinks and indoor fittings. 316 adds molybdenum, which makes it much more resistant to pitting from chlorides: salt water, sea air, swimming pools, de-icing salt and some cleaning chemicals. For anything near the coast or regularly wetted by salt, choose 316. Fasteners follow the same pattern, sold as A2 (304 type) and A4 (316 type). Both are largely non-magnetic, which can matter around magnets and sensors, though cold working makes them slightly magnetic. Ferritic grades such as 430 are cheaper and magnetic but less corrosion resistant.

The others in brief

Mild steel is the default for low-cost structural parts, provided it is protected by paint, powder coating, zinc plating or galvanising. Brass is the easiest common metal to machine and suits small turned parts, but check restrictions on lead content if the part touches drinking water or sits in certain consumer products. Zinc die-cast alloys suit small, detailed parts made in volume, often plated or painted. Titanium is worth its cost only when weight, corrosion or biocompatibility truly demand it.

How the process shapes the choice

The metal and the process should be chosen together. See manufacturing processes compared for the wider picture.

  • CNC machining: works with almost any metal, but cost tracks machining time. Brass and aluminium cut quickly; mild steel is slower; stainless steel and titanium are much more expensive to machine. The CNC machining design rules cover the geometry side.
  • Sheet metal: laser cutting, punching and bending suit mild steel, stainless steel and formable aluminium such as 5052, at low tooling cost. Bend radii depend on the material and thickness, as explained in sheet metal design basics.
  • Die casting: high-volume parts with complex shapes, mostly in zinc or aluminium casting alloys. Tooling is expensive, so it pays only at volume. Cast aluminium alloys contain silicon to help them flow, which also makes them anodise poorly. Die casting explained covers the process, design rules and finishing in more detail.
  • Extrusion: long, constant-section aluminium profiles such as frames, rails and heat sinks, cut to length and machined. Die costs are modest, which makes extrusion attractive even at moderate volumes. Aluminium extrusions for product enclosures covers profile design, end caps and finishing.
Practical rule: specify the alloy and the temper, not just "aluminium" or "stainless". 6082 in its hard temper and 5052 in a soft temper behave very differently on a press brake, and 304 and 316 behave very differently by the sea.

Corrosion and galvanic pairs

Mild steel rusts unless protected. Aluminium and stainless steel protect themselves with a thin oxide layer, but that layer can break down: stainless steel can pit in chloride environments, and aluminium can corrode in strongly alkaline or salty conditions.

Galvanic corrosion happens when two different metals touch and are bridged by water, particularly salt water. The less noble metal, such as aluminium or zinc, corrodes faster; the more noble one, such as stainless steel, is protected. In general terms:

  • In dry indoor products it is rarely a problem.
  • Outdoors or in wet use, prefer the same metal throughout, or metals close together in the galvanic series.
  • Where different metals must meet, insulate them with plastic washers, sleeves, sealant or a coating, and keep water out of the joint.
  • Area ratio matters. Small stainless screws in a large aluminium panel are usually tolerable; aluminium rivets in a large stainless sheet will corrode quickly.

Finishes

Finishes protect the metal, set its appearance and affect cost. Common pairings: anodising for aluminium; powder coating for mild steel and aluminium; zinc plating or galvanising for steel; brushed, bead-blasted, passivated or electropolished surfaces for stainless; plating or painting for zinc die-castings. Plan the finish when choosing the metal, not after, and allow for its thickness on tight fits and threads. The surface finishes guide covers each process in more detail.

Metal or plastic?

Metal suits parts that must be stiff, strong, hot, conductive, hard-wearing or premium to the touch, and processes such as machining and sheet metal need little tooling at low volume. Plastic suits complex shapes with clips, bosses and integrated features, light weight, electrical insulation and radio transparency, and becomes very cheap per part once a mould is paid for. Many products use both: a plastic housing with a metal chassis or bracket inside. For the plastic side of the decision, see choosing plastic materials, and shortlist polymers with the Plastic Material Selector.

Worked example: a bracket for an outdoor sensor

Illustrative example. A small company needs a wall bracket to hold a sensor unit of about 2 kg on buildings, including coastal sites. Requirements: no visible rust for at least ten years, little or no maintenance, fixing with stainless steel screws, a clean appearance, and a few hundred brackets a year, so low tooling cost matters.

Candidate Assessment
Mild steel sheet, powder coated Cheapest and stiff, but any chip or cut edge will rust at a coastal site. Rejected for the long life required
Machined 6082 aluminium, anodised Light and attractive, but machining from solid is slow for a simple bracket, and stainless screws in sea air create a galvanic pair. Rejected on cost
5052 aluminium sheet, powder coated Light, bends well, resists marine air. Workable, but needs insulating washers at the screws and relies on the coating staying intact. Kept as the lower-cost alternative
304 stainless sheet No coating needed, but prone to staining and pitting at coastal sites. Rejected for the coastal sites
316 stainless sheet Resists chlorides, matches A4 stainless screws so there is no galvanic pair, and needs no coating to chip. Higher material cost, but the bracket is small and simple. Chosen

The team specifies a laser-cut and bent 316 bracket with a brushed finish and passivation, checks the bend radii with the fabricator, and asks for a material certificate with each batch. Before committing, they fit samples to a test site and inspect them over a winter. Prices and lead times come from real quotes, not from this example.

Common mistakes

Writing "aluminium" or "stainless" on the drawing. Without the alloy and temper, the supplier will choose, and their choice may not suit your bends, finish or environment.
Using 304 by the sea. 304 stainless can stain and pit in salt air. Use 316 where chlorides are present.
Mixing metals in wet places. Aluminium parts with stainless or brass fixings, left wet, can corrode quickly at the joint. Insulate them or use one metal.
Choosing on price per kilogram. Machining time, scrap and finishing often cost more than the metal. A cheaper alloy that is slow to machine can make a dearer part.
Choosing metal by habit. A metal part copied from a prototype may be cheaper and lighter as a moulded plastic part at volume, or vice versa. Revisit the choice when volumes change.

FAQ

Should I use aluminium or steel for my product?

Use aluminium when weight, natural corrosion resistance or an anodised finish matters, and steel when stiffness in a small section, weldability or low material cost matters more. Aluminium is roughly a third of the density of steel but also about a third as stiff, so an aluminium part often needs thicker sections to match.

What is the difference between 304 and 316 stainless steel?

316 contains molybdenum, which gives it much better resistance to pitting from chlorides such as salt water, sea air and some cleaning chemicals. 304 is cheaper and fine for most indoor and kitchen use; 316 is the usual choice for marine, coastal and pool environments.

Which aluminium alloy should I use for sheet metal parts?

5052 is the usual choice because it bends tightly without cracking and resists corrosion well. Heat-treated alloys such as 6061 and 6082 machine well but tend to crack on tight bends, so check with the fabricator before specifying them for sheet work.

Can I use stainless steel screws in aluminium?

Often, yes, indoors and in dry conditions. Outdoors or in wet use the pair can cause galvanic corrosion of the aluminium, so isolate the screws with plastic washers or sealant, keep water out of the joint, and check the coating covers the hole.

Is titanium worth it for a consumer product?

Usually only when weight, corrosion resistance or skin contact genuinely demand it, or the premium is part of the product's appeal. It is expensive and slow to machine, so a well-designed aluminium or stainless part is normally the better value.
