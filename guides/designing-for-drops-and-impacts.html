Designing a product to survive drops and impacts

Why products break when they are dropped, the design changes that make the biggest difference, how to set a realistic drop requirement from the way the product is used, and how to test prototypes so the results mean something.

Quick answer

A product survives a drop when the energy of the impact is spread out, absorbed or steered away from the weak points, and when nothing heavy inside is free to move.

Round the corners, tie bosses and ribs into the walls, choose a tough material that stays tough in the cold, hold batteries and boards firmly, and give the corners something to absorb the hit. Set the drop heights, surfaces and orientations from how the product is really used, then drop prototypes on faces, edges and corners, open them up after each drop and record what you find.

Why products fail when dropped

In a drop, the product's energy has to go somewhere in a fraction of a second, and it goes into whatever bends, breaks or moves first. Most failures fall into a handful of patterns:

  • Cracks at stress raisers. Sharp internal corners, the roots of bosses and ribs, holes near edges and sudden changes in wall thickness concentrate stress, and cracks start there.
  • Snap fits and clips letting go. The housing flexes, the halves spread for an instant and the hooks slip off their catches, so the product pops open without breaking.
  • Screw bosses splitting. A boss already stressed by its screw splits under a shock load, especially when it stands free of the wall or sits in a corner.
  • Heavy internal parts moving. Batteries, magnets, motors and populated boards keep travelling when the housing stops, hammering whatever holds them.
  • Solder joints and connectors. Board flex can crack joints under large or heavy components, and connectors can unseat or pull pads off the board.
  • Displays and glass. Glass often cracks from an edge, especially if the housing twists or a hard point presses on it.
  • Seals shifting. A housing that flexes and springs back can leave a gasket out of its groove, so a product that was waterproof is no longer.

Several of these are not cracks at all. A product that looks fine outside can hide a loose battery, a cracked joint or a displaced seal, which is why inspection after each test drop matters.

Design responses

Toughness is designed in through geometry, material and how the inside is held together. Each change below is cheap in CAD and expensive once a mould has been cut.

Generous radii

Round the outside corners and edges of the housing, and round the inside corners too. A sharp internal corner is a ready-made crack starter, and larger external radii can help the product skid on impact rather than stop dead on a point.

Ribs and bosses tied into the walls

A free-standing boss behaves like a post that can snap off at its base. Connect bosses to the nearest wall with a short rib or gussets, keep a radius at every root, and avoid putting a boss right in a corner where the impact load arrives first. Size ribs and bosses to the wall as described in the injection moulding DFM checklist, so the part still moulds without sink marks or voids, which are weak points of their own.

Tough materials, and the effect of temperature

Tough plastics bend and absorb energy before breaking; brittle ones are stiff but crack with little warning. Fillers such as glass fibre usually add stiffness at some cost to toughness, and many plastics become more brittle in the cold, so a housing that survives indoors may crack on a winter morning. Tougher grades can be less stiff, less heat resistant or dearer, so shortlist against all the requirements using the guide to choosing plastic materials, and check the supplier's data for the exact grade at the lowest temperature the product will see.

Wall thickness

Walls that are too thin flex too far and crack; walls that are too thick mould poorly and can hide voids. Aim for even, adequate walls with ribs for stiffness rather than thickening everything, and avoid sudden steps in thickness.

Securing heavy parts

Anything with mass needs a positive location in every direction, not just friction or a strip of foam tape. Cradle batteries with ribs or a moulded pocket, clamp them with a cover or bracket, and leave little room for them to gain speed. Magnets, weights and motors need the same. Battery safety adds its own requirements, covered in electronics in a physical product.

Board mounting and heavy components

Support the circuit board near its heavy components and connectors, not just at its four corners, so it cannot flex like a trampoline. Large components may need adhesive staking or a support rib. Keep tall parts clear of the walls and retain cables and connectors so they cannot shake loose.

Bumpers, overmoulds and corner protection

A soft material at the corners spreads the impact over a longer time, lowering the peak force: an overmoulded rubber-like layer, clip-on bumpers or a sleeve. Raised corners can keep a display off the floor in a face-down drop. Overmoulding adds tooling cost, so weigh it against geometry changes.

Letting energy go somewhere deliberate

Sometimes the best answer is to choose where the energy goes. A flexible rib, a sprung mount or a crumple feature on a cheap replaceable part can take the hit so the housing and electronics do not. The snap fits guide covers keeping hooks within the strain the material can take.

Practical rule: if a drop breaks the product, find out where the energy went first. The fix is usually to spread the load, slow it down or redirect it, rather than to make everything thicker.

Defining the drop requirement

"It must survive being dropped" cannot be designed or tested to. Write down:

  • Height: from real use: a handheld device from pocket or ear height, a desk product from a table, a tool from a bench or ladder.
  • Surface: carpet, wood, tile and concrete differ greatly. Use the hardest surface the product is likely to meet.
  • Orientation: faces, edges and corners, prioritising the most likely ones.
  • Number of drops and temperature: one drop or many, at room temperature or in the cold.
  • Pass criteria: still works, no cracks, cosmetic marks allowed or not, still sealed, and safe if it does break.

Some product standards include a drop or impact test. Check whether the standard that applies to your product specifies one, because that becomes a minimum; the guide to product certification basics explains how to find it.

Keep this separate from packaging transit tests, which check the boxed product during handling and delivery and tell you about the packaging, not about the product being dropped by its user. Plan those with the guide to packaging design for shipping.

Testing drops on prototypes

Drop early. A crude test on the first integrated prototype shows where the battery moves or which boss is wrong while those are easy to change. Treat the results with care: 3D-printed parts are usually weaker between layers, behave differently from the moulded material and lack its knit lines. A printed part that cracks might be fine moulded, and one that survives is not proof. Use prints to find obvious problems, then repeat the decisive tests on samples from the production tool.

A useful drop test looks like this:

  1. Every orientation: faces, edges and corners, starting with the likeliest and the ones you suspect are weakest.
  2. Cold testing: condition some units to the lowest expected temperature and drop them before they warm up.
  3. Repeated drops: damage accumulates, so a unit that survives one drop may crack on a later one.
  4. Inspect inside after each drop: check function, then open the unit for cracks, loose parts, moved cables and displaced seals.
  5. Record everything: unit, build state, material, temperature, height, surface, orientation, result and photographs. Slow-motion phone video shows what flexes and moves.

Test more than one unit and retest after any design, material or supplier change. Fit drop testing into the wider product test plan with clear pass criteria, and use the Product Test Plan Builder to set it out. For sealed products, repeat the water test after the drop sequence, because a drop can undo a good seal; see sealing and waterproofing an enclosure.

Where simulation helps

Drop simulation can show where stresses peak and how parts move, which helps compare options, spot a weak boss before parts exist and understand a failure seen in testing. It depends on material data that is often limited for plastics under impact and in the cold. Treat it as a complement that narrows the options, not a replacement for dropping real parts.

Worked example: a handheld device that cracks at a corner

Illustrative example. A start-up is developing a handheld battery-powered gauge with a two-part moulded housing held together by four screws into bosses at the corners. A rechargeable battery sits loose in a pocket between two ribs. The requirement is to keep working with no cracks after a sequence of drops from typical hand height onto a hard floor, on every face, edge and corner, at room temperature and in the cold.

In early drops on printed prototypes, the housing cracks on a corner drop, starting at the root of the free-standing corner screw boss. Opening the unit also shows the battery has moved and marked the ribs. Because prints can exaggerate cracking, the team treats this as a warning about geometry rather than a verdict on the material.

Finding Change made
Crack starts at a sharp root on a free-standing corner boss Move the boss slightly inboard from the corner, tie it to both walls with gussets and add a radius at the root
Small external corner radius concentrates the impact Increase the outside corner radius and add matching internal radii
Battery moves and hammers the ribs Add a rib cage that locates the battery on all sides and a foam pad under the cover to take up clearance
The chosen grade loses toughness in the cold Review tougher grades with the material supplier, keeping heat and chemical requirements in view
Unprotected corners take each corner drop directly Add an overmoulded soft corner band, priced against the extra tooling cost before committing

The retest plan uses several units of the revised design, each taken through the full sequence of faces, edges and corners, with half conditioned in the cold first. Every unit is checked and opened after each drop, with results logged and photographed. Once the revised prints pass, the same sequence is scheduled on first samples from the production tool before they are approved.

Common mistakes

No written drop requirement. Without a height, surface, orientation and pass criteria, nobody can say whether a result is good enough.
Only testing flat face drops. Corners and edges usually find the weak points. Test them all.
Testing only at room temperature. Many plastics are more brittle in the cold, which is often when products get dropped outdoors.
Judging only from the outside. A unit that looks fine may have a loose battery, a cracked joint or a displaced seal. Open it up.
Trusting printed prototypes too far. Use them to find problems early, then confirm on moulded samples.
Confusing product and packaging drops. A boxed product surviving a transit test tells you nothing about the product surviving its user.

FAQ

What height should I drop-test my product from?

Start from how the product is used: the height it is usually held, carried or placed at, and the hardest surface it is likely to land on. Then check whether a standard that applies to your product specifies a drop test, because that sets a minimum.

Is a product drop test the same as a packaging drop test?

No. A product drop test checks the unpackaged product being dropped by its user. A packaging transit test checks the boxed product surviving handling and delivery. Most products need both, planned separately.

Can I drop-test 3D-printed prototypes?

Yes, and it is worth doing early to find loose parts and weak features. Printed parts behave differently from moulded ones, usually weaker between layers, so confirm the results on samples from the production tool.

Why test drops in the cold?

Many plastics become more brittle at low temperatures, so a housing that survives indoors can crack outdoors in winter. Condition units to the lowest temperature the product will see and drop them before they warm up.

Can simulation replace physical drop testing?

No. Simulation is useful for comparing options and finding weak points before parts exist, but it relies on material data that is often limited for plastics. Use it alongside physical tests, not instead of them.
