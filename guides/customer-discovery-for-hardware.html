Customer discovery for hardware: learning what matters before you design

How to learn from the people who have the problem before you design a physical product: who to talk to, questions that give evidence rather than politeness, observing real use, and turning conversations into decisions.

Quick answer

Customer discovery means talking to the people who have the problem, about their past behaviour, before you design the product.

Find people who experience the problem, ask how they deal with it today and what it costs them, and listen for patterns. Avoid pitching your idea or asking whether they would buy it; people are polite and bad at predicting their own behaviour. For hardware, add observation in the real environment, because use conditions shape the design. Then test the strongest signals with something that asks for commitment, such as a pre-order or a pilot.

Why it matters more for hardware

Changing a physical product after tooling is slow and expensive, so learning what matters before design starts saves far more than it does for software. Discovery also reveals the use environment, who buys versus who uses, and the price people already pay for alternatives. It is the first step of validating a product idea.

Who to talk to

  • Users who experience the problem directly.
  • Buyers, if different: parents, purchasing teams, gift buyers.
  • Influencers and gatekeepers: retailers, installers, clinicians, facilities managers.
  • People who have given up on existing solutions, and those who rely on workarounds.

Find them through communities, forums, trade associations, events, your own network and introductions from earlier interviews. Aim for variety, not just friendly contacts.

How to run a good interview

Ask about Example question
The last time it happened "Tell me about the last time you had to deal with this."
What they do now "How do you handle it at the moment? What have you tried?"
What it costs them "What does that cost you in time, money or hassle?"
What they have spent "Have you paid for anything to solve it? What happened?"
The context "Where are you, and what else is going on, when it happens?"
Practical rule: ask about the past, not the future. "What did you do last time?" gives evidence; "Would you buy this?" gives politeness.

Questions to avoid

  • "Would you buy a product that…?" — hypothetical and leading.
  • "How much would you pay?" — stated prices are unreliable; see how to price a physical product.
  • "Do you like my idea?" — invites compliments, not information.

Observe as well as ask

For physical products, watch people in the real setting where possible: the kitchen, workshop, ward or vehicle. Note the space, lighting, gloves, mess, noise and interruptions. These become requirements in your product requirements document. Once you have a model to put in people's hands, the same habits of watching rather than asking carry into usability testing a prototype.

Make sense of what you hear

  1. Write up each conversation the same day, separating what was said from your interpretation.
  2. Look for patterns across interviews: repeated problems, workarounds and spending.
  3. Rank problems by how often they occur, how painful they are, and whether people already pay to solve them.
  4. Keep going until new conversations stop revealing new themes, then test the strongest with a commitment: a pre-order, deposit, pilot or letter of intent.

Common mistakes

Pitching instead of listening. Once you describe your solution, people respond to it instead of telling you about their problem.
Only asking friends and family. They want to be supportive, not accurate.
Counting compliments as evidence. Enthusiasm without commitment is a weak signal.
Stopping after a handful of chats. Keep going until the patterns are clear and stable.

FAQ

What is customer discovery?

Talking to people who have the problem you want to solve, about how they experience and deal with it today, before designing the product. It finds out whether the problem matters, to whom, and what they already do and pay.

How many customer interviews should I do?

There is no fixed number. Keep going, with a varied group of users and buyers, until new conversations stop revealing new themes, then test the strongest findings with something that asks for commitment.

What questions should I ask in a customer interview?

Ask about specific past experiences: the last time the problem happened, what they did, what it cost them, what they have tried or paid for, and the context. Avoid hypothetical questions about buying your idea.

Should I show people my product idea?

Not at first. Once you describe a solution, people react to it rather than telling you about their problem. Show concepts later, and look for commitment rather than compliments.

How is customer discovery different for hardware?

The use environment, physical constraints and the split between buyer and user matter more, and design changes after tooling are expensive. Observing people in the real setting is especially valuable.
