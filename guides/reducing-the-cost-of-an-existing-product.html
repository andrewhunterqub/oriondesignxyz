Reducing the cost of an existing product

How to find where the cost of a product in production really sits, which kinds of change usually lower it, how to make those changes without breaking function, safety or certification, and how to decide which ones are worth doing first.

Quick answer

Start from a full cost breakdown, not a hunch, then rank possible changes by saving per unit times volume, against the effort, one-off cost and risk of making each one.

Cost in a product that is already being made often hides in bought-in parts, assembly time, scrap, packaging and freight rather than in the main moulded or machined parts. The best cost-down changes remove parts, operations or over-specification that customers do not value, and every change is validated and controlled before it reaches production, because a cheaper product that fails, or no longer meets its certification, is not cheaper.

What value engineering means for a product in production

Value engineering asks what each part, feature and process step does for the customer, and whether the same function can be delivered for less. Once a product is in production that is harder to act on: tools are cut, suppliers are set up, test reports and certificates refer to the current design, and customers know how it looks and feels.

So cost reduction on an existing product is a balance. Every change saves something per unit but costs something once: engineering time, tool changes, new samples, retesting and the risk of a fault reaching customers. A change is worth making when the first comfortably outweighs the second at the volumes you will actually sell.

Start from a cost breakdown

Before choosing changes, rebuild what one unit really costs, line by line, at your current order volume. The guide to estimating manufacturing cost sets out the method: materials, process, bought-in parts, assembly labour and packaging, then a scrap allowance and an overhead loading, then tooling and landed costs on top.

List every part in the BOM Cost Builder with its quantity and current price, then load the totals into the Product Cost Estimator, which shows the largest cost driver and how sensitive the result is to each input. Use real invoice prices and measured build times: that is the advantage of a product already in production. The breakdown is also the baseline every saving is measured against.

Where cost hides

The main enclosure or chassis gets the attention, but it is often not where most of the money goes. Look hardest at:

  • Bought-in parts. Electronics, cables, fasteners, inserts, labels and accessories add up, and many were chosen quickly during development.
  • Process and cycle time. A moulding with thick walls cools slowly; a machined part with tight features takes longer and needs more setups. Cycle time is usually priced into each part.
  • Assembly labour. Each screw, cable, label and reorientation takes seconds, and seconds multiply across every unit. The assembly time guide shows how to estimate them, and joining methods compared shows the faster alternatives.
  • Scrap and quality. Parts rejected for cosmetic marks, units failing end-of-line test and rework all add cost that may sit inside an allowance nobody questions. Quality control and first-article inspection covers how to find the causes.
  • Packaging and freight. An oversized box costs more to make, fits fewer units on a pallet and can cost more to ship. See packaging design for shipping and landed cost: freight, duty and VAT.
  • Tooling amortisation. If tooling cost is being recovered per unit, low sales mean each unit carries more of it. That is a volume problem, not a design one, so do not try to fix it with redesigns that need new tools.

Cost-down ideas by category

How much each is worth depends entirely on your product, volumes and suppliers, so treat these as places to look, not promises.

  • Part consolidation. Two parts that never move relative to each other can sometimes become one moulding, removing a part, a join and an assembly step. The new part may need a new or modified tool.
  • Material changes. A lower-cost grade, an unfilled resin in place of a glass-filled one, or a standard sheet thickness can save money, but material changes affect strength, appearance, flammability and sometimes certification. Choosing plastic materials explains the trade-offs.
  • Tolerance relaxation. Tight tolerances on features that do not need them add machining time, inspection and scrap. Review the drawing against what each feature actually does, as described in tolerances and engineering drawings.
  • Finish simplification. A moulded-in texture in place of paint, one print colour instead of two, or a finish only on visible faces can remove whole operations. See surface finishes for plastic and metal parts.
  • Fastener reduction. Fewer screws, one screw type throughout, or snap fits where the joint is not opened for service all cut parts and assembly time.
  • Packaging size. A smaller box, fewer inserts and instructions printed on the box itself can reduce material and freight cost, provided the product still survives transit.
  • Supplier and route changes. Re-quoting a part, combining parts with one supplier or moving where the product is made can change cost substantially, but a cheaper unit price can bring higher freight, minimum order quantities or quality risk. Compare like for like using getting and comparing manufacturing quotes and UK vs overseas manufacturing.

Protecting function, safety and brand

A product in production has already been proven. Each change reopens part of that proof, so treat it as a small design project:

  • Validate before you switch. Decide which tests the change could affect, such as drop, strength, temperature, ingress, transit or user feel, and repeat them on parts made by the production process. The testing and validation plans guide and the Test Plan Builder help you choose.
  • Use change control. Record each change with a reason, a new part or drawing revision, the date or batch it takes effect, and who approved it, so that a field problem can be traced to the build it came from.
  • Check certification. Changes to materials, the enclosure, electronics, power supply, labelling or construction may affect the evidence behind a declaration of conformity or a test report. Ask whoever holds your technical file, or your test house, whether the change needs assessing before you make it. Product certification basics outlines how conformity is shown.
  • Protect what customers value. The feel of a button, the weight in the hand and the unboxing can be part of why people buy. Ask customers, or check reviews and returns, before removing something that looks like a cost but works as a reason to buy.

Talking to suppliers

Your suppliers make these parts every day and often know where the cost is. Ask them openly: which features slow the cycle, cause rejects or need extra operations, and what they would change if they could. A supplier who can plan longer runs from an honest forecast may lower the price without any design change.

Ask for any new price to be quoted against the same specification, volume and terms as the current one, and to show the one-off cost of tool changes and new samples separately. Agree who pays for tool modifications and who owns the modified tool. A brief like the one in how to find and brief a manufacturer keeps the request clear.

Prioritising: saving × volume against effort and risk

Annual saving = loaded saving per unit × units per year

Compare that annual saving with the one-off cost of the change (engineering, tool modification, samples, retesting, any certification work) to find how many units it takes to pay back. Then weigh the risk: a packaging change that a drop test can prove is lower risk than a material change to a load-bearing part.

If scrap and overhead are charged as percentages, a direct saving is worth slightly more once they are applied. Start with changes that are high in saving × volume, low in risk and quick to prove.

Worked example: three changes to a small device

Illustrative figures only. This continues the worked example in the manufacturing cost guide: £4.80 of material, £2.40 of process, £5.50 of bought-in parts (£12.70 together), £4.80 of labour (12 minutes at £24 an hour) and £1.20 of packaging (£6.00 together). The direct cost is £18.70; a 6% scrap allowance (£1.12) gives £19.82, and a 14% overhead loading (£2.77) gives a COGS of £22.59.

The team tests three assumed changes, each priced with the same method and rounding:

Change (assumed) Direct cost New COGS Saving
Snap fits replace four screws Labour −£1.20 (12 to 9 min); screws −£0.30 £20.78 £1.81
Smaller box, one fewer insert Packaging −£0.25 £22.30 £0.29
No paint on a hidden face Process −£0.30 £22.23 £0.36
All three together £18.70 to £16.65 £20.12 £2.47

With all three, the direct cost falls to £16.65; scrap at 6% is £1.00, giving £17.65, and overhead at 14% is £2.47, giving a COGS of £20.12. The individual savings add up to £2.46 rather than £2.47 because each figure is rounded to the penny.

At an assumed 10,000 units a year, the three changes save £24,700 a year. Suppose the tool modification for the snap fits costs £2,500, and samples, a transit drop test and retesting cost another £1,500. The £4,000 of one-off cost is repaid after about 1,620 units (£4,000 ÷ £2.47), well inside the first year.

At 500 units a year the same changes save £1,235 a year, and the payback takes more than three years. The packaging change on its own, at £0.29 a unit, would save £145 a year at that volume, which is unlikely to cover even a drop test.

Two cautions. The overhead saving assumes overhead is charged as a percentage, as a supplier's loading usually is; a fixed overhead of your own will not fall. And the snap fits must still allow any service access the product needs, proven by drop and opening tests.

Common mistakes

Cutting cost that customers value. A thinner wall that flexes, a cheaper switch that feels loose or a flimsier box can lose more in returns, reviews and price than it saves.
Changes without validation. A part that looks equivalent can behave differently in a drop, in heat or after months of use. Prove each change on production-process parts before switching.
Chasing small savings on low-volume parts. A few pence on a part made a few hundred times a year rarely repays the engineering, samples and testing it needs.
Ignoring tooling change costs. Tool modifications, new tools, scrapped stock of the old part and supplier set-up charges all belong in the payback calculation.
Forgetting certification. A material, component or construction change can affect the evidence behind your declaration. Check before the change, not after.

Decision rules

  • Measure first: rebuild the full unit cost before choosing any change.
  • Rank by annual saving (saving per unit × units per year), then by risk and one-off cost.
  • Prefer removing parts, operations and unneeded precision over cheapening what remains.
  • Validate and record every change, and check whether it touches certification.
  • Leave alone what customers notice and value, unless they tell you otherwise.

FAQ

What is value engineering?

A structured review of what each part, feature and process step does for the customer, looking for ways to deliver the same function for less cost. On a product in production it is usually called cost reduction or cost-down.

Where should I look first to reduce product cost?

Rebuild the full unit cost and look at the largest lines first. Bought-in parts, assembly time, scrap and packaging are often bigger than expected, and changes that remove parts or operations usually save more than squeezing the price of what remains.

Do I need to retest after a cost-down change?

Usually, yes, for whatever the change could affect. A new material, a different joint or smaller packaging should be checked with the relevant drop, strength, temperature or transit tests on parts made by the production process.

Can a cost-down change affect my product certification?

It can. Changes to materials, the enclosure, electronics, power supply, labelling or construction may affect the evidence behind your declaration of conformity. Check with whoever maintains your technical file, or your test house, before making the change.

How do I know if a cost saving is worth making?

Multiply the saving per unit by the units you expect to sell each year, then compare it with the one-off cost of tooling changes, samples and testing to find the payback. Favour changes that pay back quickly and carry little risk.
