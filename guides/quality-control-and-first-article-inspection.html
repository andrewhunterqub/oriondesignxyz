Quality control and first-article inspection for new products

A practical approach to quality for a new product: define what good looks like, check the first production parts in full, inspect during production and before shipment, and agree how problems will be handled before they happen.

Quick answer

Agree in writing what "good" means before production, check the first parts against it in full, then sample every batch against the same standard.

Quality control starts with a clear specification: drawings with tolerances, critical-to-quality features, cosmetic standards and golden samples. A first-article inspection (FAI) measures and tests the first production parts against every requirement before volume production is approved. After that, incoming, in-process and pre-shipment inspections catch problems while they are still cheap to fix.

Why quality control needs planning

A factory can only meet a standard it has been given. Many disputes between brands and manufacturers come from requirements that were never written down: a colour that was "close enough", a gap that was within the drawing but felt wrong, or a function nobody tested. Planning quality early is far cheaper than sorting or reworking a shipment.

Step 1: define what good looks like

  • Drawings and tolerances for every part, with the critical dimensions marked. See the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator.
  • Critical-to-quality (CTQ) features: the few dimensions and functions that decide whether the product works, is safe or looks right.
  • Cosmetic standards: which surfaces matter, which defects are acceptable, at what viewing distance and lighting.
  • Golden samples: approved reference units, signed off and kept by both you and the factory.
  • Functional tests: what each unit, or each sample, must do before it ships.
  • Packaging and labelling requirements, including drop and transit expectations.

Step 2: first-article inspection

A first-article inspection checks the first parts made with production tooling, materials and processes against every requirement on the drawings and specification. It confirms that the process can make the part correctly before you commit to volume.

FAI covers What to check
Dimensions Every dimension on the drawing, with the critical ones measured on several parts, and results recorded in an inspection report
Materials Material grade, colour and finish match the specification, with supplier certificates where relevant
Assembly and fit Parts assemble as intended, with correct gaps, alignment and fastener torque
Function The product passes its functional tests and any safety checks
Cosmetics Surfaces compared with the golden sample and cosmetic standard

For moulded parts this usually follows tool trials (T1, T2 and so on) and texture or colour approval. See Ready for Tooling and the injection moulding DFM checklist. Repeat the FAI whenever the tool, material, process or supplier changes. Mould tool trials and sample approval explains what to ask for with each trial round and how to read the first moulded samples. Once first articles are approved, a pilot production run checks that the full line makes good, packed products repeatedly before volume.

Practical rule: do not approve volume production from a sample that was hand-finished or made on a different process. Approve parts made exactly as production parts will be made.

Step 3: inspection during production

Stage Purpose
Incoming inspection Check bought-in parts and materials before they are used
In-process checks Catch drift early, for example measuring critical dimensions at set intervals
End-of-line testing Functional or safety tests on each unit, or on a defined sample
Pre-shipment inspection A sample from the finished, packed batch checked before it leaves the factory, often by an independent inspector

Sampling and acceptance levels

Inspecting every unit is rarely practical, so batches are usually checked by sampling. Acceptance sampling standards such as ISO 2859-1 set how many units to inspect for a given batch size and how many defects are acceptable, expressed as an acceptance quality limit (AQL), the term ISO 2859-1 uses (older texts say acceptable quality level). Agree the AQL for critical, major and minor defects with your manufacturer before production, with critical defects (safety or regulatory) usually allowed none.

When things go wrong

  • Contain: stop shipment and separate suspect stock.
  • Find the root cause: tool wear, material change, process drift or an unclear specification.
  • Correct and prevent: fix the cause, update the specification or checks, and confirm with a fresh inspection. For problems found by customers, see after launch: returns, feedback and design changes.
  • Agree responsibility in advance in your supply agreement: who pays for sorting, rework and replacement.

Common mistakes

No written cosmetic standard. "It looks wrong" is hard to enforce without agreed criteria and golden samples.
Skipping FAI to save time. Problems found in a full shipment are far more expensive than in the first parts.
Only checking at the end. Pre-shipment inspection finds problems after the batch is made; earlier checks prevent them.
Not repeating checks after changes. A new material, tool repair or second supplier can change the parts without anyone noticing.

FAQ

What is a first-article inspection?

A full check of the first parts made with production tooling, materials and processes against every requirement on the drawings and specification, covering dimensions, materials, fit, function and cosmetics, before volume production is approved.

When should a first-article inspection be repeated?

Whenever something that could change the part changes: a new or repaired tool, a different material or supplier, a process change or a move to another factory.

What is AQL in quality control?

The acceptance quality limit (often still called the acceptable quality level): the defect level used with a sampling standard such as ISO 2859-1 to decide whether a batch passes, based on inspecting a sample. Brands usually set separate limits for critical, major and minor defects, with none allowed for critical defects.

What is a golden sample?

An approved reference unit, signed off by the brand and the manufacturer, that production is compared against, especially for colour, finish and feel that drawings cannot fully describe.

Do I need an independent inspector?

Not always, but an independent pre-shipment inspection is common when manufacturing overseas or with a new supplier, because it gives an objective check before you pay for and ship the goods.
