Product liability basics: what product developers should know

An overview of product liability for people developing physical products: who can be responsible when a product causes harm, the kinds of defect, and practical steps from design to insurance that reduce the risk. Take professional advice for your situation.

Quick answer

If your product causes harm because it is defective, you may be liable even if you were not careless, so design, test, document and insure with that in mind.

In the UK, product liability law can make producers, including importers and businesses that put their own brand on a product, responsible for damage caused by defective products. Reduce the risk through safe design, testing, clear instructions and warnings, traceability and good records, and talk to an insurer and a solicitor before launch. This guide is an overview, not legal advice.

What product liability means

Product liability is responsibility for harm caused by a product. In Great Britain, Part 1 of the Consumer Protection Act 1987 sets out liability for damage caused by defective products, without the injured person needing to prove negligence, with equivalent provisions in Northern Ireland. Claims can also arise under contract and negligence. Separately, product safety regulations require that products placed on the market are safe; see the official guidance on product safety for manufacturers.

Who can be liable

  • The manufacturer of the finished product or a component.
  • The importer that brings the product into the UK from outside.
  • An own-brander that presents itself as the producer by putting its name or brand on the product.
  • Suppliers in some circumstances, for example if they cannot identify who supplied them.

For a start-up having products made by a contract manufacturer and sold under its own brand, this usually means the start-up carries producer responsibilities.

Kinds of defect

Defect Example How to reduce the risk
Design A hinge that can trap fingers in normal use Risk assessment, design reviews, foreseeable misuse, testing
Manufacturing A batch moulded in the wrong material that cracks Specifications, first-article inspection, production checks, traceability
Warnings and instructions Missing guidance on safe charging Clear instructions, warnings and labels, tested with real users
Practical rule: design out hazards first, guard against what remains, and only then rely on warnings. A warning does not make an unsafe design acceptable.

Reducing the risk

  1. Assess risks early, including foreseeable misuse and vulnerable users such as children.
  2. Design for safety and meet the regulations and standards that apply; see product certification basics.
  3. Test with a structured plan; see product testing and validation plans.
  4. Control production quality; see quality control and first-article inspection.
  5. Write clear instructions and warnings.
  6. Make products traceable with batch or serial numbers, so problems can be traced and recalls targeted.
  7. Keep records of design decisions, risk assessments, tests and supplier certificates.
  8. Agree responsibilities in supply contracts, including quality, indemnities and insurance.
  9. Insure: discuss product liability insurance with a broker before you sell.
  10. Plan for problems: how you would handle complaints, incidents and, if needed, a recall. After launch: returns, feedback and design changes covers logging complaints and controlling fixes.

Common mistakes

Assuming the factory is responsible. If you sell under your brand or import the product, you may be treated as the producer.
Relying on warnings. Warnings support a safe design; they do not replace it.
No traceability. Without batch records, a small problem can mean recalling everything.
Insurance as an afterthought. Retailers and marketplaces may ask for proof of cover before listing your product.

FAQ

What is product liability?

Legal responsibility for harm caused by a product. In the UK, producers of defective products that cause damage can be liable without the injured person proving negligence, and claims can also arise in contract and negligence.

Am I liable if a factory makes my product?

Possibly. If you import the product into the UK or present yourself as the producer by putting your brand on it, you may be treated as a producer with liability for defects, even though another company made it.

Do I need product liability insurance?

It is strongly advisable for most physical products, and retailers and marketplaces may require it. Discuss the cover you need with an insurance broker before you start selling.

Do warnings protect me from liability?

Clear warnings and instructions matter, but they do not make an unsafe design acceptable. Design out hazards first, guard against what remains, then warn about residual risks.

How can I reduce product liability risk?

Assess risks early, design for safety, meet applicable regulations, test thoroughly, control production quality, write clear instructions, make products traceable, keep records, agree supplier responsibilities and insure.
