Calculating hardware startup cash flow

Hardware businesses fail on cash more often than on spreadsheet profit. The difference is timing: when cash leaves for tooling, stock, and overhead versus when customers or channels finally pay you.

Audience Founders and commercial leads
Tool Cashflow Projection Tool

Quick answer

Cash flow is a timing problem, not just a margin problem.

Closing cash = opening cash + cash received - cash paid

To calculate hardware startup cash flow, list each month's cash in (customer and channel payments, when they actually arrive) and cash out (upfront development and tooling, stock, overhead), then carry the closing balance forward. A business can grow in revenue and still run out of cash if it pays for stock and overhead before customer cash arrives.

This is especially common in hardware because growth often increases the need for working capital. You buy more stock, carry more packaging, pay more suppliers, and still wait thirty or sixty days for cash to return. Software businesses can often scale with little extra cash tied up. Hardware businesses cannot: every extra unit sold usually has to be paid for first.

Key definitions

Monthly net cash flow = cash received - (stock payments + overhead + one-off spend)
Runway (months) = cash available / average monthly net cash outflow
Cash conversion cycle (days) = days stock is held + days customers take to pay - days you take to pay suppliers

The cash conversion cycle is the most useful single idea here. It measures how long each pound spent on stock is away from the business before it comes back. The longer the cycle, the more cash growth consumes.

The four cashflow traps hardware teams hit early

Inventory is paid before it is sold. Even if the business is profitable per unit, the stock still has to be financed first. Many overseas suppliers ask for a deposit at order and the balance before shipment, weeks before the stock reaches a customer.
Channels pay late. Marketplaces, distributors, and retailers often introduce a delay between sale and cash receipt.
Upfront project spend is treated as one-off and ignored. Tooling, development, compliance, and launch assets can create a hole that growth does not refill quickly enough.
Fixed overhead keeps leaving every month. Rent, people, software, contractors, and basic operations do not wait for the revenue curve to mature.

What a useful early cashflow model needs

  1. opening cash
  2. one-off launch or development spend, placed in the month it is actually paid
  3. monthly unit sales and growth
  4. COGS per unit, including freight, duty and packaging
  5. fixed monthly overhead
  6. payment delay from customers or channels
  7. supplier payment terms and minimum order quantities, once you have them

The business does not run out of cash because growth is bad. It runs out of cash because growth changes the timing of cash faster than the team planned for.

A worked example

Illustrative figures only. Start with GBP85,000 in cash, spend GBP18,000 upfront on development or tooling, sell 120 units in month 1, grow 12% per month, and carry GBP22 of COGS on a GBP49 sale price. Add GBP9,500 of monthly overhead and a one-month payment delay. Stock is paid in the month it is sold.

Each unit contributes GBP27, so the business needs about 352 units a month just to cover overhead. Sales do not reach that level until month 11, and because cash arrives a month late, the bank balance keeps falling for the whole first year.

Month Units sold Cash in Cash out Closing cash
1 120 GBP0 GBP30,140 GBP54,860
3 151 GBP6,566 GBP12,822 GBP42,036
6 211 GBP9,261 GBP14,142 GBP25,959
9 297 GBP12,985 GBP16,034 GBP14,818
12 417 GBP18,277 GBP18,674 GBP10,759

The low point is roughly GBP10,800 in month 12, and cash only starts to rise in month 13. The P&L may look healthy later in the year, but the cash graph dips much earlier because month 1 and 2 outflows happen before revenue arrives.

Now change one input. If customers or channels pay after two months instead of one, the same business runs out of cash in month 10 and is still roughly GBP9,600 short in month 14. Nothing about the product or the margin changed. Only the timing did.

Practical rule: find the month with the lowest closing cash in your most cautious scenario, then make sure the business can survive it with a buffer of several months' overhead.

Common cash flow modelling mistakes

Modelling profit instead of cash. Profit records a sale when it happens. Cash records it when the money lands. In hardware the gap can be months.
Annual or quarterly totals. The low point usually sits inside a single month. Model monthly for at least the first eighteen months.
Ignoring minimum order quantities. A supplier MOQ can force you to buy several months of stock at once, which often creates a deeper low point than launch itself.
Forgetting tax timing. VAT and import duty paid on stock, and VAT collected on sales, move cash between periods. Check the timing with your accountant rather than assuming it nets out.
No re-order lead time. If stock takes ten to twelve weeks to arrive, the next order must be paid for well before the current batch is sold.

How to use the model well

  • run a conservative, expected, and optimistic sales case
  • test both same-month payment and delayed-payment scenarios
  • check whether your first large stock order creates the real low point, not the launch month
  • pair the result with startup-cost and break-even tools, not in isolation; building a hardware financial model shows how the pieces connect

Practical next steps

  1. Estimate the pre-launch spend with the Startup Cost Planner and the approach in estimating hardware development costs.
  2. Ask likely suppliers and channels for their payment terms, deposits and MOQs, even informally.
  3. Build a monthly model in the Cashflow Projection Tool and identify the low point in each scenario.
  4. Check how far away unit break-even is using financial break-even for hardware products.
  5. Decide how much funding, or which changes to terms, stock or launch timing, would keep the low point safely above zero.

FAQ

What is the difference between cash flow and profit?

Profit measures whether sales exceed costs over a period. Cash flow measures when money actually enters and leaves the bank. A hardware business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash because it pays for stock and tooling long before customers or channels pay it.

Why do growing hardware businesses run out of cash?

Because each extra unit sold usually has to be bought, shipped and stored before it is paid for. Faster growth means larger stock orders sooner, so more cash is tied up in inventory and unpaid invoices at any one time.

How do I calculate cash runway?

Divide the cash you have by your average monthly net cash outflow. For an early hardware business, a monthly cash flow model is more reliable than a single average, because burn changes as stock orders, launch costs and sales arrive at different times.

How much runway does a hardware startup need?

There is no fixed rule, but many founders aim for twelve to eighteen months of runway after a funding round. Hardware often needs the upper end, or more, because tooling, certification and manufacturing lead times make delays common and expensive.

What is working capital in a hardware business?

Working capital is the cash tied up in running the business day to day, mainly stock, goods in transit and money owed by customers, minus what you owe suppliers. Longer supplier lead times and slower customer payment both increase the working capital you need.
