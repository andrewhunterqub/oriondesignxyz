Design for repair and disassembly: making products that last longer

A practical approach to repairable products: find the parts that will fail first, design so they can be reached and replaced, support repair after sale, and plan for separating materials at the end of life, while managing the trade-offs with cost, sealing and safety.

Quick answer

Design so the parts most likely to fail can be reached, removed and replaced with ordinary tools, and so materials can be separated at the end of life.

Identify which parts wear out or fail first, such as batteries, buttons, seals, hinges and cables, and make those easy to access. Prefer screws and clips to glue, use standard fasteners and few tool types, make spare parts and repair information available, and avoid permanently joining different materials. A longer, repairable life usually does more for a product's environmental impact than recyclable materials alone.

Why repairability matters

The impact of making a product is spent once, so every extra year of use spreads it further. Products that cannot be opened or repaired are often replaced for the sake of one failed part. Repairability also reduces warranty costs, supports a reputation for quality, and is increasingly expected by customers and regulators. See why product lifetime matters more than recycling.

Step 1: find the parts that will fail first

Before choosing fixings, list the likely failure points. Typical candidates:

  • Batteries lose capacity with charge cycles and age.
  • Moving parts: buttons, switches, hinges, latches, motors and bearings.
  • Seals and gaskets harden and crack over time; sealing an enclosure with replaceable O-rings or gaskets keeps them serviceable.
  • Cables and connectors flex, wear and break at stress points.
  • Cosmetic surfaces that scratch, such as lenses, screens and covers.
  • Consumables such as filters, blades and brushes.

Field returns from similar products (see after launch: returns, feedback and design changes for how to log them), accelerated life tests and your own design reviews show which matter most. Plan these in your test plan.

Step 2: design for access and disassembly

Principle In practice
Reversible fixings Screws, clips and snap fits that can be undone, rather than adhesives and welds, for parts that may need replacing
Few, common tools Standard screw heads and as few different types and sizes as possible
Short disassembly paths The parts that fail first should come out first, without removing everything else
Modular sub-assemblies Group parts into modules that can be swapped as a unit, such as a battery pack or a switch module
Connectors, not solder Plug-in connections for parts likely to be replaced
Clear and robust Fixings and clips that survive being opened more than once, with features that guide reassembly
Practical rule: for each likely failure, count the steps and tools needed to replace it. If replacing the most common failure needs more than a few steps or a special tool, look for a better design.

Step 3: support repair after sale

  • Spare parts for the common failures, available for a reasonable period after the product goes off sale.
  • Repair information: exploded views, part numbers and short guides, for professional repairers and, where safe, users.
  • Software and firmware that does not block genuine replacement parts without a safety reason.

Step 4: design for end of life

  • Avoid permanently joining different materials, such as over-moulding incompatible plastics or bonding metal to plastic, where separation matters. Joining methods compared shows which joints can be undone.
  • Mark plastic parts with their material, and use fewer material types overall.
  • Make batteries easy to remove; battery and electrical producer responsibilities may apply (see the Sustainability and product life hub).

Trade-offs to manage

Repairable designs can conflict with other goals: screws add parts and assembly time, opening a product can affect water resistance or safety, and modularity can add size and cost. The answer is rarely all or nothing; target repairability at the failures that actually happen, and check the assembly effect with the Assembly Time Estimator. Where sealing or safety rule out user repair, make professional repair practical instead.

Regulation

Repairability requirements are growing, especially for energy-related products under ecodesign rules, which can include spare-part availability and repair information. Requirements differ by product category and between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and the EU, so check current guidance on energy-related products for your category, and see the right to repair and ecodesign rules overview.

Common mistakes

Gluing everything for a slimmer product. It often ends the product's life at the first battery or button failure.
Making the wrong parts repairable. Focus on what actually fails, not what is easiest to make removable.
One-time clips. Clips that break when opened turn a repair into a replacement.
No spare parts plan. A repairable design without available parts is not repairable in practice.

FAQ

What is design for repair?

Designing a product so the parts most likely to fail can be reached, removed and replaced with ordinary tools, and supporting that with spare parts and repair information. It extends product life and reduces waste.

Which parts should be easy to replace?

The ones that fail or wear first: typically batteries, buttons and switches, hinges and latches, seals, cables and connectors, cosmetic covers and consumables. Use field returns and life testing to decide.

Does design for repair cost more?

It can add parts or assembly time, for example screws instead of glue. Targeted at the failures that actually happen, the cost is often modest, and can be offset by lower warranty costs and a longer-lasting reputation.

Is design for repair required by law?

For some product categories, ecodesign rules include repair-related requirements such as spare-part availability and repair information, and these are expanding. Requirements differ by category and between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and the EU, so check current guidance.

How do repair and recycling relate?

Repair keeps the product in use, which usually saves far more than recycling at the end of life. Designing for disassembly helps both: it makes repair easier and lets materials be separated for recycling.
