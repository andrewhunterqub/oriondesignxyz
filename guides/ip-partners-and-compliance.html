IP, partners and compliance: protecting a product, working with partners and meeting the rules

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about protecting a physical product and putting it on the market legally: the types of intellectual property, the disclosure mistake to avoid, what to agree with designers and manufacturers, and how UK product compliance and safety duties work. It is general information with links to official sources, not legal advice.

Quick answer

Decide early what to protect and how, put partner relationships in writing, and design for the regulations that apply from the start.

Patents protect how a new invention works and must be applied for before the idea is made public. Registered designs protect appearance; trade marks protect the brand; copyright and unregistered design right arise automatically but give narrower protection. Use confidentiality agreements and clear contracts with designers and manufacturers, especially on who owns the design and the tooling. And identify the product regulations and standards that apply before the design is fixed, because compliance problems found after tooling are expensive. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Types of intellectual property for physical products

Right What it protects How you get it Points to know
Patent How a new invention works: a mechanism, process or technical solution Application to the Intellectual Property Office, examination, fees Must be new and inventive; can last up to 20 years with renewal fees; expensive to file and defend internationally
Registered design The appearance of a product: shape, lines, contours, decoration Application to the Intellectual Property Office Cheaper and quicker than a patent; renewable every 5 years up to 25 years
Unregistered design right The shape and configuration of a product Automatic Protects against copying only, and is harder to prove than a registration
Trade mark Brand names, logos and other signs Registration for specific classes of goods and services Renewable every 10 years; check availability before you invest in a name
Copyright Drawings, manuals, software, artwork, photographs Automatic Make sure contracts say who owns work produced for you
Confidential information Know-how, unpublished designs, processes, supplier details Keeping it secret, supported by confidentiality agreements Lost once it becomes public

Many physical products rely on a combination: perhaps a patent on one key mechanism, a registered design on the appearance, a trade mark on the name, and confidentiality for manufacturing know-how. The Intellectual Property Office's overview of intellectual property is a good starting point, and how to protect product IP before talking to manufacturers covers the practical steps.

Disclosure: the mistake that cannot be undone

An invention generally needs to be new when a patent application is filed. Showing it publicly first, through a crowdfunding page, a trade show, a social media post or an unprotected pitch, can prevent you from patenting it. Registered designs have different rules on disclosure, but the safest approach is the same: take advice and decide what to file before the product is shown outside a confidential circle.

Practical rule: until you have decided on protection, share only under a confidentiality agreement, share only what the other party needs, and keep a dated record of what you shared and with whom.

Working with designers, manufacturers and distributors

Many IP problems with partners come from things that were never written down. Agree these points before work starts:

  • Confidentiality: a non-disclosure agreement before sharing designs. The NDA Generator produces a starting draft to review.
  • Ownership of design work: make sure work done for you by a designer or consultancy is assigned to your company, not just licensed.
  • Tooling: who owns the tools you paid for, where they are kept, maintenance, and how you can move them to another supplier.
  • Quality and specifications: drawings, tolerances, inspection and what happens when parts are out of specification.
  • Exclusivity and territory: for distributors and licensees, which products, which countries, for how long, and what targets apply.

Good documents also make partners more effective. See the documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer. Supply agreements with a manufacturer sets out the topics to agree before tooling and the first order. For contracts with real value, have them reviewed by a solicitor.

Product compliance in the UK

Consumer products placed on the UK market must be safe, and many categories have specific regulations: electrical equipment, electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), radio equipment, toys, machinery, medical devices and others. For many of these, the manufacturer must show conformity, keep a technical file, sign a declaration of conformity and apply a marking.

Identify the regulations at the requirements stage, not after the design is finished (product certification basics covers the process in more detail): they can affect materials, enclosures, labelling, instructions and test budgets. Pre-compliance testing during development, described in the Development and prototyping hub, reduces the risk of failing formal tests. Official starting points: using the UKCA marking, CE marking, and the Office for Product Safety and Standards.

Northern Ireland and Great Britain

Product marking rules differ between Great Britain and Northern Ireland. Broadly, products for Northern Ireland follow the EU rules and use CE marking, with UKNI marking in some cases, while Great Britain uses UKCA marking and also recognises CE marking for many product types. General consumer product safety in Northern Ireland is also covered by the EU General Product Safety Regulation, which has applied there since 13 December 2024. The rules have changed several times and differ by product category, so check the current guidance for your product before printing labels or packaging.

Product safety and liability

Whoever places a product on the market is responsible for it being safe, and producers can be liable for harm caused by defective products. Practical protection comes from a documented risk assessment, testing to recognised standards, clear instructions and warnings, traceability of batches, a process for handling complaints and recalls, and appropriate product liability insurance. Insurers and retailers may ask for evidence of these. GOV.UK's product safety guidance for manufacturers sets out the duties.

Common mistakes

Showing the product before deciding on protection. A public post or pitch can prevent a later patent.
Assuming you own work you paid for. Without an assignment, a designer may own the copyright or design rights in their work.
No written agreement on tool ownership. Moving production becomes difficult or impossible.
Discovering regulations at the test house. Requirements that should have shaped the design arrive as expensive changes.
Relying on an NDA as complete protection. An NDA helps keep information confidential but does not stop independent development and is only as useful as your ability to enforce it.

FAQ

Should I patent my product idea?

Only if the invention is genuinely new and inventive, the protection would be commercially valuable, and you can afford to file and enforce it in the markets that matter. Many products are better protected by a registered design, a trade mark, speed to market and confidentiality. Take advice from a registered patent attorney before you disclose the idea.

Does an NDA protect my product idea?

It helps keep shared information confidential and makes clear the other party may not use it, but it does not stop someone developing a similar idea independently, and enforcement can be costly. Use an NDA alongside sharing only what is necessary and deciding on formal protection early.

Who owns the design if I pay a designer or consultancy?

Not automatically you. Copyright and design rights can stay with the person who created the work unless the contract assigns them to you. Make sure your agreement includes an assignment of intellectual property in the work produced for you.

Do I need UKCA or CE marking?

Many product categories, including electrical equipment, radio equipment, toys and machinery, need a conformity marking. Great Britain uses UKCA and recognises CE marking for many products; Northern Ireland uses CE marking, with UKNI in some cases. Check the official GOV.UK guidance for your product category.

Who is responsible if my product causes harm?

Whoever places the product on the market has duties to make sure it is safe, and producers can be liable for harm caused by defective products. A documented risk assessment, testing, clear instructions, traceability and product liability insurance are the usual protections. This is general information; take legal advice for your situation.
