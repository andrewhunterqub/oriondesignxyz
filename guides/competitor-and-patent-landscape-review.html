Competitor and patent landscape review: mapping what already exists

How to map the products customers can already buy and the patents and designs around your idea: where to look, what to record, and how to turn findings into positioning, requirements, price and IP decisions.

Quick answer

Before investing in development, map what already exists: the products customers can buy today, how they compare, and the patents and designs around the idea.

A landscape review shows where there is room for your product, what customers already accept on price and features, which ideas have been tried, and which rights might limit you. Keep it proportionate: a few focused days early can save months of developing something that already exists or cannot be sold freely.

Why review the landscape

  • Avoid reinventing something already on sale, or already tried and abandoned.
  • Find the gap: what existing products do badly, and who they leave out.
  • Set expectations for price, features and quality from what customers already buy.
  • Spot IP risks early, while the design is easy to change.
  • Strengthen your case for investors, grant applications and retailers.

Part 1: the competitor review

Include direct competitors, indirect alternatives and the do-nothing or workaround option customers use today.

Look at Where to find it
Products, features and prices Retailers, marketplaces, brand websites, trade catalogues
What customers like and dislike Reviews, forums, social media, customer interviews
How products are made Buying and taking apart key competitors (a teardown)
Where they are sold Retail and online channels, distributors, trade shows
Failed or abandoned products Crowdfunding archives, discontinued listings, industry news

Summarise the findings in a simple comparison grid of features, price, strengths and weaknesses, and note where your concept is clearly better. The Pugh Concept Selector can score your concepts against the best existing product as a baseline.

Practical rule: read the one- and two-star reviews of the leading products. They are often the clearest list of unmet needs you will find.

Part 2: the patent and design landscape

  1. Describe the product's key features and functions in plain words, and list alternative terms for each.
  2. Search patents and published applications through the official patent search routes and the international databases they link to.
  3. Search registered designs for similar-looking products.
  4. Note who holds patents in the area, how recent they are, and the countries they cover.
  5. Flag anything close to your product for professional review.

Patent documents are also a rich source of technical ideas and of approaches others have tried. An early search is not a legal opinion; see freedom to operate explained and design registration vs patents.

Turn findings into decisions

  • Positioning: which customers and needs you will serve better than the alternatives.
  • Requirements: must-have features customers already expect, and the differences that matter. Add them to your product requirements document.
  • Price range: where your product can sit; check the margins with the Pricing Ladder.
  • IP actions: features to design around, protect or have checked.

Common mistakes

"There is no competition." There is almost always an alternative, even if it is a workaround or doing nothing.
Only looking at direct competitors. Customers compare across categories when they solve the same problem.
Searching patents with one keyword. Patents often use unusual terms; search by function and use several phrasings.
Doing it once. Markets and patent filings change; refresh the review at major decisions.

FAQ

What is a competitor and patent landscape review?

A review of the products customers can already buy, how they compare on features, price and quality, and the patents and registered designs in the same area, done early to find the gap and spot risks.

How do I find competitors for a new product?

Look at retailers, marketplaces, brand websites, trade catalogues and shows, crowdfunding archives and reviews, and ask target customers what they use today, including workarounds and doing nothing.

How do I search for patents?

Describe the product's features in plain words with alternative terms, then search official patent databases, starting from the GOV.UK patent search pages and the international databases they link to. Flag anything close for professional review.

Is a patent search the same as a freedom to operate opinion?

No. An early search helps you understand the landscape and spot obvious risks; a freedom to operate opinion is a professional judgement on whether your specific product infringes rights in force where you sell it.

How long should a landscape review take?

Keep it proportionate to the investment. A few focused days early in a project is often enough to make better decisions, with deeper reviews before major commitments such as tooling.
