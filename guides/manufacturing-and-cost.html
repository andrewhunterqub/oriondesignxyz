Manufacturing and cost: how physical products are made, and what drives the price

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about making a physical product: which processes suit which volumes, what actually sets the unit cost, when tooling is worth paying for, and how to get to a number you can plan around. Each section links to a deeper guide or a free tool.

Quick answer

Unit cost is materials, process time and labour, plus tooling shared across the volume you will really sell.

Unit cost ≈ materials + process time × machine rate + assembly labour + packaging + (tooling ÷ lifetime volume) + supplier overhead and margin

The manufacturing route you choose decides which of those terms dominates. Low-volume processes such as 3D printing and CNC machining need little or no tooling but cost more per part. High-volume processes such as injection moulding and die casting need expensive tooling but make each part cheaply. The right route is the one that fits your realistic volume, how settled the design is, and how much cash you can commit before you sell.

Manufacturing processes compared

Most physical products are made from a handful of process families. The table gives typical working ranges, not hard limits: the right answer for a given part depends on its size, material, tolerances and finish, and a supplier quote will always beat a rule of thumb.

Process Typical volumes Upfront tooling Cost per part Typical use
3D printing (FDM, SLA, SLS, MJF) One-offs to a few hundred None High, and roughly flat with volume Prototypes, complex low-volume parts, jigs and fixtures
CNC machining One-offs to low thousands Fixtures and programming only High, falling slowly as set-up is shared Metal parts, precise features, works-like prototypes
Vacuum (urethane) casting Tens to a few hundred Low: a master pattern and silicone moulds, each good for a limited number of shots Medium Production-looking prototypes, pilot batches, early sales units
Injection moulding, aluminium tool Hundreds to tens of thousands Medium Low Bridge production while demand and design settle
Injection moulding, steel tool Tens of thousands to millions High Lowest Stable designs at volume
Sheet metal (laser cutting and bending) One-offs to tens of thousands None for laser and press-brake work; press tools only at high volume Medium Enclosures, brackets, chassis (see sheet metal design basics)
Vacuum forming and thermoforming Tens to thousands Low to medium Low to medium Covers, trays, large thin shells, packaging inserts
Die casting Thousands upwards High Low Metal housings and structural parts at volume

Processes are not either-or for the life of a product. Many products start in 3D printing or vacuum casting, move to an aluminium tool for the first production runs, and only commit to hardened steel tooling once sales and design have settled. How tooling works is covered in injection moulding explained. That sequence is described in the risk-progressive manufacturing strategy and prototyping versus production.

How volume changes cost

Every route has a fixed part (tooling, fixtures, set-up, programming) and a variable part (material, machine time, labour per unit). Cost per part is the fixed part divided by the number of parts, plus the variable part. Low-tooling routes keep the fixed part small, so they win at low volume. Tooled routes have a large fixed part but a much smaller variable part, so they win once volume is high enough.

The crossover point is a simple calculation: tooling cost ÷ (cost per part on the cheaper-to-start route − cost per part on the tooled route). The Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser draws these curves for your own figures, and when does injection moulding become cheaper? explains why the break-even line alone is not enough to decide.

What drives unit cost

The same product can cost very different amounts depending on decisions made in design. These are the drivers that most often move the number:

Driver Why it matters What usually reduces it
Part count Every part needs its own tool or set-up, its own stock, and assembly time Combine parts, use snap fits or living hinges where they suit the load and life
Material Material price per kilogram varies many-fold between commodity and engineering grades Choose the least demanding grade that meets the real requirement (see choosing plastic materials); reduce part mass
Cycle time In moulding and casting, thick sections cool slowly and machine time is charged by the hour Even, appropriate wall thickness; ribs instead of solid sections
Tolerances Tight tolerances need slower processes, extra operations and more inspection Hold tight tolerances only on features that genuinely need them
Finishes Painting, plating, texturing and printing add operations, handling and scrap Moulded-in colour and texture; fewer cosmetic surfaces
Assembly Labour is paid on every unit, for the life of the product Fewer fasteners, self-locating parts, one assembly direction (see estimating assembly time)
Yield and scrap Rejected parts are paid for by the good ones Robust design margins and a stable, well-documented process
Order quantity Suppliers price set-up and minimum order quantities into small batches Plan batch sizes against cash, storage and demand rather than unit price alone
Practical rule: most of a product's cost is decided in design, long before a factory quotes it. Changes are cheap on a CAD model, expensive on a prototype and very expensive once a tool is cut.

Tooling decisions

Tooling is often one of the largest single commitments between prototype and launch, and one that is easy to make too early. A few principles help:

  • Tool life should match the plan. As a broad rule of thumb, aluminium injection-mould tools suit volumes in the thousands to tens of thousands of shots, and hardened steel tools suit hundreds of thousands or more. Actual life depends heavily on the plastic, the part geometry and how the tool is built, so ask the toolmaker to state it.
  • Expect modifications. First samples (often called T1) rarely come off a new tool perfect. Budget time and money for at least one round of changes before production approval.
  • Agree ownership in writing. Make sure the contract says who owns the tool, where it is kept, what maintenance is included, and how you can move it to another supplier.
  • Check the design before quoting. Run the injection moulding DFM checklist on your CAD so the tool is quoted on a mouldable design.
  • Tool when the design has stopped moving. If user testing, certification or a key component could still change the geometry, a cheaper bridge route usually costs less overall than modifying a production tool.
  • Count the whole set. A product with eight moulded parts may need eight tools (or multi-cavity and family tools with their own trade-offs), plus assembly and test fixtures.

Worked example: one enclosure part, three routes

Illustrative figures only. A start-up needs a palm-sized plastic enclosure part. Quotes come back at about £14 a part for SLS 3D printing, about £9 a part plus £300 set-up for CNC machining, and £16,000 for an injection-mould tool with parts at about £1.20 each. The team expects to sell 600 units in the first year and is fairly confident of 3,000 over three years.

Volume 3D printing CNC machining Injection moulding
100 parts £1,400 (£14.00 each) £1,200 (£12.00 each) £16,120 (£161.20 each)
600 parts £8,400 (£14.00 each) £5,700 (£9.50 each) £16,720 (£27.87 each)
1,250 parts £17,500 (£14.00 each) £11,550 (£9.24 each) £17,500 (£14.00 each)
3,000 parts £42,000 (£14.00 each) £27,300 (£9.10 each) £19,600 (£6.53 each)

On paper, moulding beats 3D printing at about 1,250 parts and beats CNC machining at about 2,000. But the team only has confident demand for 600 units this year, and the enclosure may still change after user testing. A sensible plan would be to launch on the low-tooling route, keep the design stable for a few months of real use, then tool once the three-year volume looks real. The cash that is not tied up in a tool can fund stock and marketing instead. That is the judgement the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser and the injection moulding break-even guide are designed to support. From factory price to landed cost

The price a supplier quotes is rarely what a unit costs you. Depending on the terms of the quote, you may still need to add packaging, freight, insurance, import duty and VAT, customs brokerage, inbound handling, and warehousing before a unit is ready to sell. For parts and products made overseas these can add a substantial share on top of the ex-works price, so build them into the cost model from the start rather than discovering them on the first invoice. For duty rates and import VAT, use the official UK Trade Tariff rather than estimates.

Common mistakes

Choosing the process by unit price alone. The cheapest part at 100,000 units is often the most expensive at 1,000. Use the volume you will realistically sell, not the volume you hope for.
Tooling before the design is stable. Tool modifications cost time and money, and some changes cannot be made to an existing tool at all.
Forgetting assembly, packaging and logistics. These are paid on every unit and are often larger than the difference between two part quotes.
Tolerances copied from a default title block. Blanket tight tolerances raise cost on every feature. Specify what the function needs.
Briefing suppliers with incomplete information. Quotes are only as good as the drawings, quantities and requirements you send. See the documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer.

FAQ

What is the cheapest way to manufacture a small number of products?

For tens to a few hundred units, processes with little or no tooling are usually cheapest overall: 3D printing, CNC machining, vacuum casting, sheet metal fabrication or vacuum forming, depending on the part. The cost per part is higher, but you avoid paying for tooling that a small volume cannot recover.

At what volume does injection moulding become worth it?

There is no single number. Divide the tooling cost by the saving per part compared with your low-volume route to find the break-even volume, then ask whether you are confident of selling well beyond it and whether the design is stable enough to tool. In this page's illustrative example it falls at about 1,250 parts against 3D printing; for your own part it depends on the tool cost and the saving per part, so work it out from real quotes.

How is tooling cost included in the unit cost?

Spread it across the number of units you expect the tool to make for you, either over its expected life or over a planning horizon such as the first two or three years. In cash terms, though, tooling is paid up front, usually in stages, so it also belongs in the start-up budget and cash flow.

What has the biggest effect on manufacturing cost?

Usually decisions made in design: the number of parts, the material, wall thickness and cycle time, tolerances, finishes and how easily the product assembles. Volume and the choice of process then decide how those decisions turn into a price.

Should I manufacture in the UK or overseas?

Compare landed cost rather than the factory price: freight, duty, VAT, lead time, minimum order quantities, communication and the cost of quality problems all count. Local suppliers often suit prototypes, early batches and products that change often; overseas suppliers can suit stable products at higher volume.
