Surface finishes for plastic and metal parts

The common finishes for moulded plastic and metal parts, what each one changes, how finishes affect fits, masking and colour matching, and how to specify a finish so suppliers quote and make the same thing.

Quick answer

Choose a finish for what it must do (look, feel, wear, corrosion or cleaning) and apply cosmetic finishes only to the faces people see and touch.

For moulded plastics, the cheapest finish is usually the one built into the tool: a texture or polish level with colour in the material. For metals, bead blasting, brushing, anodising, powder coating, painting, plating and passivation each solve a different problem. Coatings add thickness, need masking and hanging points, and must be matched to an agreed colour reference, so write the finish, the faces it applies to and any masking on the drawing and in the quote request.

What a finish is for

A surface finish is anything done to a part's surface as or after it is formed: a moulded texture, a blasted or brushed surface, a conversion such as anodising, or a coating such as paint, powder or plating. It has one or more of five jobs: appearance (colour, gloss, hiding marks), feel (grip, warmth, softness), wear (resisting scratches and handling), corrosion protection, and function (electrical insulation or conductivity, cleanability, friction).

Start from the job, not the finish. A face nobody sees rarely needs more than the process gives naturally, and each extra step adds cost, handling, lead time and another thing that can go wrong.

Finishes for moulded plastic parts

Texture and polish in the tool

An injection-moulded part copies the surface of the tool. A polished tool gives a gloss part; a blasted, etched or laser-textured tool gives a matt or patterned one. Texture hides small flow lines, sink and scratches and adds grip, at no cost per part once the tool is made. Agree polish and texture by the moulder's or texture supplier's reference and a physical sample, not a description such as "light matt".

Texture needs more draft. As the injection moulding DFM checklist explains, a plain face commonly needs at least 0.5 to 1 degree of draft, but a textured face can need several degrees, and the texture supplier will usually state a minimum. Without it the texture scrapes as the part is ejected. A high gloss is the hardest finish to keep perfect: it shows every sink mark, weld line and scuff.

Colour: in the material or painted

Colouring the plastic itself, by buying a pre-coloured grade or adding a masterbatch at the press, is usually cheapest and most durable: a scratch shows the same colour underneath. Painting suits high gloss, metallic or soft-touch effects that moulded colour cannot give, or several colours on one part, but it adds a process, masking, reject risk and a coating that can chip or wear. Choosing plastic materials covers which plastics take paint well.

Printing and decoration

Pad printing transfers ink from a soft silicone pad, so it can print logos and markings onto curved or recessed surfaces. Screen printing pushes ink through a mesh and suits flat or gently curved faces and larger areas. Both need a fixture and agreed ink colours. In-mould decoration places a printed film in the tool and moulds it into the part, giving durable graphics; it adds tooling and set-up effort, so it is worth asking about at higher volumes or where graphics must not wear off. Lettering engraved into the tool is often the simplest and most durable marking of all.

Finishes for metal parts

  • Bead blasting fires fine media at the surface for an even matt texture that hides machining marks. It is often done before anodising and removes a very small amount of material.
  • Brushing gives fine parallel lines by rubbing with abrasive in one direction. It suits flat or simple faces; state the grain direction.
  • Anodising (aluminium, and a few other metals) grows a hard oxide layer out of the metal itself, partly into the surface and partly on top. Clear or dyed, it resists wear and corrosion and does not chip like paint. Hard anodising gives a thicker, tougher layer. It is electrically insulating.
  • Powder coating sprays a dry powder onto the part, then bakes it into a tough, even coat. Common on steel and aluminium sheet metal, it comes in many colours and textures, but it is noticeably thicker than other finishes and also insulates.
  • Wet painting gives thinner coats and a wide choice of colours and effects, on metal or plastic, but needs good preparation and is usually less hard-wearing than powder.
  • Plating deposits a thin metal layer, such as zinc on steel for corrosion protection or nickel and chrome for a decorative, hard-wearing surface. Some plating processes need an extra treatment for high-strength steels; ask the plater.
  • Passivation is a chemical treatment for stainless steel that removes surface contamination, such as iron from tools, so the steel's own corrosion resistance works as intended. It barely changes the look and is often specified after machining or welding.

What each finish changes

The comparison below is qualitative; the right choice depends on the material, the supplier and how the product is used.

Finish Main benefit What to watch
Moulded texture or polish Look and grip built in; no extra cost per part Needs draft; changing it means reworking the tool
Paint on plastic Gloss, metallic and soft-touch effects Extra process, masking and rejects; can chip or wear
Anodising Hard, wear and corrosion resistant, part of the metal Aluminium only in practice; insulates; dyed colours can vary between batches
Powder coating Tough, wide colour range, good protection for steel Thickest build-up; masking; hanging points; insulates
Plating Corrosion protection or decorative metal look Uneven thickness in holes and corners; process choice for the steel

Every added finish adds cost and lead time, more so when parts travel to a separate finisher. Finishes built into the tool or material cost least per part.

Designing for the finish

Thickness build-up affects fits. Coatings and anodising add material to every surface they cover, including the walls of holes and the sides of tabs. A hole gets smaller and a shaft or tab gets bigger, so a fit that worked bare can bind once finished. Decide whether each critical dimension applies before or after finishing, and allow for it; tolerances and engineering drawings explains fits and how tolerances add up.

  • Masking. Threads, bearing seats, sealing faces and earth points usually stay bare. Masking is often manual, so keep masked areas few and simple and show them on the drawing.
  • Hanging and contact points. Parts are hung on hooks or racks for coating, plating and anodising, leaving a mark. Provide a hole or edge on a hidden face and say where marks are acceptable.
  • Electrical paths. Anodising and powder coating insulate. If a metal case needs earthing or shielding, mask the contact areas; electronics in a physical product covers enclosure decisions that affect this.
  • Edges and corners. Coatings thin on sharp edges and pool in sharp inside corners; small radii help.
  • Order of operations. Either allow for the build-up on precise features, or finish first and machine them afterwards, accepting bare metal there.
  • Colour matching. The same colour on plastic and metal, or from two suppliers, rarely matches by name alone. Agree a physical colour and gloss reference, then approve golden samples made on the production process, and check against them at inspection.

Specifying a finish on a drawing and in a quote request

A clear specification lets suppliers quote, and inspectors check, the same thing. On the drawing, state:

  1. The finish and process, such as "anodise, black" or "powder coat, fine texture", plus any type, class or thickness the product needs.
  2. The colour and gloss reference: a named colour system reference or, better, an approved physical sample.
  3. Which faces it applies to, and which faces are cosmetic, often shown by shading or a note such as "cosmetic face A".
  4. Masked areas, contact points and where rack or hanging marks are acceptable.
  5. Whether dimensions apply before or after finishing, at least for the critical ones.
  6. A cosmetic standard: how blemishes are judged, at what distance and lighting.

In the request for quotation, repeat the finish for each part and ask whether suppliers finish in house or subcontract it. Finishes, repair and recycling

Finishes can extend a product's life by protecting it from wear and corrosion, usually the bigger environmental win. But some make recycling and repair harder: paint and plating on plastics can contaminate recycled material, bonded films are hard to separate, and coatings over fasteners or joints make a product hard to open without damage. Prefer colour and texture built into the material, avoid finishes that serve no purpose, and keep fasteners and service points uncoated and accessible. Worked example: a metal enclosure and a plastic housing

Illustrative example, qualitative choices only. A team has two products: a handheld measuring device with a machined aluminium enclosure, used outdoors and sometimes dropped, and a desktop unit with an injection-moulded housing that is wiped clean.

Decision Aluminium handheld Moulded desktop housing
Main jobs of the finish Wear, corrosion, grip, hide machining marks Look, hide moulding marks, easy cleaning
Chosen finish Bead blast then anodise, dyed dark grey Light texture in the tool on visible faces, colour in the material, internal faces as moulded
Rejected options Powder coat: thicker, chips on drops, covers the edges unevenly Painted gloss: extra process, shows scratches, hinders recycling
Design changes Mask the earth contact and the threaded screw holes; allow for the layer on the lid fit; radius sharp edges Increase draft on textured faces to the texture supplier's minimum; moulded-in logo instead of a print
Colour control Approved anodised sample; check batches against it Approved moulded colour and texture sample from the production tool

The aluminium part needs a finish for protection and feel; the plastic part gets almost everything from the tool and material. Both are specified face by face, with a physical reference, and fits are checked after finishing.

Common mistakes

Specifying a cosmetic finish everywhere. It raises cost and rejects on faces nobody sees. Mark cosmetic faces and leave the rest.
Forgetting thickness build-up. Coated holes shrink and coated tabs grow, so parts that fitted as prototypes can bind once finished.
No colour reference. A colour name or screen image is not a specification. Without a physical sample, batches and suppliers can differ.
Finishing tolerance-critical features without allowance. A precise bore or thread coated with no masking or allowance may need reworking or rejecting.

FAQ

What is the cheapest finish for an injection-moulded part?

Usually a texture or polish built into the tool, with colour in the material. Once the tool is made it adds no cost per part, and scratches show the same colour underneath. Painting and printing add processes, handling and rejects.

Should I anodise or powder coat aluminium?

Anodising grows a thin, hard layer from the metal itself, so it suits wear-prone, precise or handheld parts and does not chip. Powder coating is thicker, comes in more colours and suits larger sheet metal parts, but it changes fits more and can chip on impact.

Does a coating change the size of a part?

Yes. Coatings and anodising add material to every covered surface, so holes get smaller and tabs or shafts get larger. Mask critical features or allow for the build-up in the tolerances, and state whether dimensions apply before or after finishing.

What is passivation of stainless steel?

A chemical treatment that removes contamination such as iron from tools from the surface, so the steel's natural corrosion resistance works as intended. It barely changes the look, so it is easy to forget to specify after machining or welding.

How do I make sure the colour is right in production?

Agree a physical colour and gloss reference rather than a name or screen image, approve golden samples made on the production process, and check batches against them at inspection.
