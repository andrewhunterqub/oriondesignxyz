Financial break-even for hardware products

Break-even analysis answers a very narrow question: how many units must be sold to recover fixed cost at the current contribution margin. It becomes useful only when the product cost is credible and the team understands what fixed costs are actually being recovered.

Quick answer

Break-even happens when total contribution equals fixed cost.

Break-even units = fixed costs / (selling price - variable cost)

Subtract the variable cost of each unit from the net price you actually receive, then divide the fixed costs you need to recover by that contribution. The trap is treating the result as a full business decision when it is really one checkpoint inside a wider model.

For hardware, the inputs need care. The selling price should be the net price the business receives after VAT and any retailer or marketplace share. Variable cost should include everything that rises with each unit sold, not just the factory price. Get those two numbers right and break-even becomes a sharp test of whether the commercial model is viable.

Key definitions

Contribution per unit = net selling price - variable cost per unit
Break-even revenue = fixed costs / contribution margin ratio, where the ratio = contribution per unit / net selling price
Margin of safety = (forecast units - break-even units) / forecast units
Cost type Typical hardware examples
Variable cost Components, assembly, packaging, inbound freight and duty, payment fees, pick and pack, outbound shipping, returns allowance, channel commission
Fixed cost Tooling, design and engineering, certification, launch assets, and the overhead you choose to recover over the period being tested

Be explicit about which fixed costs are in the calculation. A project break-even asks how many units recover the one-off investment in development, tooling and certification. An operating break-even asks how many units a month cover recurring overhead. Both are useful, but mixing them without saying so makes the result hard to interpret.

What break-even is good at

  • testing whether the current contribution per unit is strong enough
  • showing whether the sales target is plausible at all
  • comparing how price or cost changes move the required volume
  • comparing routes to market on a like-for-like basis

What break-even is bad at

  • capturing payment timing and working-capital pressure
  • showing whether the first production run can be financed
  • including channel fees, returns, or route-to-market complexity unless those are already in variable cost
  • reflecting that unit cost usually falls as volume grows and changes when components are re-sourced

Worked example

Illustrative figures only. Recover GBP65,000 of fixed cost with a GBP49 net sale price and GBP22 of variable cost. Contribution per unit is GBP27, so break-even arrives at roughly 2,408 units, or about GBP118,000 of revenue. The contribution margin ratio is about 55%.

If the team forecasts 3,500 units in the first year, the margin of safety is about 31%: sales could fall by almost a third before the fixed cost is no longer recovered. At 200 units a month, break-even takes roughly twelve months to reach.

Now test the same product against the changes that commonly happen during development and launch.

Scenario Net price Variable cost Contribution Break-even units
Base case, direct sales GBP49.00 GBP22.00 GBP27.00 2,408
Unit cost rises by GBP3 GBP49.00 GBP25.00 GBP24.00 2,709
Price cut to GBP45 GBP45.00 GBP22.00 GBP23.00 2,827
Sold through a retailer keeping 40% GBP29.40 GBP22.00 GBP7.40 8,784

The last row is the one that catches teams out. Retailer margins commonly sit somewhere between 30 and 50 per cent of the retail price before VAT, so a product designed for direct sales can need more than three times the volume to break even once it moves into retail. That is a design and cost problem, not just a sales problem.

Practical rule: if small changes in cost or price move the break-even point dramatically, the business model probably needs more margin headroom.

Common break-even mistakes

Using RRP instead of net price. VAT and channel share are not yours to keep, so they cannot contribute to fixed cost.
Factory price as variable cost. Freight, duty, packaging, fulfilment, payment fees and returns all scale with volume and belong in the calculation.
Leaving tooling out. Tooling is often the largest single fixed cost in a moulded product. If it is excluded, say so and check it separately.
Ignoring time. Break-even volume means little without a realistic sales rate. Two thousand units in a year and two thousand units in four years are very different businesses.
Treating break-even as the goal. Breaking even only recovers cost. The business still needs margin to fund the next product, stock growth and overhead.

FAQ

How do you calculate break-even for a product?

Divide the fixed costs you need to recover by the contribution per unit, which is the net selling price minus the variable cost of each unit. Round up to the next whole unit. Multiply by the net price if you want the answer as revenue.

Should tooling be included in break-even?

Usually yes, because tooling is a fixed cost the product has to recover and is often one of the largest. Some teams calculate an operating break-even without it as well, but they should state clearly which version they are using.

What is a good contribution margin for a hardware product?

It depends on the category and channel, but a product needs enough headroom that modest changes in price or cost do not multiply the break-even volume. If a small cost increase or a move into retail makes break-even several times harder, the margin is probably too thin.

What is the difference between break-even and payback?

Break-even is a volume: the number of units needed to recover fixed cost. Payback is a time: how long it takes for profit to recover the investment. A product can have an achievable break-even volume and still a long payback if sales build slowly.

Does break-even analysis include VAT?

No. In the UK, VAT collected on sales is passed to HMRC rather than kept by the business, so break-even should use the price net of VAT and of any retailer or marketplace share.
