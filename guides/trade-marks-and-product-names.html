Trade marks and product names: the basics

What a trade mark protects and what it does not, why a product name should be checked before it is printed on a box or moulded into a part, how to make basic checks yourself, what registration involves in general terms, and what to do if someone objects to your name.

Quick answer

A trade mark protects your brand, such as a product name or logo, not the product's design or how it works. Check a name is free to use before you commit to packaging, tooling or domains.

Basic checks are quick: search the official trade mark register for the countries you will sell in, then look at marketplaces, domain names, social media handles and a general web search. Registration is made for particular goods and services and only covers the countries or regions where you register. Use ™ to show you treat a name as a trade mark, and ® only once it is registered. This guide is general information, not legal advice: for anything close or contested, talk to a trade mark attorney or other IP professional and read the national IP office's guidance.

What a trade mark protects

A trade mark is a sign that tells customers whose product they are buying. For a physical product that usually means the product or brand name and the logo. Its job is to stop others using a confusingly similar sign for similar goods, so customers are not misled about who made what they buy.

A trade mark does not protect the product itself. It says nothing about the shape, the mechanism or the way the product works. Those are the territory of other rights:

  • Patents protect how a new invention works: a mechanism, a process or a technical solution.
  • Registered designs protect appearance: shape, lines, contours and decoration.
  • Trade marks protect the brand: the name and signs customers use to recognise you.

Many products rely on a combination. Design registration vs patents compares the first two, and the IP, partners and compliance hub sets out the full range, including copyright and confidential information. The Intellectual Property Office's overview of intellectual property is a good official starting point.

Why check a name early

A name spreads quickly. Within weeks of choosing it, a team has often bought a domain, set up social accounts, briefed a packaging designer and, sometimes, added the logo to a moulded part. All of that depends on the name being free to use.

Changing a name on a website is a nuisance. Changing it after packaging is printed, a crowdfunding campaign has launched or a logo has been cut into a mould tool is expensive and slow. As the freedom to operate guide puts it for patents: check before you commit, while changing course is still cheap.

Practical rule: shortlist several names, check them all, and only commit packaging, tooling and launch materials to a name that has passed the basic checks and, where the investment justifies it, a professional search.

Basic checks you can make yourself

Informal checks will not give certainty, but they quickly rule out names that are plainly taken and show which names deserve a closer look.

  1. The official trade mark register. Search the national IP office's register for each country you plan to sell in. In the UK, the Intellectual Property Office provides a trade mark search. Look for similar names as well as identical ones, including different spellings and names that sound alike, and note what goods and services each mark covers.
  2. Marketplaces. Search the online marketplaces where you expect to sell. A name in active use on similar products is a warning even if you cannot find a registration.
  3. Domain names. See whether the obvious domains are taken and, if so, what they are used for.
  4. Social media handles. Check the platforms you expect to use, and whether existing accounts sell anything like your product.
  5. A general web search. Search the name alone and with words describing your product. Unregistered businesses can still have rights from using a name, so use matters, not just registrations.

Record what you searched, where and when. If anything looks close, in either the name or the goods, take advice before going further.

Registering a trade mark, in general terms

You can use a name without registering it, but registration generally makes your rights much clearer and easier to enforce. GOV.UK explains how to register a trade mark in the UK. Two general ideas matter most when planning:

  • Classes of goods and services. A registration covers particular goods and services, grouped into classes. The same word can be registered by different owners for unrelated goods. Choose the goods and services you actually sell, and plan to sell.
  • Territories. Protection is national or regional. A registration in one country does not protect you in another. Think about where you will sell, including online and through distributors abroad, and take advice on which routes cover those markets.

Not every name can be registered. Names that simply describe the product or its qualities are generally hard to protect, because other traders need to be free to use ordinary descriptive words. Distinctive or invented names tend to be stronger.

™ and ®

™ can be used next to a name you treat as a trade mark, whether or not it is registered. It signals that you regard the name as your brand. ® indicates a registered trade mark and should only be used once the mark is registered, and only in the countries where it is registered. Using ® for an unregistered mark can be misleading and, in some countries, an offence. If you sell in several markets, check each one.

Using the mark consistently

A trade mark is strongest when it is used consistently as a brand. Use the same spelling and form across packaging, the product, the website and listings. Use the name as a brand alongside a description of the product, such as "the [Name] herb keeper", rather than as the word for the product itself, so it does not drift into a general term. If the logo changes significantly, check your registration still covers it.

Domain names and marketplace brand registries

Owning a domain or registering a company name does not, on its own, give you trade mark rights, or stop you infringing someone else's mark. It only shows nobody else has registered that exact name in that registry.

Some large marketplaces run brand registry programmes that help brand owners manage listings and report copies. They generally require a trade mark, registered or applied for, in the relevant country. If you plan to sell on marketplaces, check each one's requirements early, because they can affect where and when you register.

If someone objects to your name

If you receive a letter saying your name infringes someone else's rights, or your application is opposed, take it seriously but do not reply in haste. Note any deadline, keep the correspondence, and get advice from a trade mark attorney or solicitor before responding. Whether there is a real problem depends on details such as how similar the names and goods are, who used the name first, and where. Outcomes range from no action, to an agreement on how each party uses its name, to a change of name, which is far cheaper before launch than after.

Worked example: naming a kitchen gadget

Illustrative and qualitative. A founder has developed a small kitchen gadget for keeping fresh herbs. Prototypes are working, a packaging designer is about to start, and the plan includes the name moulded into the lid.

The founder's favourite name plainly describes what the product does. A quick search shows several sellers using similar words, and a descriptive name would be hard to protect, so it is rejected.

The founder shortlists three invented names and checks each: the official registers for the UK and the other first-year markets, the main marketplaces, domains, social handles and a web search. One is registered by another company for kitchenware, so it is dropped. A second is free on the register but used by an active online shop selling garden products, which feels too close. The third shows no conflicts the founder can find, and a suitable domain is available.

Before briefing the packaging designer and toolmaker, the founder asks a trade mark attorney to advise on the third name, applies to register it for the relevant goods in the planned markets, and uses ™ on samples. The logo stays off the mould until the position is clear, so an unexpected objection would cost new artwork, not a tooling change.

Common mistakes

Moulding a logo into a tool before checking the name. A name cut into steel is expensive to change. Check and, where justified, register the name before it reaches tooling, and consider a printed or applied logo until you are confident.
Assuming a company name or domain is a trade mark. Registering a company or buying a domain does not give trade mark rights or prove the name is free to use for your goods.
Choosing a descriptive name. Names that simply describe the product are generally hard to protect and hard to stand out with. Distinctive names are usually stronger.
Ignoring other countries you will sell into. Protection is national or regional. A name that is clear at home may already belong to someone else in a market you plan to enter through a distributor or a marketplace.

Decision rules

  • Treat the name as a decision with a deadline: settle it before packaging, tooling, domains and launch materials.
  • Check several names, not one favourite, on the official register, marketplaces, domains, social handles and the web.
  • Prefer distinctive names over descriptive ones.
  • Register for the goods you sell, in the places you sell, and take professional advice on scope and routes.
  • Use ™ until registered, ® only after, and get advice before answering any objection.

FAQ

What does a trade mark protect?

A trade mark protects signs that identify your brand, such as a product name or logo, against confusingly similar use on similar goods. It does not protect the product's design or how it works; registered designs and patents cover those.

When should I check my product name?

Before you commit money to it: before packaging is printed, a logo is moulded into a tool, domains and accounts are built up, or the product is announced. Changing a name gets more expensive with every step.

Does owning the domain or company name mean I own the trade mark?

No. A domain or company registration only shows nobody else has registered that exact name in that registry. It does not give trade mark rights or prove the name is free to use for your goods.

Can I use the ® symbol before my trade mark is registered?

No. Use ™ for a name you treat as your brand. Use ® only once the mark is registered, and only in the countries where it is registered; misusing it can be misleading and, in some places, an offence.

Does a UK trade mark protect me abroad?

No. Trade mark protection is national or regional, so you need protection in each country or region where you sell. Take advice from an IP professional on which routes cover your markets.

What should I do if someone says my name infringes their trade mark?

Note any deadline, keep the correspondence and get advice from a trade mark attorney or solicitor before replying. Whether there is a real conflict depends on the details, and outcomes range from no action to an agreement or a change of name.
