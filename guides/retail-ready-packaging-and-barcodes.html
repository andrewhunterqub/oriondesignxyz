Retail-ready packaging and barcodes: the basics

What changes when a product moves from your website onto a shop shelf: what retailers commonly ask for, barcodes, outer cases, on-pack information, display, sustainability, and how print, tooling and minimum orders affect cash and the schedule.

Quick answer

Retail packaging must sell the product on a shelf and fit the retailer's systems: a barcode for each variant, a clear outer case specification, accurate dimensions and weights, and the right information and marks.

Ask your first retailer or distributor for their supplier requirements early, because they shape the box, carton and schedule. Retailers commonly require a GS1 barcode, carrying a number called a GTIN, on every product; a business obtains these through GS1 membership in its country, and each variant needs its own. Design the pack alongside the product, sample and test it, and keep the first print run modest until pilot feedback is in.

How retail packaging differs from shipping-only packaging

Packaging for parcels sent direct to customers is mostly about protection and unboxing; packaging design for shipping covers that journey. Retail packaging must still protect, but takes on extra jobs:

  • Sell without you there. On a shelf or a hook, the box is the only salesperson: a shopper should grasp what the product is and does within seconds.
  • Work in someone else's systems. Retailers receive, count and sell stock using barcodes, case labels and data you supply. Packaging that does not fit causes delays or refused deliveries.
  • Survive handling in store. Boxes are handled, opened by curious shoppers and restocked, so seals, windows and hang tabs need to cope.
  • Travel in cases and on pallets. Retail stock usually moves in outer cases on pallets, so case and pallet fit matter as much as the box.

Some products need a retail box for shops and a different pack for online orders; decide early whether one design can do both.

What retailers and distributors commonly ask for

Each retailer and distributor sets its own requirements, usually in a supplier manual or new-product form; ask for it as soon as talks become serious. In general terms, expect questions about:

Item What it usually means
Barcodes A barcode on each product, and often on the outer case, with numbers registered to your business
Outer case specification The carton the units ship in: material, size, strength and how it is closed
Case quantity How many units in each case, and in each inner pack if used, often chosen to suit the retailer's ordering
Case labelling Description, product code, quantity, barcode and sometimes batch information on the carton
Dimensions and weights Measured size and weight of the unit pack, inner and outer case
Pallet arrangement Cases per layer, layers per pallet, and any height or weight limits
Drop or transit testing Sometimes, evidence that the packed product survives handling
Product information Images, descriptions, materials and compliance documents for listings and their own checks

Plan any drop or transit tests in your test plan so the evidence is ready when asked. Retail and distributor margins explained covers the commercial terms that come alongside.

Barcodes: the essentials

Retailers commonly require a GS1 barcode on each product. The number behind it is a GTIN (Global Trade Item Number), identifying one specific product to tills and stock systems. A business obtains GTINs through membership of GS1, the organisation that runs the system, in its own country; in the UK that is GS1 UK. Check the current membership terms with GS1 directly.

  • One number per variant. Each colour, size, kit or pack quantity a shopper could buy needs its own GTIN, or nobody can tell variants apart in stock or sales.
  • Ask about cases. Retailers may also want an identifier on the outer case so a whole case can be scanned.
  • Keep a register. Record which number belongs to which variant and pack, and never reuse one for a different product.
  • Print and check. Place it on a flat, clear area, follow your printer's and GS1's guidance on size and clear space, and scan printed samples before approving the run.

Set barcodes up before artwork is finalised: the numbers have to be in the print files.

Information on the pack

At a general level, the pack and the product together need to tell a shopper and a retailer:

  • Product name and what it is, clear enough to understand at a glance from the shelf.
  • What is in the box: product, accessories, batteries and instructions.
  • Who you are and how to reach you: your business name and contact details.
  • Required marks and information. Product certification basics describes these in general terms: conformity marking where it applies, the manufacturer's (and importer's) name and address, product identification for traceability, and instructions and safety information in English. Check that guide and its official links before artwork is signed off.
  • A batch or date code, so a problem can be traced to a production run later.

Labelling is part of compliance, not decoration: ask whoever maintains your technical file to check the pack copy.

Shelf presence: hanging or standing

How the product will be displayed shapes the box from the start. Ask the retailer which they expect:

  • Hanging display, on pegs or hooks. A strong hang tab, a pack that hangs straight, and a front that works at eye level. Common for small, light products.
  • Standing display, on shelves. A stable base, a front that reads side by side with others, and information on more than one face.
  • Shelf-ready cases. Some retailers prefer outer cases that open into a display tray. Agree this before the case is specified.

The box should say what the product does from a distance, then give detail close up. Windows show the product but add material; a good image on card often does the same job.

Sustainability

Retail packs tend to use more print and more mixed materials than a plain shipping box. The principles in packaging design for shipping apply here too: right-size the pack, use materials that are widely recycled, avoid combining materials that cannot be separated, and mark materials and disposal guidance clearly. That guide also points to the official guidance on packaging producer responsibilities.

Card or moulded pulp inserts in place of plastic trays are a common simplification. Packaging is part of the product's footprint, so read sustainable product design and the sustainability and product life hub, and print only environmental claims you can evidence.

Print, tooling, minimum orders and cash

Retail packaging has its own costs and minimum orders, separate from the product:

  • Print set-up. Printed boxes carry set-up costs and get cheaper per unit in larger runs.
  • Tooling. Cutting dies (cutters) for the box shape, and tools for moulded inserts or trays, are one-off costs that take time to make. Changing the box shape later usually means a new cutter.
  • Minimum order quantities. Packaging suppliers set their own minimums, which may not match your product order, and boxes bought ahead of sales tie up cash like any stock; see minimum order quantities and cash.
  • Unit cost and freight. Packaging adds to unit cost, and its size and weight change freight, so include both in your landed cost.

Working with packaging suppliers

Treat packaging as a component with its own drawing, specification and supplier. Brief it with the product's dimensions, display type, case quantity, the retailer's requirements, materials, print, quantities and testing. Ask several suppliers to quote on the same basis, as in comparing manufacturing quotes, and check which costs are one-off and which are per unit.

Sample in stages:

  1. Plain samples to check size, fit, inserts and closure with real products.
  2. Printed proofs to check colours, text, marks and that the barcode scans.
  3. Pre-production samples made by the production process and packed with production-intent products, for fit, transit testing and retailer approval where they ask for it.

Fitting packaging into the schedule

Packaging tends to be planned last and needed first: retailers may want samples, dimensions and barcodes before ordering, and boxes must reach the factory before packing. Work backwards from the first delivery:

  • Retailer requirements and display type known before the box is designed.
  • Pack size fixed while the product can still change slightly, because a few millimetres can change how many units fit a case or pallet.
  • Barcodes allocated and compliance text agreed before artwork is finalised.
  • Sampling, testing and approval before the print order.
  • Lead times for cutters, inserts, printing and delivery to the factory.

Use the real packaging in your pilot batch; planning a pilot production run explains how a pilot tests packing alongside the product. Add these steps to your plan with the Timeline Planner.

Worked example: a small gadget's first retailer

Illustrative example only. A start-up sells a small rechargeable desk gadget through its own website, in a plain shipping box. A gift retailer agrees to stock it in two colours and sends its requirements.

Questions first. The retailer uses shelves, so a standing box; it wants a case quantity that suits its store orders, in a labelled outer case. It also asks for unit and case dimensions and weights, images, and confirmation the product meets the rules that apply.

Checklist the team works through:

  • GS1 membership arranged; one GTIN for each colour, a case identifier if the retailer wants one, and a register of which number belongs to which variant.
  • A standing retail box with a card insert: a front face showing the product, a "what's in the box" panel, contact details, the marks and information identified with the certification guide, a batch code area and recycling information.
  • An outer case sized for the agreed quantity, checked against the pallet before the size is fixed, with a label showing description, code, quantity and barcode.
  • Plain samples to check fit, printed proofs scanned with a barcode reader, and a drop test of a packed case added to the test plan.

Decisions. The team keeps its website box for online orders rather than forcing one pack to do both jobs. It orders a modest first print run to cover the pilot batch and the retailer's first order, accepting a higher unit cost, because feedback from store staff and shoppers may change the artwork. It does not share one barcode across both colours, which would have mixed their stock and sales from day one.

Common mistakes

One barcode for all variants. Variants become impossible to tell apart in stock and sales, and fixing it means relabelling stock.
Carton sizes that do not suit pallets or retailers. Cases that overhang or underfill a pallet waste freight and get damaged; quantities that do not suit the retailer cause rework.
Designing the box last. It delays launch, squeezes out sampling and testing, and can force compromises on the product.
Printing large runs before pilot feedback. Big runs are cheaper per box, but one error or product change can leave you with boxes you cannot use.

Decision rules

  • Ask for supplier requirements first, before the box is designed.
  • One GTIN per variant, recorded in a register and set up before artwork.
  • Fix pack and case sizes early, checked against the pallet and the retailer's needs.
  • Treat packaging as a component: brief, quotes, samples and approval.
  • Test the packed product the way it will travel.
  • Keep the first print run modest until pilot feedback is in.

FAQ

Do I need a barcode to sell through retailers?

Usually. Retailers commonly require a GS1 barcode on each product, carrying a GTIN registered to your business. Ask each retailer what they need, and set barcodes up before your artwork is finalised.

How do I get a GS1 barcode?

Through membership of GS1 in your country; in the UK that is GS1 UK. Membership gives your business numbers to allocate to its products. Check the current terms directly with GS1.

Does each colour or size need its own barcode?

Yes. Each variant a shopper can buy, including different colours, sizes, kits and pack quantities, needs its own GTIN so retailers can track stock and sales separately.

What is an outer case specification?

The details of the carton your products ship in: its size, material and strength, how many units it holds, how it is labelled and how cases sit on a pallet. Retailers and distributors commonly ask for it before the first order.

Can my retail box also be used for online orders?

Sometimes, but not always. Parcels are handled individually and dropped more often, so a retail box may need an extra outer or different protection. Decide early whether one pack can do both jobs, and test it.
