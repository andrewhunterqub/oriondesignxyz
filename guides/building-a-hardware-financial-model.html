Building a hardware financial model

A hardware financial model is not a forecast to impress anyone. It is a small machine that links what the product costs, what it sells for, how many you sell and when cash moves, so you can see which decision changes the outcome and how much money the plan really needs.

Topic Financial modelling
Audience Founders, spin-outs, small teams
Tools Cost, margin, break-even and cash tools
Use it for Price, route, volume and funding decisions

Quick answer

A hardware financial model links unit cost, net price, volume, fixed costs and payment timing to find profit, break-even and the cash low point.

Contribution per unit = net price (after VAT and channel share) - landed unit cost

Build it in blocks: landed unit cost, net price by route to market, monthly volume, fixed costs and upfront spend (development, tooling, certification), then the timing of stock and customer payments. Contribution and fixed costs give break-even; the monthly cash flow gives the low point, which sets the funding need. Run conservative, expected and optimistic scenarios, because a single set of numbers hides the assumption that matters most.

Most early teams already have pieces of a model: a costed bill of materials, a target price, a development budget. The pieces are rarely connected. Change the route to market and margin, break-even, stock and funding need all change. A model is useful when one change flows through to every answer.

What the model is for

Build the model around the decisions it supports, not around a template:

  • Price: is there enough margin left after VAT, channel share and selling costs?
  • Route to market: each route has a different net price and payment delay.
  • Volume: how many units cover fixed costs, and is that realistic?
  • Funding need: how much cash the business must have access to before sales carry it.
  • Timing: when to commit to tooling, how large the first order should be, and when to raise money.

If a line in the spreadsheet does not affect one of these decisions, it can wait. Five or six well-argued inputs beat fifty tabs nobody can explain.

The building blocks and how they connect

Unit economics: cost of goods and landed cost

Start with what one sellable unit costs you once it is in your warehouse: parts, assembly, packaging, inspection, scrap allowance, freight, duty and insurance. That landed cost is the cost of goods sold (COGS) the model should use, not the factory price. See landed cost: freight, duty and VAT.

Price, VAT and channel margins

Revenue is the net price the business receives, not the shelf price. Remove VAT (UK consumer prices normally include it, at a standard rate of 20%), then the retailer's margin or marketplace fees and your selling costs. Each route earns a different amount per unit, which is why revenue and margin planning works backwards from the price to a target COGS for each route.

Margin = (price - cost) / price. Markup = (price - cost) / cost.

Volume and demand

Build volume upwards from channels and conversion you can defend, month by month, rather than as a share of a large market. A launch month, a growth rate and a ceiling set by stock or sales capacity are usually enough for a first pass. The hardware business case guide explains reachable market and conversion.

Fixed costs, development spend and tooling

Separate monthly fixed costs (people, rent, software, insurance, marketing) from one-off upfront spend (design, prototypes, tooling, certification). Fixed costs set break-even; upfront spend sets how deep the cash hole is before the first sale. Tooling appears twice: as cash, paid (often in stages) before production, and as a cost per unit when amortised. Keep both views, and never let the amortised figure hide the cash payment.

Tooling per unit = tooling cost / units the tool is expected to make over the period you choose

Working capital: stock, MOQs and payment terms

Factories commonly ask for a deposit at order and the balance before shipment, minimum order quantities force larger batches, and retailers may pay 30 to 90 days after delivery. That is working capital: cash tied up between paying for stock and being paid for it. Minimum order quantities and cash shows how a cheaper unit price can cost more cash.

Monthly cash flow, the low point and break-even

Break-even is the volume at which contribution covers fixed costs. It does not say whether you survive until then. For that, lay out cash in and out month by month and carry the balance forward. The lowest closing balance is the cash low point; the business needs enough cash to keep it above zero with a buffer. Financial break-even for hardware products and calculating hardware start-up cash flow cover each method in depth.

Worked example: one product, one route

Illustrative figures only, the same ones used in the cash flow guide and the break-even guide, so you can follow them across the site. A product sells direct for a net price of £49 (about £58.80 to a UK consumer once 20% VAT is added). Landed COGS is £22. Fixed overhead is £9,500 a month. The business spends £18,000 upfront on development and tooling, starts with £85,000 in the bank, sells 120 units in month 1 and grows 12% a month. Customers' money arrives one month after the sale, and stock is paid for in the month it is sold.

Block Calculation Result
Contribution per unit £49 - £22 £27
Margin and markup £27 / £49 and £27 / £22 55% margin, 123% markup
Monthly break-even £9,500 / £27 352 units, first reached in month 11
Year 1 volume 120 units growing 12% a month About 2,900 units
Upfront spend per unit, if spread over year 1 £18,000 / 2,896 About £6.20
Cash low point Monthly cash flow, one-month payment delay About £10,800, in month 12

Read the rows together. A 55% margin looks healthy, yet sales only cover overhead in month 11, and because sales are paid a month late, cash falls until month 12. The business uses about £74,200 of its £85,000 before it turns, leaving a low point of roughly £10,800, a little over one month's overhead. If the team wanted three months' overhead (£28,500) in hand at the low point, it would need roughly £102,700 at the start, or a smaller first year of spending.

The upfront spend row shows both views: about £6.20 a unit in the profit view, trimming contribution to about £20.80, while the full £18,000 left the bank in month 1.

Scenarios and sensitivity

Change one input at a time and watch the low point. These rows rerun the same illustrative model with a single change each.

Change Monthly break-even Cash low point
Base case 352 units About £10,800 (month 12)
Landed cost up £3 to £25 396 units About £1,300 (month 13)
Net price cut to £45 413 units Just runs out in month 13
Growth 8% a month, not 12% 352 units Runs out in month 13, about £5,400 short by month 16
Customers pay two months late 352 units Runs out in month 10, about £9,600 short by month 14

No change is dramatic on its own, and break-even does not move at all for the last two. The cash low point is far more sensitive, which is why cash is modelled monthly. The row that moves the outcome most is where to gather evidence next: a firmer quote, a price test, early sales or better payment terms.

Decision rule: if the plan only survives when price, cost, growth and payment timing all go your way, it needs more margin, a smaller first commitment or more funding before tooling is ordered.

Common modelling mistakes

Mixing margin and markup. A 50% markup is only a 33% margin. Agree which one a supplier, retailer or spreadsheet means before comparing figures.
Ignoring VAT. VAT in a consumer price is not revenue; counting it overstates revenue by a sixth of the price. VAT on imports and sales also moves cash between periods, so check the timing with your accountant.
Ignoring payment timing. Annual totals and same-month payment hide the low point. Model monthly, with real deposit, balance and customer payment terms.
Single-point estimates. One forecast hides fragility. Show conservative, expected and optimistic cases, and note which input moves the result most.
Forgetting tooling and certification. Both are paid before the first sale, may need modifying or repeating, and often land in the same few months as the first stock order.

Practical next steps

  1. Write down the decisions the model has to support, and the date each one must be made.
  2. Get a landed unit cost, even as a range, from the Product Cost Estimator and Landed Cost Calculator.
  3. Work out the net price and target COGS for each route you are considering.
  4. Budget upfront spend with the Startup Cost Planner, then find break-even.
  5. Build the monthly cash flow, find the low point in each scenario, and add contingency to get the funding need.
  6. Match that need to the options in funding routes for hardware start-ups in the UK and Northern Ireland, allowing for how long each takes to arrive.

FAQ

What should a hardware financial model include?

Landed unit cost, net price by route to market, monthly volume, fixed costs, upfront spend such as development, tooling and certification, and the timing of stock and customer payments. Together they give contribution, break-even and the monthly cash low point.

Should I model monthly or annually?

Monthly, for at least the first eighteen months or until cash has clearly turned. The cash low point usually sits inside a single month, and annual totals hide it.

How do I treat tooling in the model?

Twice. In the cash flow, record the payments when they are made, often in stages before production. In the profit view, spread the cost over the units the tool is expected to make so the unit cost reflects it.

Do I include VAT in revenue?

No. Use the net price the business keeps after VAT and any channel share. VAT does affect cash timing, though, so check with your accountant how and when it will be paid and reclaimed.

How does the model tell me how much funding I need?

Find the lowest closing cash balance in a cautious scenario. The cash used to reach that point, plus a contingency buffer, is the minimum the business must have access to. Then match it to funding routes, allowing for how long each takes to arrive.
