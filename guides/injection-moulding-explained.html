Injection moulding explained: the process, tooling types, lead times and cost drivers

A plain-English explanation of injection moulding for people commissioning tooling for the first time: how the process works, the tooling choices you will be asked about, the steps from quote to production parts, and what really drives tool and part cost.

Quick answer

Injection moulding forces molten plastic into a machined metal tool, cools it, and ejects a finished part, repeating every few seconds to a minute.

Part cost ≈ material + (machine rate × cycle time ÷ cavities) + finishing and handling + scrap allowance + tooling ÷ volume

It gives low, consistent part costs and fine detail at volume, but needs an expensive tool that takes weeks to make and is costly to change. Tool cost is driven by part size, complexity, number of cavities, moving parts for undercuts, and steel grade; part cost by material, cycle time and machine size. It suits stable designs with enough volume to share the tooling cost.

How the process works

Plastic pellets are dried if needed, melted and mixed by a rotating screw in a heated barrel, then injected under high pressure into the cavity of a closed tool. The plastic cools against the tool's water-cooled walls, shrinks slightly, and is pushed out by ejector pins once the tool opens. The same tool can make hundreds of thousands of parts or more.

Types of tooling

Choice Options Trade-off
Tool material Aluminium; pre-hardened steel; hardened steel Aluminium is quicker and cheaper but wears sooner; hardened steel costs most and lasts longest, especially with abrasive filled plastics
Cavities Single cavity; multi-cavity; family tool (different parts in one tool) More cavities cut cost per part but raise tool cost; family tools save tooling but can be harder to balance
Runner system Cold runner; hot runner Hot runners waste less material and can shorten cycles but cost more to build and maintain
Moving parts Straight-pull; side actions; lifters; unscrewing mechanisms Every moving part adds cost, lead time and maintenance; design them out where possible
Inserts and modularity Master unit die (MUD) frames with interchangeable inserts Cheaper for small parts and early runs; less suitable for large or complex parts

Many start-ups begin with an aluminium or soft-steel "bridge" tool for the first few thousand parts, then move to a hardened multi-cavity tool once the design and demand are proven. The risk-progressive manufacturing strategy explains that sequence.

The tooling project, step by step

  1. Quote and DFM review: the toolmaker reviews your CAD for mouldability and proposes gate, parting line and ejector positions.
  2. Tool design: the tool is designed in detail; you approve the layout and any changes to the part.
  3. Tool manufacture: machining, spark erosion, polishing or texturing, and fitting.
  4. First samples (T1): the first parts off the tool, checked against your drawings.
  5. Modifications and further samples (T2, T3…): adjustments to dimensions, fit, finish or process.
  6. Approval and first-article inspection: you sign off production-representative parts; the process settings are recorded.
  7. Production: parts are moulded, inspected and packed to the agreed quality plan.

Lead times vary widely with the toolmaker, the country, the part's complexity and the workload of the shop. As a broad guide, simple aluminium tools are often quoted in a few weeks, and production steel tools commonly take from several weeks to a few months to first samples, before any modification rounds and shipping. Treat any lead time as a range in your plan, and ask each toolmaker for their own figures.

What drives tool cost

  • Part size: larger parts need larger blocks of steel and bigger machines.
  • Complexity: deep ribs, fine detail and tight tolerances take more machining and fitting.
  • Moving parts: each side action or lifter adds design and build work.
  • Number of cavities: more cavities, more cost, but lower cost per part.
  • Steel grade and expected life: hardened steel costs more but lasts longer.
  • Surface finish: high polish and specified textures add finishing time.
  • Runner system: hot runners add cost to the tool.
  • Where it is made: toolmaking rates differ considerably between countries; compare the full cost, including shipping, modifications and communication.

What drives part cost

Once the tool exists, the price of each part depends mainly on the plastic used (weight times price per kilogram, plus a small allowance for runners and scrap), the cycle time, the hourly rate of the moulding machine (larger parts need larger, more expensive machines), the number of cavities, and any secondary operations such as printing, inserts or assembly. Adding a soft grip or seal as a second material is covered in overmoulding and two-shot moulding explained.

Illustrative figures only. A small enclosure part weighs 40 g in ABS. With material at an assumed £3 per kilogram and a 5 per cent allowance for scrap and runners, material is about £0.13 per part. The tool has two cavities and a 30-second cycle on a machine charged at an assumed £50 per hour: £50 ÷ 3,600 seconds × 30 seconds ÷ 2 cavities ≈ £0.21 per part. Before packing, overhead and the moulder's margin, the part costs about £0.33. Cutting the cycle to 24 seconds by thinning the walls saves about £0.04 a part, roughly £4,000 over 100,000 parts.

When injection moulding is, and is not, the right choice

Good fit Poor fit
Volumes high enough to share the tool cost A few dozen or a few hundred parts
A design that has stopped changing A design still changing after user testing or certification
Parts needing consistent quality, fine detail or snap fits Very large, simple parts better suited to vacuum forming or rotational moulding
Enough cash to pay for tooling before sales A cash position that cannot absorb the tool and first stock together

Common mistakes

Comparing tool quotes on price alone. Check tool material, expected life, cavities, ownership, included sample rounds and modification costs.
Assuming T1 parts will be right. Plan time and money for at least one modification round.
Ordering more cavities than the volume needs. A four-cavity tool for a few thousand parts a year ties up cash for little saving.
Not agreeing who owns the tool. Put ownership, storage, maintenance and transfer terms in writing before paying.
Choosing the material late. Shrinkage differs between plastics; the tool is cut for a specific material.

FAQ

How does injection moulding work?

Plastic pellets are melted in a heated barrel and injected under pressure into a closed metal tool. The plastic cools and solidifies against the tool walls, the tool opens and ejector pins push the part out. The cycle then repeats, typically every few seconds to a minute.

How long does it take to make an injection mould tool?

It varies with complexity, tool material, the toolmaker and the country. Simple aluminium tools are often quoted in a few weeks, while production steel tools commonly take from several weeks to a few months to first samples, plus time for modifications and shipping. Ask each toolmaker for their own lead time.

What is the difference between aluminium and steel moulds?

Aluminium tools are quicker and cheaper to make but wear sooner, so they suit prototypes and lower volumes. Steel tools cost more and take longer but last much longer, especially hardened steel, and suit high volumes and abrasive filled plastics.

What is a multi-cavity mould?

A tool that makes several identical parts in each cycle. It costs more to build than a single-cavity tool but reduces the cost per part, so it suits higher volumes. A family tool makes several different parts in one cycle.

What are T1 samples?

The first parts moulded from a new tool. They are checked against the drawings, and the tool is usually adjusted before later samples (T2, T3 and so on) are approved for production. Plan for at least one modification round.
