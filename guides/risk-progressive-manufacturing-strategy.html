The risk-progressive manufacturing strategy.

The best manufacturing route is rarely chosen in one jump. Most teams need a staged path that learns quickly first, validates repeatability second, and only then commits hard to scale.

Quick answer

A risk-progressive manufacturing strategy commits money in stages as evidence improves.

Prototype (buy learning) → Bridge (buy evidence) → Scale (buy repeatable economics)

The product moves from prototype to bridge to scale manufacturing only when demand, design stability and quality evidence justify the next commitment. It accepts a higher unit cost early in exchange for a much lower chance of expensive tooling changes, unsellable inventory or a launch built on unproven processes.

Too many hardware teams frame the decision as prototype route versus final production route. In practice there is usually a more sensible sequence: prototype, bridge, and scale. Each phase is buying a different kind of confidence, and each one makes the next commitment safer.

Useful framing: prototype manufacturing buys learning speed, bridge manufacturing buys evidence, and scaled manufacturing buys repeatable economics.

What each stage is for

StagePrimary goalTypical processesMain risk if skipped
PrototypeLearn fast and change often3D printing, CNC machining, hand assembly, development boardsTeams lock decisions before the design has earned it
BridgeTest demand, process capability, and quality repeatabilityVacuum casting, aluminium or simplified tooling, small-batch PCB assembly, manual assembly with simple fixturesScale investment arrives before the product proves itself
ScaleReduce recurring unit cost and increase throughputHardened steel and multi-cavity moulds, dedicated fixtures, automated test, contract manufacturingCapex gets committed against weak evidence or unstable geometry

What should trigger movement between stages

  • demand certainty improving, not just optimism increasing
  • product geometry stabilising enough that tooling changes are unlikely
  • quality requirements becoming clear and testable
  • the team being able to tolerate the lead time and capex of the next commitment

Stage gates: the evidence to collect

GateEvidence to collect before moving
Prototype to bridgeCritical functions verified on representative parts; positive user testing; a DFM review with likely suppliers; a costed BOM that supports the target price
Bridge to scaleOrders, pre-sales or channel commitments; no open changes to critical geometry; yield, defect and returns data from the bridge run; a supplier able to meet volume and quality; cash for tooling, inventory and lead time

The gates are not paperwork. They are the points where the team decides whether the next, larger cheque is justified, or whether one more round of cheaper learning is the better use of money. Before scale, a pilot production run is a good way to collect the yield and defect evidence the last gate asks for.

Illustrative example: a desk lamp startup

A product may appear ready for tooling because the cost curve is attractive, yet still benefit from a bridge run if the quality regime is immature or demand is not proven. That extra stage can prevent a much more expensive mistake.

Imagine a startup making an LED desk lamp with a moulded arm, a machined base and a small control PCB. In the prototype stage, the team builds a handful of lamps from printed and machined parts to test the hinge, heat and light quality. For the bridge stage, it orders aluminium tooling for the arm and a small PCB batch, and sells a few hundred lamps to early customers.

Returns data shows the arm hinge loosening with repeated use. The fix is a modest change to the aluminium tool, made in days rather than weeks. The bridge run also shows that assembly takes longer than planned, which feeds into fixture design. Only then does the team order hardened multi-cavity tooling for scale. Had it gone straight to steel, the same hinge fix would have meant modifying a far more expensive tool after inventory had already been built. The scenario is illustrative, but the pattern is common.

Moving too early versus moving too late

Too early: capex is locked against unstable geometry, tool changes erase the planned savings, and inventory may be built for a version customers do not want.
Too late: high bridge unit costs eat margin, supply struggles to keep up with demand, and quality can vary between small batches.
The balance: move when the evidence for the next stage is stronger than the cost of being wrong, not when the spreadsheet first says the unit cost is lower.

Bridge manufacturing is not a delay. It is the cheapest place to find the problems that would otherwise be found in steel.

Common mistakes

  • treating bridge production as a failure to commit rather than a deliberate evidence stage
  • using bridge runs without measuring anything: yields, defects, assembly time and returns should all be recorded
  • ordering scale tooling on forecast optimism rather than orders or channel commitments
  • forgetting that tooling lead time, first samples and corrections often add weeks to months before saleable stock exists
  • planning the scale stage around one supplier without understanding their capacity and quality systems

FAQ

What is bridge manufacturing?

Bridge manufacturing is low-to-mid volume production that sits between prototyping and full-scale manufacturing. It typically uses processes such as vacuum casting or aluminium tooling to make real, saleable units in modest quantities. Its purpose is to test demand, quality and assembly before committing to expensive scale tooling.

How long does injection mould tooling take?

Tool manufacture commonly takes from several weeks for simple tools to a few months for large or complex ones, depending on the supplier’s workload. First samples then usually need checking and adjustment before production approval. Plan for that whole sequence, not just the quoted tool build time.

Is an aluminium mould good enough for production?

For lower volumes it often is. Aluminium tools are typically quicker and cheaper to make and modify than hardened steel, but they wear faster, particularly with abrasive or glass-filled materials. They suit pilot runs and modest annual volumes; high-volume products usually move to steel.

When should a startup move from bridge to mass production?

When there is real demand evidence such as orders or channel commitments, critical geometry has stopped changing, bridge-run data shows acceptable yield and quality, and the business can fund tooling, inventory and lead time. Lower unit cost on its own is not enough.

What is the biggest risk of scaling manufacturing too early?

Locking expensive tooling and inventory around a design or demand forecast that later proves wrong. Changes to hardened tools are slow and costly, and stock built to an earlier revision may be hard to sell. Staging the commitment keeps those mistakes small.
