CNC machining design rules: designing parts that are quick to machine

Practical rules for designing CNC-machined parts: what round cutting tools mean for corners and pockets, sensible walls, holes and threads, fewer set-ups, and tolerances and finishes that control cost. Figures are typical shop guidance to confirm with your machinist.

Quick answer

Design CNC parts around round cutting tools: give internal corners a radius, keep pockets and holes shallow relative to their width, avoid very thin walls, and only tighten tolerances where they matter.

CNC machining removes material with rotating tools, so every internal corner has a radius, deep narrow features need long tools that deflect, and each extra set-up or special tool adds cost. Use standard hole and thread sizes, minimise the number of sides that must be machined, and agree a general tolerance with a few tighter callouts on critical features. The figures below are typical shop guidance, not fixed limits; confirm them with your machinist.

When CNC machining suits a product

CNC is ideal for prototypes, low to medium volumes, precise metal parts and parts in engineering plastics. There is no tooling cost, so it is often used before or instead of moulding or casting. Unit cost stays relatively high as volumes grow; compare routes with the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser.

Key design rules

Feature Typical guidance Why
Internal corners Add a radius slightly larger than the cutter radius; larger is cheaper Round tools cannot cut sharp internal corners; a slightly larger radius lets the tool move smoothly
Pocket depth Commonly up to about 3 to 4 times the cutter diameter Longer tools deflect and chatter, slowing cutting and hurting accuracy
Wall thickness Avoid very thin walls; plastics need thicker walls than metals Thin walls vibrate and deform during cutting
Holes Standard drill sizes; depth commonly up to about 4 times the diameter for easy drilling Standard drills are faster and cheaper than milling a custom size
Threads Standard sizes; thread depth rarely needs to exceed about 1.5 to 2 times the diameter Extra depth adds little strength and raises the risk of tap breakage
Text and fine detail Engraved rather than raised, with a simple, bold font Raised text needs material removed around it
Practical rule: if a feature could only be made with a very small or very long tool, change the feature. Small, long tools are slow, fragile and expensive to run.

Reduce set-ups

Each time a part has to be turned over or re-clamped, there is set-up time and a chance of misalignment. Where possible, put features on as few sides as possible, align them with the main axes, and avoid undercuts that need special cutters. Parts that need features on many faces may need 5-axis machining, which costs more per hour.

Tolerances and finishes

  • Use a general tolerance, for example to a recognised standard such as ISO 2768, and call out tighter tolerances only on critical features. Tolerances and engineering drawings explains general tolerances, fits and datums.
  • Tight tolerances cost more: slower cutting, more inspection and sometimes extra operations. Check how tolerances combine with the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator.
  • Specify surface finish only where it matters. As-machined is cheapest; bead blasting, anodising and polishing add cost and can change dimensions. Surface finishes for plastic and metal parts compares the options.

Materials

Aluminium alloys are popular because they machine quickly and well. Steels and stainless steels are stronger but slower to cut. Engineering plastics such as acetal and nylon machine well but move with heat and moisture, so allow for that in tolerances. Material choice also affects finishes: anodising, for example, is mainly used on aluminium (and a few other metals such as titanium), not on steel. The Material Selector compares material properties.

Common mistakes

Sharp internal corners. They cannot be milled; the part comes back with radii or needs costly extra operations.
Tight tolerances everywhere. They raise cost without improving the product.
Designing a moulded part, then machining it. Thin ribs and deep pockets that suit moulding are slow to machine; design for the process you will use.
No drawing. A 3D model alone does not say which features are critical, which threads are needed or what finish is expected.

FAQ

Why do CNC parts need internal corner radii?

Milling cutters are round, so they leave a radius in every internal corner. Designing a radius slightly larger than the cutter lets the tool move smoothly and keeps cost down; truly sharp internal corners need extra processes.

How deep can a CNC pocket be?

As typical shop guidance, pockets up to about 3 to 4 times the cutter diameter are straightforward. Deeper pockets need longer tools that deflect, which slows machining and reduces accuracy. Check with your machinist.

What tolerance should I use for CNC parts?

Use a general tolerance, for example to ISO 2768, for most features and call out tighter tolerances only on critical fits. Tighter tolerances add machining and inspection cost.

Is CNC machining good for production?

It suits prototypes and low to medium volumes, precise metal parts and engineering plastics, as there is no tooling cost. At higher volumes, moulding, casting or sheet metal are often cheaper per part.

How can I reduce CNC machining cost?

Use generous corner radii, shallow pockets, standard hole and thread sizes, fewer set-ups, a general tolerance and as-machined finishes where possible, and choose an easily machined material.
