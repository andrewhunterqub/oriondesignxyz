UK vs overseas manufacturing: choosing where to make your product

How to choose between manufacturing close to home and further away: comparing landed cost rather than factory price, the effect on cash and lead times, quality and communication, Northern Ireland trade rules, and a staged approach many products follow.

Quick answer

Compare total landed cost, cash tied up, speed, control and risk, not just the factory unit price.

Overseas manufacturing often gives a lower unit price, especially at higher volumes and for labour-intensive assembly, but adds freight, duty, longer lead times, larger orders and more distance for quality and communication. Local manufacturing in the UK or Ireland often costs more per unit but can mean smaller batches, faster changes and less cash tied up. Many products use a mix: local for prototypes, early batches or critical parts, and overseas once the design and demand are stable.

The factors that matter

Factor Closer to home Further away
Unit price Often higher, especially for labour-intensive work Often lower, especially at volume
Freight and duty Lower, or none from UK suppliers Added to every shipment, with import VAT to pay up front (normally reclaimable if you are VAT-registered); see landed cost
Lead time Shorter, so less stock and faster reorders Longer, especially by sea
Minimum orders Often more flexible for small batches Often larger; see MOQs and cash
Communication and visits Same time zone, easy to visit Time zones, language and travel make problems slower to resolve
Design changes Quicker and cheaper to iterate Changes take longer and may involve stock in transit
Supply chain depth May need to source some components abroad anyway Some regions have dense clusters of component suppliers
Brand and sustainability Local manufacture can be a selling point; less transport Depends on the factory, materials and transport mode

Compare like with like

A quote from an overseas factory and a quote from a local one rarely include the same things. Bring both to a landed cost per unit at your realistic volumes, including tooling, freight, duty, inspection, packaging and the cost of holding stock. The Landed Cost Calculator does this, and the Cashflow Projection Tool shows the effect of larger orders and longer lead times on cash.

Illustrative figures only. An overseas quote is £5.00 a unit and a local quote £7.00. Adding freight, duty and inspection brings the overseas part to about £6.20 landed, with a 3,000-unit minimum and a long lead time, against a 500-unit minimum locally. At early volumes, the £0.80 saving may be outweighed by the cash tied up and the cost of changes; once sales are steady, the saving may justify moving.

Practical rule: decide on landed cost per unit plus cash and risk at your actual volumes. A lower factory price is not a saving if it ties up cash you need to sell the product.

Northern Ireland, Great Britain and the EU

For businesses in Northern Ireland, trading rules with Great Britain and the EU differ from the rest of the UK, which can affect where you manufacture, how goods move and which product markings apply. Check current official guidance on moving goods into, out of or through Northern Ireland and the product certification basics guide.

A staged approach

  1. Prototypes and pilot batches close to the design team, where changes are fast.
  2. Early production where batch sizes and cash are manageable, often local or nearby.
  3. Scale once the design and demand are stable, choosing on landed cost and risk.
  4. Review regularly: costs, freight, tariffs and your volumes change.

The Production Strategy Planner helps choose a route for each stage.

Common mistakes

Comparing factory prices only. Freight, duty, inspection and stock holding can close much of the gap.
Moving too early. Offshoring an unstable design multiplies the cost of every change.
No presence or inspection. Distance makes quality problems slower and costlier to find; plan inspection from the start.
One supplier, no backup. Consider what happens if a factory, route or tariff changes.

FAQ

Is it cheaper to manufacture overseas?

The factory unit price is often lower, especially at volume and for labour-intensive assembly. Once freight, duty, inspection, larger minimum orders and stock holding are included, the gap can be much smaller, and sometimes reverses at low volumes.

When does UK manufacturing make sense?

Often for prototypes, pilot batches and early production, when batches are small, the design is still changing and cash is limited, and for products where local manufacture, speed or close control matters.

Should I move production overseas later?

Many products start local and move once the design and demand are stable and volumes justify larger orders. Compare landed cost, cash and risk at your actual volumes before deciding.

What extra costs come with importing?

Freight, insurance, customs duty where it applies, import VAT (normally reclaimable if you are VAT-registered, but paid before you sell), customs brokerage, inspection and the cost of holding more stock for longer lead times. Check current official guidance for your goods.

Does Northern Ireland have different rules?

Yes, trading arrangements for goods moving between Northern Ireland, Great Britain and the EU differ from the rest of the UK. Check current official guidance before choosing where to make and ship from.
