Die casting explained: the process, alloys, design rules and when it pays

A plain-English explanation of die casting for people considering it for the first time: how the process works, the alloys you will be offered, the design rules that make a part castable, what happens to a casting afterwards, and how to tell whether a die will pay for itself.

Quick answer

Die casting forces molten metal under pressure into a hardened steel die, giving detailed, near-finished metal parts at a low cost per part once the die is paid for.

Part cost ≈ metal + (machine rate × cycle time ÷ cavities) + trimming + machining + finishing + scrap allowance + tooling ÷ volume

Zinc is usually cast in hot-chamber machines and aluminium in cold-chamber machines. Design the part much like a moulding: even walls, draft, radii, ribs instead of thick sections, few undercuts. Castings contain some porosity, so sealing faces and threads are usually machined afterwards. The die is expensive and slow to change, so die casting suits stable designs at volumes that can share its cost.

How die casting works

A die-casting die is a pair of hardened steel blocks with the shape of the part cut into them, much like an injection mould but built to survive molten metal. The die is clamped shut, molten metal is driven into the cavity at high speed and pressure, and once it solidifies the die opens and ejector pins push the casting out, with its runner, overflows and flash still attached for trimming.

Fast, pressurised filling gives fine detail, thin walls and short cycles. It also traps some gas and leaves shrinkage porosity, which shapes several design rules below.

Hot-chamber and cold-chamber machines

There are two main kinds of die-casting machine. The alloy usually decides which is used, but knowing the difference helps you follow a quote.

Machine How the metal gets to the die Usually used for
Hot-chamber The injection mechanism sits in the molten metal; a plunger pushes each shot straight into the die, keeping cycles short Zinc and other low-melting alloys, some magnesium
Cold-chamber Metal is ladled from a separate furnace into a shot sleeve, then a plunger drives it into the die Aluminium, which would attack parts left sitting in it, and much magnesium

Some die casters specialise in one alloy family, so ask early whether a supplier casts the alloy you need.

Common die-casting alloys

Few alloys suit die casting, and they differ from the wrought alloys used for machined and sheet parts: they are formulated to flow and fill the die, so their properties and finishes differ too. Choosing metals for a product covers the wider choice.

Alloy family Strengths Watch out for Typical uses
Aluminium die-casting alloys Light, stiff for the weight, conduct heat well, reasonable corrosion resistance Contain silicon to help them flow, which also tends to make them anodise poorly; dies wear faster than for zinc Housings, enclosures, heat-sinking covers, brackets, frames
Zinc die-casting alloys Fine detail, thin walls, long die life, good base for plating Heavy, nearly as dense as steel; weaker than steel; creeps under load when warm Handles, knobs, buckles, lock parts, small housings and decorative trim
Magnesium die-casting alloys The lightest of the three for a given size Needs specialist handling and corrosion protection; fewer suppliers Housings and frames for portable equipment where every gram counts
Practical rule: do not put a wrought alloy such as 6082 on a die-casting drawing. Ask the die caster which casting alloy they recommend and check your design against its datasheet.

When die casting makes sense

Die casting is a tooled process: expensive to start, cheap to repeat. It makes sense when the design is stable, volumes can share the die cost, and the part needs metal. Manufacturing processes compared sets it alongside the alternatives in relative terms.

Alternative Choose the alternative when Die casting tends to win when
CNC machining Volumes are low, the design is still changing, or the look depends on anodised wrought aluminium Volumes grow and the shape has pockets, ribs and bosses that are slow to machine
Sheet metal The part is a folded shell, panel or bracket of even thickness The part needs ribs, bosses or rounded 3D forms
Investment casting Volumes are lower, or the part must be steel or another alloy that cannot be die cast; its tooling generally costs less Volumes are higher and the part suits aluminium, zinc or magnesium, where faster cycles cut the cost per part
Plastic injection moulding The part does not need metal; plastic is lighter and usually cheaper per part The part must be stiff in thin sections, carry heat, screen electronics or feel solid

When does injection moulding become cheaper? explains why demand confidence and design stability matter as much as break-even volume; the same applies to a die.

Design rules for die-cast parts

Many rules echo the injection moulding DFM checklist. Treat this as typical guidance and confirm values with your die caster, who will set them by alloy, part size and process.

Feature Typical guidance Why
Wall thickness Keep walls uniform and change thickness gradually; zinc generally allows thinner walls than aluminium Thick sections freeze last and gather porosity and sink
Draft Draft faces parallel to the die opening, more on internal walls The casting shrinks onto cores and would drag or stick
Radii Round internal and external corners; avoid sharp edges Better flow, lower stress, and sharp die corners are prone to cracking
Ribs Stiffen with ribs rather than thicker walls; keep ribs thinner than the wall they join Stiffness without a thick, porous section
Bosses Core bosses hollow and tie them to walls with ribs A solid boss is a thick, porous spot; a cored hole can be tapped afterwards
Undercuts Avoid them, for example by moving a hole to a face in line with the die opening; otherwise the die needs a slide Each slide adds cost, maintenance and flash to trim
Parting line Agree it early; keep it simple and away from cosmetic and sealing faces Flash forms there and trimming leaves a witness line
Ejector pins Leave flat areas for pins on hidden faces, spread across the part Pins leave marks, and uneven pushing can distort the casting

Porosity, and why some features are machined afterwards. Every die casting contains some porosity, from trapped gas and from shrinkage where thick sections freeze last; the skin that freezes against the die is usually the soundest metal. As-cast surfaces carry draft and parting lines, so they are rarely accurate enough for a gasket face, bore or thread. Those features are machined, which cuts through the skin and can open pores: a leak path on a sealing face, or weak engagement in a thread.

Practical rule: keep machined features in even sections, not thick lumps, with a small machining allowance. Ask the die caster where porosity is likely and, for parts that must seal, about leak testing and impregnation.

Trimming, machining and finishing

  • Trimming: runner, overflows and flash are removed, at volume in a press with its own trim tool, a separate cost to check for in the quote.
  • Machining critical features: sealing faces, bores and threads need fixtures to locate the casting. Mark which features are as-cast and which machined, keep tight tolerances to machined ones, and pick datums found on the raw casting (see tolerances and engineering drawings).
  • Finishing: powder coating, paint and plating are common; zinc takes decorative plating well. Surface finishes for plastic and metal parts covers each finish and masking.

Anodising is commonly reported to suit die-cast aluminium poorly compared with wrought alloys: the silicon tends to give a darker, uneven finish, and surface porosity can show through. If the look depends on anodising, test it on sample castings, or plan for powder coat or paint.

Dies, die life and trials

A die is hardened tool steel that is heated sharply by every shot and cooled again. Over time this causes fine surface cracks, called heat checking, which show as raised lines on castings until the die is repaired.

Die life varies with the alloy, part, steel and maintenance. Zinc casts at a much lower temperature, so zinc dies generally last much longer than aluminium dies. Ask each die caster for the life they expect, what maintenance is included and who pays for repairs, and put that and die ownership in writing (see supply agreements with a manufacturer).

Die cost follows part size, complexity, slides and cavities, plus the trim tool and machining fixtures.

New dies are proved in trials, like moulds. Many suppliers call the first shots T0 and the first customer samples T1, but labels vary, so agree what each means. Ask for samples machined as in production, a porosity check on critical areas (by sectioning or X-ray, for example), and a leak test if the part must seal. Ask for each die caster's lead time to first samples, and plan for at least one modification round. Mould tool trials and sample approval covers reading samples and signing them off, and most of it applies to dies.

Worked example: a housing moving from CNC to die casting

Illustrative example. A small company makes a handheld measuring instrument with a housing machined from 6082 aluminium and anodised. Sales are growing, the housing is the most expensive part on the bill of materials, and the design has been stable for a year. Would die casting cut the cost, and what must change?

Feature of the machined housing What changes for die casting
Straight pocket walls and corners sized for the cutter Draft on every wall; larger internal radii
Thick floor and walls left by the machining strategy Even walls; floor thinned and ribbed
Solid bosses, drilled and tapped Cored bosses tied to walls; threads tapped after casting
Side port for a cable connector An undercut that cannot move, so the team accepts one slide
Gasket groove and lid sealing face Still machined, with a small allowance; samples leak tested
6082 aluminium, anodised The die caster's recommended casting alloy, powder coated instead of anodised

The team gets quotes from their machining shop and two die casters for the same volumes, and enters them in the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser: machining in the CNC fields, and die casting in the tooled route, after switching that route from injection moulding to die casting. The tool cost is the die, trim tool, fixtures and redesign work, and the unit cost is the casting with trimming, machining and powder coat. They run conservative, expected and optimistic volumes.

If the crossover sits well inside the conservative case, the die is likely to pay back; if only the optimistic case reaches it, keep machining for now. This example gives no prices on purpose: get real quotes for your own part.

Common mistakes

Sending the machined part for a die-casting quote. A part designed for machining has no draft and too many thick sections. Redesign it for casting first, or ask for a DFM review alongside the quote.
Expecting cast surfaces to seal. Plan to machine sealing faces, keep them in sound metal, and leak test samples.
Keeping the wrought alloy and anodised finish by habit. Agree a casting alloy and a finish that suits it before the die is cut.
Tight tolerances on as-cast features. Hold tight tolerances only on machined features, and say which is which on the drawing.
Comparing the casting price with the machined price. Include the die, trim tool, fixtures, machining, finishing and redesign in the comparison, not just the raw casting.

FAQ

What is the difference between die casting and injection moulding?

Both force a liquid into a closed steel tool and eject the finished part. Die casting uses molten metal, so the die must survive far higher temperatures, the castings usually need trimming and some machining afterwards, and they contain some porosity that the design has to allow for.

Is die casting cheaper than CNC machining?

Per part, usually yes once volumes are high enough, but the die, trim tool and fixtures must be paid for first and the design must stop changing. Compare the total cost of each route at realistic volumes using real quotes.

Can die-cast aluminium be anodised?

It can be, but it is commonly reported to give a darker, less even finish than wrought aluminium, because of the silicon in casting alloys and porosity near the surface. Powder coat or paint are more usual; if anodising matters, test it on sample castings first.

Why do die castings need machining?

As-cast surfaces carry draft, parting lines and small variations, so they are rarely flat or accurate enough for sealing faces, bearing bores or threads. Those features are machined afterwards, and the casting is designed so the cuts land in sound metal.

How long does a die-casting die last?

It depends on the alloy, the part, the die steel and maintenance. Zinc dies generally last much longer than aluminium dies, because zinc casts at a lower temperature. Ask each die caster for the life they expect and how repairs are handled.
