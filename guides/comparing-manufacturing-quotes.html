Getting and comparing manufacturing quotes

How to ask manufacturers for quotes you can compare, what to check in each reply, and how to turn different quotes into a landed cost per unit and the cash needed before the first sale.

Topic Quotes and supplier choice
Audience Founders, engineers, small teams
Tools RFQ pack, landed cost, payoff
Use it for Choosing a supplier on the full picture

Quick answer

Send three to five suitable suppliers the same request, then compare their quotes on landed cost per unit, tooling and who owns it, and the cash you must pay before you sell anything.

Cash for the first commitment = tooling + (minimum order quantity × landed cost per unit)

A quote is only comparable when it states the quantity, delivery terms, what is excluded, lead times and payment terms. Add freight, duty, packaging and inspection to each unit price to reach a landed cost, then add tooling and the minimum order to see the cash involved. The lowest unit price is often not the lowest landed cost, and rarely the least cash.

Quotes rarely arrive in the same shape: one delivered, one priced at a port, one at the factory gate with packaging left out, each at a different quantity. Comparing headline unit prices is the most common way first-time teams choose the wrong supplier.

What to send

The quality of a quote depends on the quality of the request. Send every supplier the same pack, so differences in the replies come from the suppliers and not from what you told them:

  • 3D CAD and 2D drawings with materials, finishes and the tolerances that matter
  • quantities: the first order, expected annual volume, and the price breaks you want quoted (for example 500, 1,000 and 3,000)
  • quality and compliance requirements, including inspection and test reports
  • packaging and labelling, and the delivery terms you want the price on
  • when you need samples and first production
  • a list of the items you need back, so every reply covers the same ground

The RFQ Pack Builder turns this into a request for quotation document, and the documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer explains each item. Named price breaks matter most: they stop each supplier quoting at a quantity that suits them.

How many suppliers to ask

Three to five well-chosen suppliers is usually enough. Fewer gives little sense of what is normal; many more takes weeks to manage and rarely changes the answer. Shortlist before you ask: check they make parts of your size, material and quality, and that your volumes matter to them. How to find and brief a manufacturer covers where to look and what to check.

Include at least one supplier from each route you are seriously considering, because a nearby and an overseas supplier differ on cost, cash, lead time and how easily the design can change; UK vs overseas manufacturing sets out that trade-off.

Making quotes comparable

Put every quote into the same table before forming a view. These are the lines that most often differ:

Line What to check
Tooling cost and ownership Total cost, how it is paid, who owns the tool, whether you can move it, how many parts it is expected to make, and who pays for repairs
Unit price at stated quantities The quantity each price applies to; a price at 3,000 cannot be compared with one at 1,000
Minimum order quantity (MOQ) The smallest order accepted, per order or per colour or variant
Lead times Separately for tooling, first samples and production, plus shipping if not included
Payment terms Deposits, stage payments and when the balance is due: at order, at sample approval, before shipment or after delivery
Delivery terms The Incoterm the price is quoted on, and the named place
What is excluded Retail packaging, cartons, testing and inspection, freight, duty, and any set-up charge per order
Validity How long the price holds, and whether material price changes can be passed on

Delivery terms decide how much you still have to add. Under the Incoterms rules, an EXW (ex works) price ends at the factory, so you pay for everything after that; FOB (free on board) covers loading onto the ship; DDP (delivered duty paid) brings the goods to you with duty paid. Landed cost: freight, duty and VAT explains each cost that sits between those points.

From quote to landed cost and cash

Two numbers make quotes comparable. The first is what one unit costs once it is in your warehouse, ready to sell:

Landed cost per unit = quoted price + anything excluded (packaging, inspection) + freight and insurance + duty + clearance and UK delivery

The second is the cash you must find before the first unit is sold:

Cash for the first commitment = tooling + (MOQ × landed cost per unit)

Then check when that cash leaves: a balance due before shipment is paid weeks before the goods arrive. The Landed Cost Calculator does the per-unit sum, and minimum order quantities and cash explains why a lower unit price can cost more cash. To see how tooling changes the picture as volume grows, enter each quote in the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser.

Worked example: three quotes

Illustrative figures only, reused from other guides so you can follow them across the site. A team plans a first order of 1,000 units of a small moulded product and expects to sell about 150 a month. Unit prices and minimum orders for A and B come from the MOQ guide; the £16,000 and £6,500 tooling from the hub and mould break-even guide. Freight, insurance, clearance and delivery use the landed cost guide's per-unit allowances (£0.70, £0.06, £0.12, £0.18), with duty assumed at 4% of the customs value (price plus freight and insurance) and inspection at £0.20 a unit. Replace these with a forwarder's quote; larger shipments usually cost less per unit. Import VAT is left out, as a VAT-registered business can normally reclaim it.

Supplier A (UK) Supplier B (overseas) Supplier C (overseas)
Quoted unit price £7.50 delivered £6.00 FOB £5.60 EXW
Minimum order 1,000 3,000 2,000
Tooling and ownership £16,000, yours, can be moved £6,500, stays the supplier's property £9,000, yours on final payment
Excluded from the price Nothing listed Freight, duty, inspection Retail packaging (£0.30), transport to port (£0.20), freight, duty, inspection
Duty at 4% None £0.27 £0.27
Landed cost per unit £7.50 £7.53 £7.63
Cash for the first commitment £23,500 £29,091 £24,269
Paid to the supplier before goods arrive £19,750 (tooling, 50% deposit) £24,500 (tooling, full order before shipment) £20,800 (tooling, full order before shipment)
Stock at 150 a month About 7 months 20 months About 13 months
Cost per unit over 3,000 units, tooling included £12.83 £9.70 £10.63

Read the rows together. Supplier C's £5.60 is the lowest price on paper, but once packaging, transport to the port, freight, duty and inspection are added it lands at £7.63, the highest of the three. Supplier B lands within 3p of the UK quote, so the real differences are tooling, cash and risk, not unit cost.

Supplier A needs the least cash and stock, and you own a tool you can move, but its £16,000 tooling makes it the dearest per unit over 3,000 units. Supplier B is cheapest over 3,000 units, yet needs the most cash, locks up 20 months of stock and keeps the tool. For an early, still-changing product, A for the first batches is a reasonable choice, while asking B about tool transfer and a price at 1,000. With firmer demand or cheaper funding, B could be the better call. The table does not decide; it makes the trade-off visible.

Red flags

A price far below the others. Usually a misread specification, a cheaper material, or extras that appear later.
No questions about the design. Suppliers who have looked closely ask about tolerances and finishes.
Tooling ownership left vague. If it is not in writing, assume you do not own the tool and cannot move it.
Full payment before any samples. Deposits are normal; paying everything before you have approved a part is a large risk.
Missing lines. No delivery term, validity date or lead time usually means the quote is a starting point, not a commitment.

Questions to ask before you choose

  1. What quantity does each price assume, and what is the price at our first order quantity?
  2. Who owns the tooling, can it be moved, how many parts is it expected to make, and who pays for repairs and changes?
  3. What are the lead times to tooling, first samples and production, and what usually delays them?
  4. What exactly is excluded: packaging, cartons, testing, inspection, freight, duty?
  5. When is each payment due, and is any of it linked to sample approval or inspection?
  6. How long is the price valid, and what would change it?
  7. Which design changes would lower cost or risk?

Keep the answers in writing with the quote. Quality control and first-article inspection covers how to agree what "acceptable" means before production.

Clarifying and negotiating respectfully

Most quote problems are misunderstandings, so clarify before you negotiate. Send every supplier the same questions and any specification change, and let them requote.

When you negotiate, solve the supplier's problem rather than just asking for less: offer a realistic forecast, a standard material they already run, or payments linked to sample approval. Ask what drives the minimum order and whether a smaller first batch is possible at a slightly higher price. Do not invent competing quotes or inflate volumes; the relationship has to survive the tenth order. Tell unsuccessful suppliers promptly, as you may need them later.

Once you have chosen, write down what was agreed, including tooling ownership, quality and payment, before you pay for tooling; supply agreements with a manufacturer lists the topics to cover.

Decision rule: choose on landed cost, cash needed and risk together. If two quotes are close on landed cost, prefer the one with the smaller cash commitment, clear tool ownership and shorter lead time.

Common mistakes

Comparing unit prices only. Delivery terms and exclusions can move a unit price by a quarter or more, as supplier C shows.
Ignoring tooling ownership. A cheap tool that stays with the supplier can tie your product to them for its whole life.
Mismatched quantities. A price at 3,000 units says nothing about the price at 1,000. Ask for the same price breaks from everyone.
Ignoring payment timing. Two quotes with the same total can need cash on very different dates. Model payments month by month.

FAQ

How many manufacturers should I ask for a quote?

Usually three to five suppliers you have already checked for fit. Fewer gives little sense of what is normal; many more takes time to manage and rarely changes the answer.

Why are manufacturing quotes so different from each other?

They usually assume different quantities, delivery terms, exclusions, tooling and payment terms, and sometimes a different reading of the specification. Put them on the same basis before comparing.

Should I always choose the lowest unit price?

No. Compare landed cost per unit, tooling and its ownership, the minimum order and the cash needed before the first sale. The lowest unit price is often not the lowest landed cost.

Who owns the injection mould tool?

Whoever the contract says. Many suppliers transfer ownership once the tool is paid for, but some keep it or tie it to an order volume. Get ownership, transfer rights and maintenance responsibility in writing.

How long is a manufacturing quote valid?

It varies by supplier and is usually stated on the quote. If it is missing, ask, and ask whether material or currency changes can alter the price before you order.
