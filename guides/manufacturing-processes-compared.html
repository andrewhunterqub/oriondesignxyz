Manufacturing processes compared: volumes, tooling, cost and constraints

A side-by-side reference of common manufacturing processes, from 3D printing to injection moulding, comparing volumes, tooling, unit cost, lead time and design constraints in relative terms, with how to choose and when to move between them.

Quick answer

Choose a process by volume, tooling budget, unit cost and the design features you need; low-volume processes avoid tooling but cost more per part.

At one-offs and small batches, 3D printing, CNC machining and vacuum casting avoid expensive tooling. As volumes grow, processes with tooling, such as injection moulding, die casting and pressed sheet metal, bring the unit cost down but need investment and a stable design. The table compares common processes in relative terms; real break points depend on the part, material and supplier, so use quotes to decide.

Processes at a glance

Relative, typical characteristics for planning, not quotes. Volumes and lead times vary widely by part size, complexity and supplier.

Vacuum casting is rarely the cheapest option in a chart like this; it is chosen for moulding-like appearance and materials in small batches, before a production mould is justified.

How to choose

  1. Volume over the product's life, not just the first order.
  2. Design stability: tooling locks in the design; keep flexible processes while it is still changing.
  3. Cash: tooling is paid up front; low-volume processes spread cost per part instead.
  4. Features and quality: surface finish, tolerances, materials and strength may rule processes in or out.
  5. Lead time: tooled processes take longer to the first part but are faster per part after.
Practical rule: compare total cost (tooling plus unit cost times volume) at your realistic volumes, and remember that many products move from one process to another as they scale.

Moving between processes

A common path is 3D printing for early prototypes, CNC or vacuum casting for pilot batches, then tooling once demand and design are stable. Designing early with the final process in mind avoids redesign later; see prototyping vs production. The Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser shows where tooling pays back, and the Production Strategy Planner suggests a route for each stage. For a product's housing specifically, choosing how to make an enclosure narrows these options to the routes that suit enclosures.

Common mistakes

Choosing on unit cost alone. Tooling, minimum orders and lead time can outweigh a lower part price.
Tooling too early. Design changes after tooling are slow and expensive.
Designing for the prototype process. A part that prints well may not mould or machine well.

FAQ

Which manufacturing process is cheapest?

It depends on volume. Processes without tooling, such as 3D printing and CNC machining, are usually cheapest for small quantities; tooled processes such as injection moulding become cheaper per part at higher volumes once tooling is paid for.

When should I switch from 3D printing to injection moulding?

When the design is stable and expected volumes make the tooling cost worthwhile. Compare total cost, tooling plus unit cost times volume, at realistic volumes, and use quotes rather than rules of thumb.

What is vacuum casting used for?

Making small batches of moulding-like parts from silicone moulds taken from a master pattern, often for pilot runs, testing and pre-production samples.

Is CNC machining suitable for production?

Yes, for low to medium volumes, precise metal parts and engineering plastics, as it needs no tooling. At higher volumes, tooled processes are usually cheaper per part.

Why do lead times differ so much between processes?

Tooled processes need moulds or dies to be designed, made and tested before the first part, which takes weeks to months. Untooled processes can start within days but take longer per part.
