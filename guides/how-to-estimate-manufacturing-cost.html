How to estimate manufacturing cost for a hardware product

Early cost work is rarely about finding the exact number. It is about building a cost structure that is accurate enough to guide the product architecture, route to market, and target price before expensive assumptions spread through the project.

Topic Bottom-up product costing
Audience Founders, engineers, and product leads
Tool Product Cost Estimator
Use it for Setting a first realistic COGS target

Quick answer

Manufacturing cost is more than materials and process.

COGS = materials + bought-in parts + process + labour + packaging + scrap + overhead

To estimate it, build the cost bottom-up from the bill of materials at the volume you expect to order, add assembly time, packaging, a scrap allowance and overhead, then spread tooling over the units it will make and add freight and duty to get a landed cost. If one of those terms is missing, the estimate is probably too low and the commercial model built on top of it will be fragile.

Many early hardware cost estimates look neat because they are incomplete. The team prices the obvious physical part, then mistakes that for a product cost. A working estimate needs to include the recurring cost of making the product repeatedly, not just the raw geometry.

Practical rule: cost the product at the level you expect to build and ship it, not the level you first imagine it in CAD.

The seven blocks that usually matter first

  1. Raw materials: resin, metal, fabric, or other base material directly consumed.
  2. Bought-in parts: electronics, fasteners, seals, inserts, packaging inserts, and other purchased components.
  3. Process cost: moulding, machining, printing, cutting, finishing, or external conversion work.
  4. Assembly labour: manual assembly, test steps, inspection, and packing effort.
  5. Packaging: retail packaging, shipping cartons, instructions, labels, and secondary packing.
  6. Scrap and rework: process losses, cosmetic rejects, yield loss, or damaged components.
  7. Overhead: factory burden, quality support, management time, and operating costs absorbed into production.

Two more lines: tooling and landed cost

The seven blocks give the recurring cost of building one unit. Two further lines decide what each unit really costs the business.

  • Tooling amortisation. Moulds, press tools, fixtures and test jigs are one-off costs, but they exist only to make the product. Dividing the tooling cost by the number of units you expect it to produce gives a per-unit figure to carry alongside COGS. Keep it as a separate line so you can see how it changes with volume.
  • Landed cost. A price quoted ex-works or free on board stops at the factory or the port. Freight, insurance, customs clearance, import duty and delivery to your warehouse all come on top. For a VAT-registered business, import VAT is usually reclaimable and so is normally left out of unit cost, but check this with your accountant. Landed cost: freight, duty and import VAT covers this in detail.
Unit cost to the business = COGS + (tooling ÷ units the tooling will make) + landed-cost additions per unit

Development, certification and design fees are also real costs, but they are not usually part of unit cost. Plan them separately as project costs; the guide to estimating hardware development costs covers them.

A step-by-step method

  1. Fix the volume and the version. State the order quantity and the configuration you are costing. Prices for 500 units and 10,000 units can be very different.
  2. Build a bill of materials. List every part, including fasteners, labels, adhesives and packaging, with quantity per product. The BOM Cost Builder gives a structure to start from.
  3. Cost each made part. Material is mass × price per kilogram, plus an allowance for runners, offcuts or swarf. Process cost is usually machine rate × cycle time ÷ parts per cycle, plus any finishing.
  4. Price bought-in parts at your volume. Use distributor price breaks or supplier quotes for the quantity you will actually order, and note any minimum order quantities.
  5. Estimate assembly, test and pack time. Multiply minutes per unit by a fully loaded labour rate. The Assembly Time Estimator helps break the build into steps.
  6. Add packaging, scrap and overhead. Apply scrap as a percentage of direct cost, then overhead as a loading, and state both assumptions.
  7. Add tooling and landed cost. Spread tooling over a realistic volume and add freight and duty per unit.
  8. Run a low and high case. A single number hides uncertainty. A range shows where to spend effort firming up quotes.

Typical allowances and how to treat them

The figures below are broad, hedged starting points for an early model, not benchmarks. Replace them with supplier figures as soon as you have them.

Line Early-model starting point What moves it
Scrap and rework Often a few per cent for a mature process; more for new processes, cosmetic parts or first runs Process maturity, cosmetic standard, tolerance demands, handling damage
Overhead A percentage loading, commonly in the tens of per cent, or already built into a supplier's rate Whether you build in-house or buy finished units; check quotes so overhead is not counted twice
Material waste A small allowance for runners and purging in moulding; more for machining from solid Process route, hot or cold runners, part nesting
Bought-in parts Distributor price at your order quantity Volume, lead time, minimum order quantities, currency
Overall estimate accuracy At concept stage, treat the total as uncertain by perhaps a third either way Narrows as the design freezes and real quotes replace assumptions

A simple worked example

Illustrative figures only. Imagine a small consumer device with £4.80 of material, £2.40 of process cost, £5.50 of bought-in parts, £4.80 of labour (12 minutes at an assumed fully loaded rate of £24 an hour), and £1.20 of packaging. Before scrap or overhead, the direct build cost is £18.70.

Add a 6% scrap allowance (£1.12) to reach £19.82, then a 14% overhead loading on that (£2.77), and the fully loaded COGS becomes £22.59. That is the figure the commercial model should be tested against, not the £12 to £15 number that a partial estimate of material, process and bought-in parts (£12.70) might suggest.

Now add the lines that sit outside COGS. An assumed £12,000 of tooling spread over a planned 10,000 units adds £1.20, giving £23.79. If the product is made overseas and freight, insurance, clearance and duty together add an assumed £1.60 per unit, the landed unit cost is £25.39, roughly double the partial estimate.

Line Per unit Running total
Material + process + bought-in parts £12.70 £12.70
Labour + packaging £6.00 £18.70
Scrap allowance (6%) £1.12 £19.82
Overhead (14%) £2.77 £22.59 (COGS)
Tooling (£12,000 ÷ 10,000 units) £1.20 £23.79
Freight, insurance, clearance and duty £1.60 £25.39 (landed)

Two quick sensitivity checks show where the effort should go:

  • Volume: if only 2,000 units sell, the same tool adds £6.00 a unit instead of £1.20, and the landed cost rises to £30.19.
  • Assembly time: cutting the build from 12 to 8 minutes saves £1.60 of labour, or about £1.93 once scrap and overhead are applied, which is around £19,300 over 10,000 units.

Common mistakes: where teams usually under-price the product

Bought-in parts are missing. The estimate focuses on the moulded or machined enclosure and forgets the electronics, fasteners, inserts, or accessories.
Assembly is treated as free. Even a short manual build time compounds quickly at scale, especially when reorientation, test, or inspection are required.
Scrap is set to zero. Real processes waste material, parts, or labour. Early models should acknowledge that instead of pretending perfect yield.
Packaging is added too late. Secondary packaging, instructions, labels, and pack-out often arrive after the selling price has already been discussed.
Tooling is left out or spread too thinly. Amortising a tool over a volume you have not yet sold makes the unit cost look better than the cash position will feel.
Prices from different volumes are mixed. Using a 10,000-unit component price with a 500-unit first order understates what the first batch will cost.
Ex-works prices are treated as landed. Freight, duty and clearance can add a noticeable share to the unit cost, especially for bulky or low-value products.
Currency is fixed. If parts or assembly are bought in another currency, test the estimate at a less favourable exchange rate.

Why this matters before pricing

Route-to-market decisions, gross-margin targets, and retail pricing all depend on a credible product cost. If the cost model is weak, the business case will only look healthy because the wrong number is holding it up.

The best sequence is usually:

  1. estimate the build cost bottom-up
  2. check break-even and cash-flow pressure
  3. work backwards from the route to market into target COGS

FAQ

What is usually missing from an early manufacturing cost estimate?

Bought-in parts, packaging, scrap, labour burden, and overhead are among the most common omissions.

Should retail price be set before product cost is understood?

No. Product cost should be understood well enough first that the margin model and route-to-market discussion are based on something credible.

How do you calculate the cost per unit of a product?

Add the cost of materials, bought-in parts, processing, assembly labour and packaging for one unit, add a scrap allowance and an overhead loading, then add tooling divided by the units it will make and any freight and duty per unit. Always state the order volume the figure applies to.

Should tooling cost be included in the unit cost?

Keep it as a separate line next to COGS. Divide the tooling cost by a realistic number of units the tool will make, and check the result at a lower volume too, because tooling is paid for up front whether or not the units sell.

How accurate does an early manufacturing cost estimate need to be?

Accurate enough to make decisions, not to quote from. At concept stage it is sensible to treat the total as uncertain by perhaps a third either way and to show a low and high case. The range should narrow as the design freezes and supplier quotes replace assumptions.
