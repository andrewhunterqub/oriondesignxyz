Working with a product design consultancy: what to expect and how to brief one

What a product design consultancy does, how it compares with a freelancer or an in-house hire, how to write a brief that gets useful proposals, what to ask before choosing, and how to agree intellectual property, files and staged work.

Quick answer

Use a product design consultancy for skills, capacity or experience you do not have, and brief it with the problem, the constraints and the decisions you need, not just the product you imagine.

A good brief covers who the product is for, what it must do (as measurable requirements), the target cost and volume, the timeline and budget range, what already exists, and what you want delivered at the end of each stage. Choose a partner on relevant experience, how they would tackle your riskiest questions, and clear terms on intellectual property ownership, and agree the work in stages with a decision point after each.

Consultancy, freelancer or in-house?

Option Strengths Watch out for Often suits
Product design consultancy Several disciplines, experience across many products, established processes and supplier contacts Often higher day rates than a freelancer; make sure you know who will actually do the work Multi-disciplinary products, first products, teams without in-house engineering
Independent designer or engineer Direct contact with the person doing the work, flexibility, often lower cost Limited capacity and range of skills; plan for illness and holidays Focused tasks, simpler products, specific skills such as CAD or DFM review
In-house hire Knowledge stays in the business; long-term continuity Recruitment time, salary commitment, one person rarely covers every skill Businesses with a pipeline of products to develop
Design-and-make (ODM) supplier Speed and lower development cost by adapting existing designs Less control and differentiation; check who owns the design Products close to something the supplier already makes

Many projects combine these: for example a consultancy for concept and engineering, a freelancer for later updates, and a contract manufacturer for production. Industrial design vs engineering design explains which skills each stage usually needs.

What a consultancy can do for you

  • Turn an idea into a clear brief and plan, including what must be learned first and what it is likely to involve.
  • Concept development: several directions, compared against your requirements.
  • Engineering and CAD: mechanical design, materials, tolerances and drawings ready for suppliers.
  • Prototyping and testing: planning rounds that answer specific questions, and managing builds.
  • Design for manufacture: preparing parts for the chosen processes and reviewing supplier feedback.
  • Manufacturing support: supplier selection, tooling reviews, first-article inspection and production issues.
  • Independent review: a second opinion on a design, cost estimate or manufacturing route before you commit.

How to brief a product design consultancy

You do not need drawings or a finished specification. You do need to be clear about the problem and the constraints. A useful brief covers:

Section What to include
The problem and the user Who it is for, the problem it solves, and the evidence you have that it matters
What it must do Essential and desirable requirements, measurable where possible (see opportunity and concept)
Commercial targets Target retail price, route to market, target unit cost and expected volumes
Constraints Size, weight, regulations, materials to avoid, brand requirements, existing parts to reuse
What exists Sketches, prototypes, competitor products, patents you know about, previous work
Timeline and budget Key dates, and a budget range for the next stage, so the proposal can be realistic
Deliverables What you want at the end of the next stage: a concept, a prototype, production-ready files
Decisions and people Who decides, how quickly, and who the consultancy should talk to
Practical rule: share a budget range. Without one, proposals range from a two-week review to a year of development, and none can be compared. A range lets a consultancy propose what is realistic.

Choosing a partner: questions to ask

  • What similar products or problems have you worked on, and what did you learn from them?
  • How would you tackle the riskiest part of this product first?
  • Who will actually do the work, and who will I speak to week to week?
  • How do you structure the work, and what decision points are there?
  • How do you handle changes to scope?
  • Who owns the intellectual property in the work, and when does it transfer?
  • What happens to CAD files and documentation at the end, and in what formats?
  • How do you work with manufacturers, and do you have any commercial relationship with them?

How engagements are usually structured

Model How it works Best for
Fixed price per stage An agreed scope, deliverables and price for each stage Well-defined stages, such as concepts or a DFM review
Time and materials Paid for time spent, usually with an estimate and regular reporting Uncertain work, such as early exploration or production problem-solving
Retainer or ongoing support A set amount of time each month Teams that need regular input across several decisions
Short review or advice A focused look at one question Second opinions before a big commitment

Staging the work protects both sides: each stage ends with a decision about whether to continue, change direction or stop, based on what was learned. Costs vary widely with the product's complexity and the depth of work, so compare proposals on scope and approach, not just price. How much it costs to develop a hardware product shows the wider budget the design work sits in.

Intellectual property and files

Unless the contract says otherwise, the designer may own copyright and design rights in their work. Make sure the agreement assigns the intellectual property in work produced for you, states when ownership transfers (often on payment), and covers delivery of native CAD files, neutral formats such as STEP, drawings and documentation. A confidentiality agreement before detailed discussions is sensible; see the IP, partners and compliance hub.

Worked example: a short brief

Illustrative example. "We are developing a wall-mounted charging dock for cordless garden tools, for homeowners who keep tools in sheds and garages. Twenty customer interviews and a pre-order page with 140 sign-ups suggest demand. It must hold two tools of different brands, survive an unheated shed (−10 to 35 °C), mount with standard fixings and cost no more than £14 to make at 3,000 units a year, to sell at £49.99 online. We have foam mock-ups and a list of competitor products. We need a tested concept and an engineering prototype by June, and have a budget range for this stage. Please propose how you would approach it, who would do the work, and the decision points." The details are invented for illustration; the structure is the point.

Working well together

  • Make decisions quickly. Slow decisions often cost more time than the engineering.
  • Give real feedback early. "Not sure" on a concept is more useful in week two than in month four.
  • Share what you learn from customers and suppliers; it changes the design.
  • Keep the requirements list current so changes in direction are visible and agreed.

Common mistakes

Briefing the solution, not the problem. You may get exactly what you asked for when a better answer existed.
Comparing proposals on day rate alone. Scope, experience and approach decide the total cost and the outcome.
Commissioning everything at once. Staged work lets you stop or change direction as you learn.
No agreement on IP and files. Fix ownership and deliverables in the contract before work starts.
Treating the consultancy as a supplier, not a partner. The best results come when they understand the business goals, not just the drawing.

FAQ

What does a product design consultancy do?

It helps turn an idea into a product that can be made: defining the brief, developing and comparing concepts, engineering and CAD, prototyping and testing, design for manufacture and support with suppliers and production. Many also offer shorter reviews and second opinions.

How do I brief a product design consultancy?

Describe the user and the problem, the requirements (measurable where possible), the target price, unit cost and volume, the constraints, what already exists, the timeline and a budget range for the next stage, the deliverables you want and who makes decisions. You do not need drawings to start.

Who owns the design when I use a consultancy?

It depends on the contract. Without an assignment, the designer may own copyright and design rights in the work. Make sure the agreement assigns the intellectual property to you, says when it transfers, and covers delivery of CAD files and documentation.

Should I use a consultancy or a freelancer?

A consultancy brings several disciplines and wider experience, which suits multi-disciplinary or first products. A freelancer can be more flexible and lower cost for focused tasks. Many projects use both at different stages.

How much does product design cost?

It varies enormously with the product's complexity and how far the work goes, from a short review to full development to production. Share a budget range in your brief and compare proposals on scope and approach as well as price.
