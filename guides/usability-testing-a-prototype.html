Usability testing a prototype: how to find out whether people can actually use it

What usability testing is and how it differs from customer discovery and technical testing, what to test at each prototype stage, who to recruit, how to plan and run task-based sessions without leading people, and how to turn what you see into prioritised design changes.

Quick answer

Usability testing means watching representative users try to do realistic tasks with your prototype, without help, and noting where they hesitate, make mistakes or work around the design.

Start early with rough models and repeat at each prototype round. A handful of sessions with people outside the team usually reveals the main problems. Give tasks rather than asking opinions, ask people to think aloud, do not help or explain, and then rank what you saw by how often it happened and how badly it affected the task. Fix the serious problems, update your requirements and test again.

What usability testing is, and what it is not

Usability testing asks one question: can the intended users do what the product is for, easily and without help? You give people a prototype and a set of realistic tasks, then watch. It is about behaviour, not opinion: what people do with the object in their hands is far more reliable than what they say about it.

It sits between two other activities that are often confused with it:

Activity Main question Typical evidence
Customer discovery Is the problem real, and how do people deal with it now? Interviews and observation, usually before there is a design
Usability testing Can people use this design to do the job? Observed tasks with a prototype: hesitations, errors, workarounds, comments
Technical verification Does the product meet its measurable requirements? Bench, durability, environmental and compliance tests

A product can pass every bench test and still fail the moment someone tries to open it.

What to test at each prototype stage

You do not need a working product to start, and the earlier a problem is found, the cheaper it is to change.

Prototype Good for testing Limits
Paper, card and foam models Overall size, grip, reach, where controls should go, the order of steps, labels and instructions Weight, feel and mechanisms are not realistic
Looks-like prototypes Handling, first impressions, whether people understand what it is and how to start, button and display layout Nothing works, so tasks have to be talked through or simulated
Works-like and integrated prototypes Real tasks end to end: set-up, use, cleaning, charging, recovering from mistakes Rough appearance can distract; later and more costly to change

Who to test with

Test with people who match your intended users: their experience, physical abilities, setting and the job they are trying to do. A handful of representative participants per round is usually enough to show the main problems, and several small rounds teach more than one large one.

  • Avoid testing only with friends, family and colleagues. They know too much, want to be kind and often do not match the users.
  • Never treat the team as users. You already know how it works, so you cannot see what is confusing.
  • Include the edges of your user group, such as first-time users, older users, left-handed users or people wearing gloves, if they will use the product.
  • Reuse your discovery contacts where they fit, and recruit fresh people for later rounds so earlier sessions do not teach them the answers.

Planning a task-based session

Write a short plan before the first session, so every participant gets the same tasks and the results can be compared:

  1. Goals. Two or three questions this round must answer, such as "can people fit the attachment without instructions?"
  2. Tasks. Realistic goals written in the user's language, not yours: "Make a smoothie for one" rather than "press the pulse button". Do not name controls in the task.
  3. A short script. A welcome, a reminder that you are testing the product rather than the person, the tasks in order, and a few closing questions.
  4. Set-up. A realistic setting where possible, any props needed (food, water, a worktop, a bag), and a way to record notes.
  5. Consent and privacy. Explain what the session involves, what you will record and how you will use and store it, and that people can stop at any time. Ask before photographing or filming, keep personal details separate from notes, and only keep what you need.

Sessions of around 30 to 60 minutes are typical for a simple product. Run one practice session with someone outside the team to find unclear tasks before the real ones.

Running the session

Hand over the prototype, read the first task and then mostly stay quiet. Ask the participant to think aloud: what they are looking for, what they expect and what surprises them.

  • Do not help, explain or lead. If someone is stuck, ask "what would you do now?" or "what are you looking for?". Only step in if they cannot continue, and note that you did.
  • Watch for hesitation, not just failure. A pause, a second look or a tentative press often marks a problem that people will not mention.
  • Note errors and workarounds, such as holding the product a way you did not intend or using two hands where one was planned.
  • Separate what you saw from what you think it means. Write "turned it over twice looking for the switch" rather than "switch hard to find".
Practical rule: when a participant asks you a question, answer with a question. Every time you explain the design, you remove the evidence you came for.

Simple measures, used qualitatively

With small numbers of participants, measures are for spotting patterns, not for statistics. Record the same few things for every task:

  • Completion: completed without help, completed with help, or not completed.
  • Time: roughly how long the task took, which mostly flags tasks that dragged.
  • Errors: wrong actions, false starts and recoveries.
  • Comments: what people said while doing it, in their own words.

A simple grid of participants against tasks makes patterns obvious. Avoid quoting percentages from a handful of sessions; say "four of five participants" instead.

Turning findings into changes

Write each problem as an observation, then rate it by how many participants it affected and how severe it was: did it stop the task, slow it down, cause a risk to safety, or just irritate? Fix problems that stop tasks or create safety risks first, then frequent slowdowns, then minor irritations.

Look for the cause, not just the symptom: if people miss a button, the answer might be its position, shape, label or the order of steps, not simply making it bigger. Turn settled findings into requirements, such as "the bowl can be removed with one hand without looking", in your product requirements document, so they are checked again on later prototypes and not lost.

Remote or in person

Physical products are usually best tested in person, because you need to see hands, posture and the way people pick things up. Remote sessions can still work well for later rounds: send a prototype in advance, agree a camera angle that shows the hands and the product, and keep tasks short. Remote testing also suits instructions, packaging and companion apps.

If you are working with Orion Design, early prototypes can be handled and reviewed together in person in Belfast, and later rounds reviewed the same way or remotely; see product design in Belfast.

Accessibility and inclusive design

Products are used by people with a wide range of strength, dexterity, sight, hearing and memory, and by everyone in poor conditions: wet hands, dim light, one hand busy. Designing for the edges often makes a product easier for everyone.

  • Include participants with a range of abilities and ages that reflects your real users, and ask what support they need to take part.
  • Test in realistic conditions, such as with gloves, at arm's length, or in dim light, where these are part of normal use.
  • Check that controls, markings and feedback do not rely on a single sense: combine colour with shape or text, and sound with light or touch.
  • Watch grip force, twisting and fine finger actions, which are common barriers.

Worked example: a handheld kitchen gadget

Illustrative example. A small team has a handheld, battery-powered kitchen gadget with one power button, a speed button and a detachable head that is washed separately. They test an integrated prototype with five home cooks recruited through a local cookery group, none of whom had seen the product. Tasks: charge it, fit the head, prepare a simple recipe, remove the head and clean it.

What the team observed Severity Change
Four of five pressed the speed button first, expecting it to switch the gadget on Slowed the task; caused confusion Swap button positions so power sits under the thumb; add a clearer power symbol
Three struggled to remove the head with wet hands; two twisted the wrong way Stopped the task for one participant Add a textured grip area and a moulded direction arrow; new requirement for wet-hand removal
Two did not notice the charging light while it was face down on the worktop Minor; caused uncertainty Move the indicator so it can be seen from above
One commented that the colour looked "cheap" Opinion, not a usability problem Noted for the appearance review; no change from one comment

The team made the first three changes on a revised looks-like model and ran a short second round with three new participants focused on the power button and head removal. The single colour comment was logged rather than acted on, because one opinion is not a pattern.

Common mistakes

Asking "do you like it?" Liking is not using. People are polite about prototypes; give them tasks and watch what they do instead.
Testing with the team. People who designed the product cannot forget how it works. Colleagues, friends and family are better than nobody for a first check, but never the only test.
Fixing the first comment heard. One participant's reaction can send the design the wrong way. Wait until the round is complete and look for patterns before changing anything.
Testing too late. Problems found on production-intent parts may need new tooling. A foam model and a few tasks can catch layout problems far earlier and more cheaply.

Decision rules

  • If you have a shape but no mechanism, test handling, layout and understanding with a looks-like or foam model now.
  • If several participants fail or hesitate on the same task, treat it as a design problem, not a user problem.
  • If a problem affects safety, fix it whatever the number of participants who met it.
  • If findings are mixed after one round, run another small round with new people rather than a bigger one.
  • Before design freeze, run a final round on an integrated or production-intent prototype against your usability requirements.

FAQ

How many people do I need for a usability test?

A handful of representative participants per round usually reveals the main problems with a simple product. Several small rounds, fixing problems in between, generally teach more than one large round. Add more people if your users vary a lot, for example in age, ability or setting.

Can I usability test before I have a working prototype?

Yes. Card, foam and looks-like models can test size, grip, control layout, labels and the order of steps. Ask people to talk through or act out tasks. Problems found this early are the cheapest to fix.

How is usability testing different from customer discovery?

Customer discovery explores the problem and how people deal with it today, usually before a design exists. Usability testing checks whether a particular design lets people do the job, by watching them use a prototype.

Should I pay or reward participants?

A small thank-you, such as a voucher, is common and makes recruitment easier and fairer. Keep it modest and the same for everyone, and make clear it does not depend on what they say about the product.

What should I do if participants disagree?

Focus on what they did rather than what they said. If behaviour differs, look at whether the participants differ in experience or setting; you may need to design for both groups or decide which matters most, and test again.
