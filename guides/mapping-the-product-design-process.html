Mapping the product design process.

Projects slow down when teams cannot see what should happen next. A process map helps prioritise discovery, prototyping, verification, industrialisation, and launch work based on the actual project context.

Quick answer

A product design process map shows the stages from discovery to launch and which of them deserve emphasis now.

Stage emphasis = biggest open uncertainty + cost of getting it wrong + time available

Most hardware projects move through discovery, concept development, prototyping, verification, industrialisation and launch. The idea-to-manufacture explainer covers each stage in turn, and the walkthrough follows one product through all of them. The stages overlap, and the time spent in each depends on product complexity, regulatory burden and how new the manufacturing route is.

No two hardware projects follow the exact same path, but almost all of them suffer when the team confuses stage pressure with process clarity. Mapping the process makes dependencies visible before the schedule starts making bad decisions for you.

Useful process rule: the right next stage is defined by what uncertainty matters most, not by which activity feels most familiar to the team.

The typical stages of hardware product development

Stage Main question Typical outputs Evidence to move on
Discovery Is there a real problem worth solving, for whom? User research, problem statement, early requirements A specific user and buyer with a clear, repeated need
Concept development Which approach best solves it? Sketches, rough models, concept selection matrix A chosen direction with its key risks named
Prototyping and detailed design Does it work, and can it be made? CAD, works-like and looks-like prototypes, draft BOM Critical functions proven on representative prototypes
Verification and validation Does it meet the specification, standards and user needs? Test plans, test reports, pre-compliance results Design passes testing with production-intent parts
Industrialisation Can it be made repeatably at the right cost? DFM changes, tooling, supplier agreements, pilot builds Pilot run meets quality and yield targets
Launch and support Can it be sold, delivered and supported? Production orders, packaging, service and spares plan Stable supply, returns and feedback being tracked

Teams with electronics often describe the later stages as EVT, DVT and PVT: engineering validation, design validation and production validation builds. The labels matter less than the principle. Each build should answer a defined question, and the next stage should not start in earnest until that question has a reasonable answer.

What changes the process emphasis

  • Product complexity and system interactions. More subsystems mean more integration work and more prototype loops.
  • Regulatory burden and verification load. Products with mains power, radios, food contact or medical claims need compliance planning from the concept stage, not the end.
  • Manufacturing novelty and supply-chain learning. A new process or a first-time supplier relationship needs more industrialisation time and earlier conversations.
  • Launch pressure. Schedule compression pushes stages to overlap, which is manageable only if the team knows which overlaps carry the most risk.

Illustrative example: two products, two maps

A low-complexity product with a long launch window can stay discovery and prototyping heavy for longer. A regulated product with manufacturing novelty may need earlier verification and industrialisation planning than the team expects.

Consider two illustrative projects. The first is a mechanical camping accessory made from two moulded parts and a stainless pin, with no electronics. Its map puts weight on discovery and prototyping, because the main risks are whether users want it and whether it survives rough handling. Verification is mostly drop and load testing, and industrialisation centres on one tooling decision.

The second is a mains-powered kitchen device with Wi-Fi. Here, electrical safety, EMC and radio requirements shape the electronics architecture, enclosure and labelling. Its map should start compliance planning during concept development, run pre-compliance checks on prototypes, and allow more time for verification and supplier qualification. Following the first project’s map would leave the second team discovering test failures after tooling has started.

How to build a process map for your project

  1. List the open uncertainties: user need, technical function, cost, compliance, supply and demand.
  2. Rank them by the cost of getting each one wrong late in the project.
  3. Match each high-ranking uncertainty to the stage and activity that resolves it.
  4. Define a simple gate for each stage: the evidence needed before committing more money.
  5. Hold a short design review at each gate and record the decision and actions.
  6. Plan the next three to six months in detail and the rest in outline only.
  7. Revisit the map at every gate, because the biggest uncertainty will change.

Common process-mapping mistakes

Treating the process as a straight line. Real projects loop back. A failed test can send a team from verification back to detailed design, and the plan should expect that.
Skipping discovery because the founder knows the user. Personal experience is a good starting point, not a substitute for talking to other buyers.
Leaving compliance to the end. Late test failures are among the most expensive surprises in hardware because they can force changes to tooled parts.
Starting tooling while verification is unresolved. Tooling against an unproven design locks in geometry that may still need to change.
Planning every stage in equal detail. Detailed plans for stages a year away usually turn out wrong. Effort is better spent on the next gate.

A good process map does not predict the project. It makes the next decision obvious and the cost of skipping a stage visible.

FAQ

What are the stages of the product design process?

For physical products the usual stages are discovery, concept development, prototyping and detailed design, verification and validation, industrialisation, and launch. In practice the stages overlap and loop back when testing reveals problems. The balance between them depends on the product’s complexity, regulation and manufacturing route.

How long does it take to develop a physical product?

It varies widely. A simple mechanical product with a stable concept can often reach production in well under a year, while a connected or regulated electronic product commonly takes a year or more. Compliance testing, tooling lead times and the number of prototype loops are usually what decide the timeline.

What do EVT, DVT and PVT mean?

They are common names for build stages in hardware development. EVT (engineering validation test) checks that the design works, DVT (design validation test) checks that production-intent parts meet the specification and standards, and PVT (production validation test) checks that the production line can make it repeatably.

Is the product design process linear?

No. It is usually drawn as a sequence, but real projects iterate. Prototyping often sends teams back to concept decisions, and verification can send them back to detailed design. A good process map plans for those loops instead of pretending they will not happen.

When should you start thinking about manufacturing?

From the concept stage. Choices about materials, part count and process shape cost and feasibility long before tooling is ordered. An early design for manufacture review is far cheaper than changing a design after it has been tooled.
