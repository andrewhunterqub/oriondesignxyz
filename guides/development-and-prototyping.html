Development and prototyping: how a hardware product gets from idea to production-ready

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about developing a physical product: the stages most products pass through, which prototype answers which question, how many rounds to plan, and how testing and design freeze keep the project under control. Each section links to a deeper guide or a free tool.

Quick answer

Product development is a sequence of questions, each answered by the cheapest build that can answer it.

A hardware product typically moves from requirements and concepts, through proof-of-principle, looks-like and works-like prototypes, to engineering prototypes, design verification and a pilot production run. Plan each prototype round around the specific risk it must retire, expect to go round the loop more than once, and keep the expensive commitments, such as production tooling and certification, until the design has stopped moving.

The stages of hardware development

Different companies use different names, but most physical products pass through the same stages. Electronics teams often call the last three EVT, DVT and PVT (engineering, design and production validation test). What matters is not the label but the question each stage answers and the evidence it leaves behind.

Stage Main outputs Typical builds Ready to move on when…
1. Define User needs, a requirements list with measurable targets, constraints (cost, size, regulation) Sketches, cardboard or foam models The team agrees what "good" looks like and how it will be measured
2. Concept Several distinct directions, a scored comparison, a chosen concept Quick mock-ups, simple rigs One direction clearly beats the others against the requirements
3. Proof of principle Evidence that the core mechanism, sensor or process works Bench rigs, breadboards, off-the-shelf parts The riskiest technical question has a convincing yes
4. Looks-like and works-like User feedback on form and use; a functional model of the system Appearance models; separate functional prototypes Users understand and want it; the system works end to end
5. Engineering prototype An integrated design that combines form and function; detailed CAD 3D-printed, CNC or vacuum-cast parts with real components It meets the specification on the bench and in early user trials
6. Design verification Test reports against requirements; pre-compliance results; design freeze Production-intent parts, sometimes from bridge tooling Tests pass with margin and the design is frozen under change control
7. Pilot production Production process, work instructions, inspection plan, first-article reports Parts from production tools, assembled on the real line The factory can build it repeatably at the quality and cost planned

The Product Design Process Map lets you place your own product on these stages, and mapping the product design process explains how to plan the work in each.

Types of prototype and what each is for

Prototype Question it answers Common methods
Proof of principle Does the core idea work? Bench rigs, development boards, modified existing products
Looks-like Is the size, shape, feel and appearance right? SLA printing, CNC, painted and finished models
Works-like Does the full system function? Functional parts in a rough housing, wired-up electronics
Engineering (alpha) Does the integrated design meet the specification? Printed, machined or vacuum-cast parts with production-intent components
Field trial (beta) What happens in real use, over time? Small batch for trusted users, often vacuum-cast or bridge-tooled
Pre-production Do production parts and processes give the right result? Parts from production or bridge tools, built on the intended line

Separating looks-like from works-like is one of the most useful habits in early development: each can be built quickly and cheaply when it does not have to do the other's job; looks-like vs works-like prototypes explains when to combine them. The Prototype Strategy Tool suggests the build method for each question, and prototyping versus production compares the processes in detail.

How many prototype rounds to plan

Many products need two or three rounds between first concept and production-intent parts, and complex, connected or safety-critical products often need more. Rather than fixing a number, list the questions that must be answered before tooling and group them into rounds:

  • Answer the riskiest question first. If the product fails because the core function does not work, nothing else matters.
  • One round, one purpose. A round that tries to prove function, appearance and manufacturability at once usually proves none of them well.
  • Use the cheapest build that can answer the question. A rig on a bench can settle a mechanism long before a housing exists.
  • Plan for at least one surprise. Budget time and money for a round you cannot yet name; one is often needed.
Practical rule: write down, before each build, the question it answers and the result that would change your plan. If you cannot, the build is probably not needed yet.

Testing and validation

Testing turns prototypes into evidence. A simple test plan lists each requirement, how it will be tested, the pass criterion and the result; the Product Test Plan Builder gives you a starting list. Typical areas include function and performance, user testing, durability and life cycles, drop and impact, temperature and humidity, and, for products that need it, pre-compliance checks for electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility before formal certification.

Pre-compliance testing is worth doing early for any electrical product: problems found at a test house after tooling are among the most expensive to fix. For which rules apply to your product, start from official guidance such as GOV.UK's guidance on UKCA marking, and take specialist advice for regulated categories. Product certification basics explains the process.

Worked example: planning the rounds for a handheld device

Illustrative example. A start-up is developing a rechargeable handheld device with a sensor, a small display and a two-part plastic housing. Instead of commissioning "a prototype", the team lists its open questions and plans four rounds.

Round Question Build Decision it unlocks
1 Can the sensor give a stable reading in real conditions? Development board and sensor on a bench rig Choose the sensor and electronics architecture
2 Is the size and grip right; can users read the display? Three printed looks-like models; a works-like unit in a rough box Fix the form, display size and button layout
3 Does the integrated design fit, work, charge and survive a drop? Five engineering prototypes with printed housings and a custom circuit board Release the design for production-intent detailing
4 Do production-intent parts pass the tests and pre-compliance checks? Twenty vacuum-cast units for testing and a short field trial Freeze the design and commit to tooling

Each round is small and has a single job. If round 3 shows the housing cracks in a drop test, the fix costs a reprint rather than a tool modification. The plan also gives the team a clear point, after round 4, at which committing to tooling is justified. Because the product contains a custom circuit board, the board and housing were developed side by side; electronics in a physical product covers what to plan early.

Keeping the design under control

As prototypes multiply, keep a light but disciplined record: a requirements list with test results against it, a revision number on every part and drawing, and a short change log saying what changed and why. At design freeze, any further change should go through a simple change-control step, because from that point changes ripple into tooling, suppliers, test reports and cost.

The same records are what a manufacturer will ask for. See the documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer.

Common mistakes

Building a beautiful prototype too early. High-finish models are slow and expensive, and teams become reluctant to change them.
Testing to confirm rather than to learn. A test that cannot fail tells you nothing. Set pass criteria before you see the result.
Skipping design for manufacture until the end. Wall thickness, draft, part count and assembly should shape the engineering prototype, not be retrofitted after it.
Leaving compliance to the end. Early pre-compliance checks are much cheaper than redesign after a failed formal test.
No written record of why decisions were made. Six months later nobody remembers why a material or dimension was chosen, and the same question gets answered again.

FAQ

What are the stages of hardware product development?

Most products pass through defining requirements, concept development, proof of principle, looks-like and works-like prototypes, engineering prototypes, design verification and pilot production. Electronics teams often call the last three EVT, DVT and PVT. Each stage answers one main question before money is committed to the next.

What is the difference between a looks-like and a works-like prototype?

A looks-like prototype shows the size, shape, finish and feel of the product but may not function. A works-like prototype proves the product functions but may look nothing like the final design. Building them separately is faster and cheaper than trying to make one model do both jobs early on.

How many prototypes does a hardware product need?

Many products need two or three rounds before production-intent parts, and complex or regulated products often need more. Plan rounds around the questions that must be answered rather than a fixed number, and allow for at least one unplanned round.

When should a design be frozen?

When testing on production-intent parts shows the requirements are met with some margin, and before committing to production tooling and formal certification. After the freeze, changes should go through a simple change-control step because they affect tools, suppliers and test reports.

How long does hardware product development take?

It varies widely with complexity, regulation and how quickly decisions are made. A simple mechanical product can move from concept to production in months, while connected, medical or safety-critical products commonly take considerably longer.
