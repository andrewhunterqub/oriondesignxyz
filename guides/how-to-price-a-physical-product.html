How to price a physical product: value, competitors, margins and testing

A practical method for pricing a physical product: start from the value to customers and their alternatives, check comparable products, work out the price floor from costs and margins, and test the price before committing to it.

Quick answer

Price from the value to the customer and their alternatives, then check that cost and every margin in the chain still work at that price.

Ceiling = what customers will pay, given their alternatives Floor = landed cost ÷ (1 − your margin) ÷ (1 − channel margins) × (1 + VAT) A viable price sits between the floor and the ceiling

Cost-plus pricing on its own tends to under-price good products and over-price weak ones. Start with what the product is worth to the buyer and what they pay today to solve the problem, look at comparable products, then work through the margins your route to market needs. If the floor is above the ceiling, change the cost, the channel or the product before launching.

Three ways to think about price

Approach How it works Strength Weakness
Cost-plus Add a standard markup to the unit cost Simple; guarantees a margin on paper Ignores what customers will pay and what competitors charge
Competitor-based Position against similar products Grounded in what buyers already accept Copies others' assumptions; weak if your product is genuinely different
Value-based Price from the benefit to the customer and the cost of their alternative Captures the value you create Needs evidence about customers; harder to estimate

In practice the best answer uses all three: value and competitors set the ceiling, cost and margins set the floor.

Step 1: understand the value and the alternatives

  • What does the customer do today? The current workaround, and what it costs in money, time or frustration, is the real reference point.
  • What is the benefit worth? Time saved, cost avoided, risk reduced, enjoyment or status gained. For business buyers, this is often measurable.
  • Who decides and who pays? A gift buyer, a parent, or a purchasing department each weigh price differently.

How to validate a product idea covers how to gather this evidence.

Step 2: look at comparable products

List the products customers will compare yours with, including indirect alternatives, and note their prices, features, reviews and where they are sold. Decide deliberately where you sit: cheaper with fewer features, similar with a clear advantage, or premium with a reason customers can see.

Step 3: work out the floor

The floor is the lowest shelf price at which everyone in the chain earns their margin. Start from the landed unit cost, add your margin, then the margins of any distributor and retailer, then VAT. The Pricing Ladder calculates this, and Target COGS by Route to Market works the other way, from a price down to the cost you can afford.

Illustrative figures only. A product lands at £8.00 a unit. Selling to a retailer that wants a 45% margin, with the brand keeping 50%, the shelf price needs to be about £34.91 including VAT. Comparable products sell for £29.99 to £39.99, and interviews suggest buyers value the time it saves highly, so £39.99 is credible and leaves about £5 of headroom. Through a distributor taking 30% as well, the floor rises to about £41.56 at a 40% brand margin, above the comparable range: that route would need a lower landed cost or a lower margin somewhere.

Step 4: check the business still works

A price that covers margins can still fail if volumes are too low to recover fixed costs. Check break-even with the Break-even Calculator and the overall picture with the Launch Margin Planner. Leave room for promotions, returns and marketing, which come out of someone's margin.

Step 5: test before you commit

  • Pre-orders or a small batch at the intended price are the strongest evidence.
  • A landing page with the price shown measures interest at a price, not just interest in the idea.
  • Conversations with retailers or distributors reveal what they believe the product can sell for in their channel.
Practical rule: it is far easier to lower a launch price than to raise it. If you are unsure, start at the higher end of a credible range and use offers, bundles or a second model to reach lower price points.

Keeping prices consistent across channels

If you sell both direct and through retailers, set prices so every channel works. Undercutting your retailers on your own website damages the relationship; many brands keep the same recommended price everywhere and compete on service, bundles or exclusives instead. See Route to market and margins.

Common mistakes

Pricing from cost alone. You may leave money on the table, or price a product nobody values at that level.
Forgetting channel margins and VAT. The shelf price is often several times the landed cost once everyone is paid.
Pricing for launch, not for scale. A direct-only price may not survive the move into retail.
Asking people what they would pay. Stated willingness to pay is unreliable; test with real offers.
No room for promotions. Discounts and offers are part of retail; plan for them in the margin.

FAQ

How do I price a physical product?

Start with the value to the customer and the price of their alternatives to find the ceiling, then work out the floor from landed cost plus your margin, channel margins and VAT. A viable price sits between the two; test it with pre-orders or a small batch before committing.

What markup should I use on a product?

There is no single right markup, because channel margins and costs differ by product and route. Work out the margins each party needs as a share of their selling price; selling through retail commonly requires a shelf price several times the landed cost.

Should I price lower to win customers at launch?

Usually not by default. It is much easier to lower a price than to raise it, and a low launch price can leave too little margin for retail later. Use time-limited offers or bundles instead of a permanently low price.

How do I know if my price is too high?

Test it: pre-orders, a landing page showing the price, or a small batch will show whether people buy. Compare with what customers pay for alternatives. Stated willingness to pay in surveys is a weak signal.

Should my website price match retail prices?

Many brands keep the same recommended price across channels to protect retailer relationships, and compete on service, bundles or exclusives rather than undercutting their own retailers.
