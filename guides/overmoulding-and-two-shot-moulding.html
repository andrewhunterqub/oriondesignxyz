Overmoulding and two-shot moulding explained

What overmoulding, two-shot and insert moulding mean, why products use them, how the two materials stay together, the design rules to agree with your moulder, the tooling trade-offs, and how to prototype a two-material part before committing to steel.

Quick answer

Overmoulding moulds a second material, often a soft thermoplastic elastomer (TPE), over or around a first part so the two come out of the tool as one component.

It gives grip, impact protection, a seal or strain relief without separate parts to fit. It works only when the materials bond well or are locked together, and it costs more in tooling than a separate part. At low volumes a glued or pressed-in part is often the better choice; at higher volumes, or where a loose part would be a reliability problem, overmoulding usually earns its place.

What the terms mean

All three processes are variations on standard injection moulding. The difference is what is already in the tool when the plastic is injected, and how it got there.

  • Overmoulding is the general term for moulding a second material over a first part, called the substrate. The most common case is a rigid plastic housing with a soft TPE grip, bumper or seal moulded onto it. The substrate may be made in a separate tool and moved across, by hand or by robot, which is often called pick-and-place overmoulding.
  • Two-shot moulding, also called 2K or multi-shot moulding, makes both materials in one machine and one tool. The machine has two injection units. The first shot forms the substrate, the tool rotates or shuttles it to a second cavity, and the second shot is moulded onto it while it is still warm.
  • Insert moulding moulds plastic around a part that is not itself moulded in that tool: a threaded metal insert, a pin, a cable, or an electronic part such as a sensor or small circuit board. The insert is placed in the tool before each shot.

Why use a second material

  • Grip and feel. A soft-touch grip on a handheld tool or device feels better, is less slippery and can hide fastener heads and joins.
  • Bumpers and impact protection. A soft band at the corners spreads the force of a drop over a longer time. The drops and impacts guide explains when that beats a geometry change.
  • Seals moulded in place. A TPE seal moulded onto a housing flange cannot be lost or fitted wrongly, though it cannot be replaced on its own. The sealing and waterproofing guide compares it with gaskets and O-rings.
  • Cable strain relief. An overmoulded boot where a lead leaves a plug or housing takes the bending and pulling away from the conductors, as described in cables, wiring and connectors.
  • Integrated buttons. A flexible membrane moulded into a rigid housing can act as a sealed button over a switch. See designing buttons, switches and controls for the feel and travel side.
  • Fewer assembly steps. One component instead of two means no adhesive, no fitting operation and nothing to fall off in use.

Bonding between materials

The two materials hold together in two ways: a chemical bond, where the hot second material partly fuses with the surface of the substrate, and a mechanical lock, where the geometry stops them separating. Most reliable parts use both.

Chemical compatibility is the first check. TPEs are commonly overmoulded onto PP, ABS or PC, for example, but whether a particular pair bonds well depends on the grades, not just the families. TPE suppliers publish compatibility data and offer grades intended to bond to specific substrates. Check that data for the exact grades you plan to use, tell your moulder which pair you have chosen, and confirm the bond with peel tests on trial parts. The choosing plastic materials guide covers the substrate side of that choice.

Where bonding is weak, or peel forces will be high, add mechanical interlocks: holes through the substrate that the second material flows through and caps behind, undercuts or grooves it wraps into, or a wrap over an edge. They are also a sensible backstop for parts that will be handled roughly or heat cycled.

Practical rule: never rely on a chemical bond you have not tested on real moulded parts. Design in at least some mechanical interlock wherever a peeled edge would be a product failure.

Design guidance

These are typical guidelines; the right values depend on the materials, part size and the moulder's equipment, so confirm each one with your moulder before the tool is designed. The general rules in the injection moulding DFM checklist still apply to both shots.

  • Shut-off edges. The second material needs a crisp line on the substrate where the tool seals against it and the soft material stops. A small step, groove or raised lip gives the tool a positive edge to clamp on; a shut-off on a smooth, flat surface is more likely to flash.
  • Avoid thin, feathered edges. A soft layer that tapers to nothing at its edge is likely to lift, peel or tear. End it against a step so it keeps a sensible thickness right to the edge.
  • Flow paths. The second material must be able to fill the whole area from its gate. Long, thin runs of soft material may not fill, so keep flow lengths reasonable or add gates, and make sure air can escape at the far end.
  • Uniform overmould thickness. As with any moulding, even thickness helps the part fill and cool evenly. Very thin areas may not fill; very thick areas can sink and take longer to cool.
  • Gates and parting lines. Agree early where each material's gate and parting line will go. Gate marks and witness lines on a soft grip are visible and can be felt, so keep them away from where the hand rests or a seal must seal.

Cost and tooling trade-offs

In relative terms, from cheapest tooling to most expensive, the options usually run like this:

Route Tooling Usually suits
Separate part, glued or pressed in A simple tool or bought-in part for the soft piece Low volumes, early production, parts that must be replaceable
Pick-and-place overmoulding Two tools: one for the substrate, one for the overmould; a standard machine can run each Moderate volumes; moulders without two-shot machines
Two-shot moulding One more complex tool, run on a specialised two-shot machine Higher volumes, where the shorter cycle and lack of handling pay back the tool

Two-shot tools are more complex to design and trial, and fewer moulders have the machines. Pick-and-place adds handling time to every part. At low volumes, a separately moulded grip or seal that is glued or pressed into a groove often does the job for far less tooling. You can move to overmoulding later once sales justify it, as part of a risk-progressive manufacturing strategy.

Prototyping a two-material part

You can approximate an overmoulded part before tooling, but none of the methods fully reproduces the real thing:

  • Cast urethane overmoulding. A soft urethane is cast in a silicone mould around a printed or machined substrate. It gives a good impression of feel and appearance and is useful for user testing, but the bond and the material are not those of the production pair.
  • Multi-material printing. Some printers can combine rigid and flexible materials in one build. Good for checking shape, layout and rough feel quickly, but the soft materials, surface finish and bond strength differ from moulded TPE.

Use prototypes to decide shape and feel, not as evidence of the production bond or durability; that comes only from trial parts off the real tool.

Worked example: from glued sleeve to overmoulded grip

Illustrative example. A small company makes a handheld measuring device with a rigid moulded housing and a rubber sleeve glued around the handle. Returns showed sleeves lifting at one end after a few months, and gluing was a slow, messy assembly step.

With volumes now steady, they ask their moulder about a TPE overmould onto the existing housing material. The moulder suggests pick-and-place overmoulding, so the existing housing tool can be modified rather than replaced, with a second tool for the grip.

The design changes agreed before the tool was made:

  • Material pair: a TPE grade the supplier lists as bonding to the housing grade, confirmed with the moulder.
  • Interlocks: a row of small holes through the handle wall at each end of the grip, where peeling had started on the old sleeve, with a recess inside so the soft material forms a head behind each hole.
  • Shut-offs: a small step around the handle at both ends of the grip, so the overmould ends at a defined edge instead of feathering out.
  • Thickness and gating: an even grip thickness, with the gate placed on the underside away from the thumb rest.

At the tool trials, the team checked trial parts for flash at the shut-offs, short shots at the far end of the grip, and sink in the thicker areas. They ran simple peel tests by hand and after a few days of heat and cold cycling, and checked that the housing had not distorted in the second shot. One shut-off showed light flash and was corrected before the samples were signed off. The gluing step was removed from the assembly line.

Common mistakes

Choosing materials by family, not grade. "TPE on ABS" says little on its own. Check the suppliers' compatibility data for the exact grades and test the bond.
No mechanical interlock. Relying only on the chemical bond leaves nothing to stop an edge lifting once it starts.
Feathered edges and vague shut-offs. A soft layer that thins to nothing peels, and a shut-off with no edge to clamp on flashes.
Overmoulding too early. Committing to a two-shot tool before the design and volumes have settled makes every change expensive. A separate part is often better for the first batches.
Trusting the prototype bond. Cast or printed parts show the feel, not how the production materials will hold together.

Next steps

  1. Write down what the second material must do: grip, protect, seal or relieve strain. That sets the material and thickness.
  2. Shortlist material pairs using the suppliers' compatibility data, then discuss them with your moulder.
  3. Decide between a separate part, pick-and-place and two-shot based on your expected volumes, and revisit it as volumes grow.
  4. Add interlocks and shut-off steps to the CAD, and check both parts with the DFM Checker.
  5. Plan bond, flash and fill checks into the tool trials.

FAQ

What is the difference between overmoulding and two-shot moulding?

Overmoulding is the general term for moulding a second material over a first part. Two-shot moulding is one way of doing it, with both materials made in one machine and one tool. Pick-and-place overmoulding uses two separate tools instead.

Will a soft grip stay bonded to a hard plastic housing?

It can, if the two grades are compatible and the part is designed well. Check the material suppliers' compatibility data for the exact grades, add mechanical interlocks where peeling would matter, and test the bond on trial parts from the real tool.

Is overmoulding worth it at low volumes?

Often not. The extra tooling is shared over few parts, so a separately moulded grip or seal that is glued or pressed in is usually cheaper at first. Overmoulding tends to pay back once volumes are steady or a loose part causes problems.

What is insert moulding used for?

Moulding plastic around a part placed in the tool before each shot, such as a threaded metal insert, a pin, a cable or a small electronic part. It gives a strong, sealed joint without a separate assembly step.

Can I prototype an overmoulded part without a mould tool?

Yes, approximately. Cast urethane over a printed substrate, or multi-material printing, shows shape and feel well enough for user testing. Neither shows how the production materials will bond, so the bond is proven only at tool trials.
