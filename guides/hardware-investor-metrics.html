Hardware investor metrics that actually matter.

Investors do not just want growth. They want to know whether growth improves the business, buys time, and demonstrates a repeatable commercial engine.

Quick answer

Investors in hardware look for real demand, healthy gross margin, efficient acquisition and enough runway to reach the next milestone.

CAC payback (orders) = customer acquisition cost / gross profit per order after fulfilment

The core set is monthly revenue, gross margin, customer acquisition cost (CAC), payback and runway. Together they show whether growth makes the business stronger or simply consumes cash faster.

Early hardware businesses often report vanity signals because they feel easier to defend. The stronger story usually sits in fewer numbers: revenue quality, gross margin, acquisition efficiency, payback, and runway.

Hardware brings its own complications. Stock is paid for before it is sold, unit cost changes with volume, and most products are bought once rather than paid for monthly. That means investor metrics built for software need adapting, and some hardware-specific numbers deserve a place alongside them.

Good reporting discipline: every metric should help answer one of three questions. Is demand real? Is the margin good enough? Is the company buying enough time to prove the next stage?

The core metrics to watch

Monthly revenueDemand converted into cash generationShows the scale of the commercial engine

How to calculate them

Gross margin % = (revenue - landed cost of goods sold) / revenue
CAC = sales and marketing spend in the period / new customers in the period
Runway (months) = cash in the bank / net monthly burn

For a one-off hardware purchase, payback is usually best expressed in orders rather than months: does the first order recover the cost of winning the customer? If the product has consumables, accessories or a subscription, repeat gross profit can be added, but only on evidence of actual repeat behaviour.

Hardware-specific metrics worth adding

Contribution after fulfilment. Gross profit minus pick and pack, shipping, payment fees and returns. This is what each order really leaves behind.
Inventory days or turns. How long stock sits before it sells. Slow-moving stock is cash the business cannot use.
Return and warranty rate. An early signal of quality or expectation problems that will erode margin as volume grows.
Current versus target COGS. Investors will want to see today's landed cost and a credible, evidenced path to the cost at scale.
Attach rate. For products with consumables or accessories, the share of customers who buy again. This is often where lifetime value comes from.

Worked example: two versions of the same month

Illustrative figures only. A direct-to-consumer hardware brand sells 600 units in a month at a net price of GBP120, giving GBP72,000 of revenue. Landed COGS is GBP54, so gross profit is GBP66 per unit and gross margin is 55%. Marketing spend of GBP18,000 brings in those 600 customers, so CAC is GBP30.

Fulfilment and payment costs add GBP12 per order, leaving GBP54 of contribution after fulfilment. After CAC, each order leaves GBP24, or GBP14,400 for the month. Fixed overhead is GBP45,000, so net burn is GBP30,600. With GBP420,000 in the bank, runway is about 13.7 months. The first order more than recovers CAC.

Now suppose the same 600 sales were only achieved with a 20% discount, at GBP96. Gross profit falls to GBP42 per unit, margin drops to under 44%, and after fulfilment and CAC each order leaves nothing at all. Revenue still looks healthy at GBP57,600, but net burn rises to GBP45,000 and runway shrinks to about nine months.

A business with rising revenue but weak gross margin and poor payback may look busy while becoming more fragile. Investors usually see that contradiction quickly.

Growth is only good news if each extra sale leaves the business stronger than before it was made.

Common reporting mistakes

Reporting units shipped instead of revenue received. Shipments to a retailer on sale-or-return or long payment terms are not the same as cash.
Gross margin on factory price. Margin should use landed cost, including freight, duty and packaging, or it will flatter the business.
Blended CAC hiding paid CAC. Mixing organic and paid customers can make acquisition look cheaper than it will be once growth depends on paid channels.
Quoting future COGS as current. Cost at scale is a target. Show it separately from the cost you are paying today.
Ignoring stock in runway. A planned stock order can shorten runway sharply. Runway should reflect committed purchases, not just overhead.

Practical next steps

  1. Calculate gross margin on landed cost and contribution after fulfilment for your current product.
  2. Separate paid and organic CAC, and check whether the first order recovers paid CAC.
  3. Build runway from a monthly cash model that includes stock orders, as set out in calculating hardware startup cash flow.
  4. Show current COGS next to target COGS, with the steps that close the gap.
  5. Tie the numbers back to the opportunity in your hardware business case.

FAQ

What metrics do investors look for in a hardware startup?

Most start with monthly revenue, gross margin, customer acquisition cost, payback and runway. For hardware they also look closely at landed unit cost and the path to lower cost at scale, inventory levels, return rates and any repeat revenue from consumables or accessories.

What gross margin do investors expect for hardware?

Expectations vary by category and channel, but many investors look for a credible path to gross margins of roughly 40 per cent or more on landed cost. Lower margins can work with strong repeat revenue or very efficient distribution, but they leave less room for mistakes.

How do you calculate CAC payback for a one-off hardware purchase?

Divide the cost of acquiring a customer by the gross profit from their order after fulfilment costs. A result below one means the first order recovers the acquisition cost. Only include repeat purchases if you have evidence customers actually make them.

What is a good LTV to CAC ratio?

A commonly quoted rule of thumb is a lifetime value of at least three times CAC. For one-off hardware purchases with little repeat business, lifetime value is close to first-order gross profit, so the more useful test is whether that first order clearly covers CAC.

How is runway calculated for a hardware business?

Runway is cash in the bank divided by net monthly burn. For hardware, burn should include committed stock purchases and supplier deposits, not just overhead, because a single stock order can shorten runway by months.
