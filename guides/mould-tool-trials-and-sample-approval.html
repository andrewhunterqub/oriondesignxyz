Mould tool trials and sample approval: from first shots to signed-off parts

What happens between approving a tool design and starting production, what to ask the toolmaker for with each round of trial samples, how to read common moulding defects, how tool changes are handled and paid for, and how to sign off the parts that production will be judged against.

Quick answer

A new mould is tried, adjusted and tried again until its parts match the drawing; only then are samples signed off as the reference for production.

Expect at least one round of changes. For each trial, ask for the shot count, the recorded process settings, the material and colour batch, a short trial report and a dimensional report against the drawing's critical dimensions. Fix dimensions and defects first, texture the tool after the dimensions are approved, then sign off approved samples and limit samples, record the approved revision and move on to a pilot run.

From steel to first shots

Once you approve the tool design, the toolmaker orders steel and starts cutting the cavity, core, slides and cooling. Injection moulding explained covers the process and the tooling project as a whole; this guide picks up when the tool is first put in a moulding machine.

At the first trial the toolmaker fills the tool for the first time, checks it opens, ejects and cools properly, and starts to find workable process settings. These first shots are rarely right, and that is normal: the trial tests the tool, not yet your product.

Trials are usually labelled with a T and a number. Many toolmakers call the very first shots T0 and the first samples sent to the customer T1, with T2, T3 and so on after each round of changes. Others start at T1 for the first shots. The naming varies by toolmaker, so ask what yours means by each label and write it into the quote or order, along with how many trial rounds are included.

What to ask for with trial samples

A box of parts on its own tells you little. Ask for the following with every trial round, and put the list in your order:

  • Shot count: how many shots were run and which shots your samples came from.
  • Recorded process settings: temperatures, pressures, times and the machine used, so you can tell whether the tool or the process changed between rounds.
  • Material and colour batch: the grade, the supplier and the batch or lot of material and colourant, so you know the samples were made in the material you specified and not a substitute.
  • A trial report: a short note of what the toolmaker saw, what they think caused it and what they propose to change.
  • A dimensional report: measurements against the critical dimensions marked on your drawing, on more than one part, with each result shown as in or out of tolerance. Quality control and first-article inspection explains how critical features and inspection reports fit into the wider quality plan.

Ask for enough parts to measure, assemble and keep a few as a record, and label each set with its trial number and date.

Practical rule: judge trial parts against the drawing and the agreed sample list, not against how the CAD model looked on screen. If a requirement is not on the drawing or in the order, it is hard to hold the toolmaker to it.

Reading the samples: common defects

Most first-trial problems fall into a short list. The same defect can come from the process (how the machine runs the tool), the tool steel or the part design, so treat the table as a starting point for a conversation with the toolmaker, not a diagnosis.

Defect What it looks like What it usually points to
Short shot The part is incomplete, often at the end furthest from the gate or at thin features Process first (pressure, speed, temperature); if it persists, venting, gate size or wall thickness in the steel or design
Sink Shallow dips on a surface opposite a thick section, rib or boss Thick sections in the design; process (hold pressure and time) can reduce it but rarely removes it
Flash Thin fins of plastic along the parting line or around inserts and ejectors Process (too much pressure) or steel that does not seal, such as a worn or poorly fitted shut-off
Warp The part bows or twists, so flat faces are not flat or mating edges do not meet Uneven cooling or uneven walls; process changes help, but cooling layout or design changes may be needed
Weld lines Fine lines where two flow fronts meet, often around holes Gate position and part geometry in the steel; process can make them fainter. They can also be weaker points
Gate marks A small blemish or raised stub where the plastic entered the part Gate type and position in the steel; trimming and process settings affect its size
Ejector marks Round witness marks or shiny spots where ejector pins pushed the part out Pin position and fit in the steel; process (cooling time, sticking) if the marks are deep or stressed
Flow lines Wavy lines or rings on the surface, often near the gate Mostly process (speed, melt and tool temperature); sometimes gate design or sudden wall changes
Colour or texture mismatch The colour, gloss or texture differs from the agreed reference Material or colourant batch and process temperature for colour; the steel finish for texture and gloss

Check parts in even light at the distance a customer would view them, and assemble them with their mating parts. Many of these defects are designed out before steel is cut; from 3D-printed prototype to moulded part covers draft, wall thickness, gates and shrinkage at the design stage.

Tool modifications and who pays

After each trial, the toolmaker proposes changes. Process changes cost little. Steel changes take longer, and there is one rule that shapes them: taking steel off is much easier than adding it back. Removing steel adds plastic to the part; adding steel usually means welding or inserting metal, which is slower and riskier.

That is why experienced toolmakers and designers leave critical features steel-safe: the tool is first cut so that any likely correction is a matter of removing steel. A hole might be cut slightly small so it can be opened up after measuring. Ask which features have been left steel-safe.

Agree who pays for each kind of change before the first trial. A common split is that the toolmaker pays to correct its own errors and to bring the tool into line with the agreed drawing, while you pay for changes to the design itself. Write this down, along with how many trial rounds the quote includes; supply agreements with a manufacturer covers tooling terms, and the right wording for your situation may be worth checking with an adviser.

Texturing comes after the dimensions

If the part has a textured or grained surface, the texture is usually applied once the dimensions are approved. Texturing changes the surface of the steel, and any later steel change on that face may mean re-texturing it. Textured faces also need extra draft; surface finishes for plastic and metal parts explains the choices.

Approve texture and colour against physical references, such as texture plaques or a colour sample, rather than a screen or a printed photo. Expect a further trial after texturing to confirm the finish and release.

Signing off the samples

Sign-off turns a good trial into the standard production is judged against. It usually has four parts:

  • Approved or golden samples: parts made on the production tool, in the production material and colour, that meet the drawing. Sign, date and keep a set at both ends. Quality control and first-article inspection covers how golden samples are then used.
  • Limit samples for cosmetics: parts that show the worst acceptable version of a cosmetic feature, such as the largest acceptable gate mark or the faintest acceptable weld line.
  • A record of the approved revision: note which drawing and model revision the samples were approved against, and any agreed deviations. Update the part's revision in your bill of materials.
  • The next step: with samples approved, the process is proven for a single part. A pilot production run then proves the whole line makes good, packed products repeatedly.

Approving samples remotely

An overseas toolmaker is no barrier to good approval, but it takes planning:

  • Photos taken in agreed lighting, of agreed faces, with a scale or ruler in shot.
  • Video of the tool opening and the parts ejecting, and of the parts being assembled, flexed or fitted.
  • Samples by courier for anything you need to handle, measure or fit to other parts. Allow for transit and customs.
  • A third-party inspection service local to the toolmaker can witness a trial, measure parts against your drawing and report back.

Worked example: one housing through two trials

Illustrative example. A start-up is tooling the lower housing of a small handheld device: a moulded shell with four screw bosses, a battery opening and a long, flat side wall that mates with the top cover. The drawing marks three critical dimensions: boss spacing, battery opening width and the flatness of the mating edge.

T0 (first shots). The toolmaker reports short shots at the corner furthest from the gate and warp along the long side wall, so the housing does not close fully against the top cover. Process changes improve the fill but do not cure it. The battery opening measures slightly narrow, as intended, because it was cut steel-safe.

Modification. The toolmaker adds venting at the corner that filled short and adjusts the cooling and process to reduce the warp. Steel is removed to open the battery opening to its nominal size. Both changes bring the tool into line with the agreed drawing, so under the agreed terms the toolmaker pays.

T1. The parts fill fully, all three critical dimensions are within tolerance on every part measured, and the housing closes against the top cover. The only issue is a gate mark on the inside face, slightly larger than the founder hoped. It is hidden once assembled and moving the gate would mean new steel, so both sides accept it and keep a part as a limit sample showing the largest acceptable gate mark. The tool is then textured, a short trial confirms the finish, golden samples are signed, the BOM records the approved revision, and the project moves on to its pilot run.

Common mistakes

Expecting the first samples to be right. A first trial is a test of the tool. Plan time and money for at least one round of changes.
Accepting parts with no reports. Without the process settings, material batch and dimensional report, you cannot tell what changed between rounds or whether the parts meet the drawing.
Texturing too early. Texture applied before the dimensions are approved may need redoing after a steel change.
Leaving payment for changes vague. Agree before the first trial who pays for toolmaker errors and who pays for design changes, and how many trial rounds are included.
Approving on looks alone. Measure the critical dimensions and fit the part to its mating parts; a part can look perfect and still warp out of tolerance.

Next steps

  2. Write the trial sample list, reports and number of included trial rounds into your tooling order.
  3. Agree in writing who pays for each kind of tool modification.

FAQ

What is the difference between T0, T1 and T2 samples?

They label successive trial rounds of a new mould. Many toolmakers call the first shots T0 and the first samples sent to the customer T1, with T2 and later numbers after each round of changes. Others start at T1, so ask your toolmaker what each label means.

How many tool trials should I expect?

It depends on the part, the tool and how settled the design was when steel was cut. Plan for at least one round of modifications, agree how many rounds the quote includes, and allow a further trial if the tool is textured after the dimensions are approved.

Who pays for tool modifications?

It is whatever you agree. A common arrangement is that the toolmaker corrects its own errors and brings the tool into line with the agreed drawing, while the customer pays for design changes. Put the split in writing before the first trial.

What is a limit sample?

A part kept to show the worst acceptable version of a cosmetic feature, such as the largest acceptable gate mark. Production parts are compared with it, so cosmetic decisions do not depend on memory or opinion.

Can I approve samples without visiting the toolmaker?

Yes, with planning. Ask for photos in agreed lighting, video of the tool running and parts being assembled, and samples by courier for anything you need to handle or measure. A local third-party inspector can also witness a trial and measure parts for you.
