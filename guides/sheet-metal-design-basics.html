Sheet metal design basics: bends, holes, reliefs, materials and tolerances

The essentials of designing parts for laser cutting and press-brake bending: how the process works, the rules of thumb that keep parts accurate and cheap, material and joining choices, and what to agree with your fabricator.

Quick answer

Design sheet-metal parts around the bending process: consistent bend radii, enough distance between holes and bends, bend reliefs, realistic flanges and tolerances.

Sheet-metal parts are cut flat (usually by laser or punch) and then folded on a press brake. Most problems come from features too close to bends, flanges too short to bend, missing reliefs, and tolerances tighter than bending can hold. The values below are common starting points that depend on material, thickness and the fabricator's tooling, so share your CAD with the fabricator early and use their bend radii and allowances.

How sheet-metal parts are made

  1. Cutting: the flat pattern is cut from sheet by laser, punching or waterjet. No part-specific tooling is needed for laser cutting, which suits prototypes and moderate volumes.
  2. Forming: bends are made on a press brake with standard punches and dies; the available tools set the practical bend radii and minimum flanges.
  3. Joining and hardware: welding, rivets, screws, or self-clinching fasteners pressed into the sheet.
  4. Finishing: for example powder coating or plating for steel, anodising for aluminium, or brushed and passivated stainless steel.

At high volumes, dedicated press tools can replace laser cutting and press-brake bending, trading tooling cost for lower part cost, the same trade-off described in the Manufacturing and cost hub.

Design rules of thumb

Feature Typical starting point Why it matters
Inside bend radius Commonly around the material thickness; use one radius throughout the part Too tight a radius can crack the outside of the bend; one radius means one set of tools
Flange length Several times the material thickness, as set by the fabricator's die A flange too short cannot be gripped and bent accurately
Holes near bends Keep holes a few material thicknesses plus the bend radius away from the bend Holes too close distort into ovals as the metal stretches
Holes near edges and each other At least about the material thickness or more between edges Thin webs distort or tear when cut or formed
Hole size Generally no smaller than the material thickness Very small holes are hard to cut cleanly, especially in thicker sheet
Bend relief A small notch where a bend meets an edge or another bend Prevents tearing and bulging at corners
Grain direction Bend across the grain where possible, especially for harder aluminium alloys Bending along the grain is more likely to crack
Tolerances Looser across bends than within a flat cut feature Each bend adds variation; dimension critical features from the same face

The flat pattern depends on the bend allowance (often expressed as a K-factor), which varies with material, thickness and tooling. CAD sheet-metal tools calculate it, but use your fabricator's values so the folded part comes out the right size.

Practical rule: send the fabricator a 3D model and a drawing showing only the critical dimensions and tolerances. Let them adjust bend allowances and reliefs to suit their tooling, and ask them to confirm any changes. See tolerances and engineering drawings for what that drawing should show.

Choosing a material

Material Strengths Watch out for
Mild steel Low cost, strong, easy to form and weld Needs a protective finish such as powder coat or plating
Galvanised steel Corrosion protection built in Welding damages the coating; cosmetic finish is limited
Stainless steel Corrosion resistant, good appearance More expensive and harder to form; springs back more
Aluminium Light, corrosion resistant, can be anodised Some alloys crack on tight bends; choose a formable grade

Joining and hardware

  • Self-clinching fasteners (nuts, studs and standoffs pressed into the sheet) give strong threads in thin material without welding.
  • Tabs and slots help locate parts for welding or assembly and reduce the need for fixtures.
  • Welding is strong but adds cost, heat distortion and finishing work; spot welds suit overlapping sheets.
  • Rivets and screws allow disassembly and joining different materials.

Common mistakes

Different bend radii on one part. Each radius may need a different tool set-up.
Holes and slots too close to bends. They distort as the bend forms.
Machining-level tolerances on bent features. Bending cannot hold them economically.
Forgetting the finish thickness. Powder coating adds thickness to holes, threads and mating faces; mask or allow for it, as surface finishes for plastic and metal parts explains.
Designing a box that cannot be folded. Later flanges can collide with earlier ones or the press brake; ask the fabricator to check the bend sequence.

FAQ

What bend radius should I use for sheet metal?

A common starting point is an inside radius around the material thickness, but it depends on the material, thickness and the fabricator's tooling. Use one radius throughout the part and confirm it with your fabricator.

How close can a hole be to a bend?

Keep holes a few material thicknesses plus the bend radius away from the bend so they do not distort. If a feature must be closer, ask the fabricator about cutting it after bending or adding a relief.

What is a bend relief?

A small notch cut where a bend meets an edge or another bend. It lets the metal fold without tearing or bulging at the corner.

What is a K-factor in sheet metal?

A value that describes where the neutral axis sits within the material as it bends, used to calculate the flat pattern. It varies with material, thickness and tooling, so use your fabricator's value.

Is sheet metal cheaper than injection moulding?

At low to moderate volumes it often is, because laser cutting and press-brake bending need no part-specific tooling. At high volumes, moulding or press-tooled sheet metal can be cheaper per part. Compare routes for your volumes.
