Product carbon footprint vs LCA: what each measures and when to use it

The difference between a product carbon footprint and a life cycle assessment, the standards behind each, how boundaries change the result, and when a quick design-stage estimate is enough.

Quick answer

A product carbon footprint measures one impact, greenhouse gas emissions; a life cycle assessment (LCA) looks at many impacts across the product's whole life.

For early design decisions, a simple carbon estimate is usually enough to compare options and find the biggest contributors. A full, standards-based footprint or LCA takes far more data and effort, and is worth it when you need to make public claims, meet a customer's requirements, or compare options where the differences are small or the trade-offs involve impacts other than carbon.

The difference at a glance

Product carbon footprint Life cycle assessment
Measures Greenhouse gas emissions, usually as kg CO₂e Several impact categories, such as climate change, water use, resource depletion and toxicity
Main standards ISO 14067; the GHG Protocol Product Standard; PAS 2050 ISO 14040 and ISO 14044
Effort Lower, especially for a design-stage estimate Higher: more data, more modelling, often external review
Good for Comparing design options on climate impact; finding hotspots Avoiding trade-offs where reducing carbon raises another impact; formal comparisons and claims

Boundaries: what is included

  • Cradle to gate: materials and manufacturing, up to the product leaving the factory.
  • Cradle to grave: adds distribution, use, maintenance and end of life.
  • Functional unit: what is being compared, for example "one year of use" rather than "one product", so products with different lifetimes compare fairly.

Results only compare fairly when the boundaries, functional unit and data sources are the same. For products that use energy, the use phase is often large, so leaving it out can be misleading; see why product lifetime matters more than recycling.

Practical rule: use a quick estimate to steer design, and a formal study to support claims. Don't publish a number from an early estimate as if it were a verified footprint.

When an estimate is enough

  • Comparing two materials, processes or transport options early in design.
  • Finding which parts or life stages dominate, so effort goes where it matters.
  • Setting internal targets and tracking improvements between design versions.

The Carbon Footprint Estimator is built for this kind of design-stage comparison; see estimating carbon footprint during design.

When you need more

  • Public environmental claims must be accurate and supported by evidence; see the CMA's Green Claims Code.
  • Customer or retailer requirements for footprints to a named standard.
  • Close comparisons where differences are within the uncertainty of an estimate.
  • Possible trade-offs beyond carbon, such as water use or toxicity, where an LCA is needed.

Common mistakes

Comparing results with different boundaries. A cradle-to-gate figure cannot be compared with a cradle-to-grave one.
Ignoring the use phase. For products that use energy or consumables, it can outweigh manufacturing.
False precision. Early estimates carry wide uncertainty; present them as ranges.
Making claims from estimates. Public claims need robust evidence.

FAQ

What is the difference between a carbon footprint and an LCA?

A product carbon footprint measures greenhouse gas emissions only, usually in kg CO₂e. A life cycle assessment looks at several environmental impacts, such as climate change, water use and resource depletion, across the product's life.

Which standards apply to product carbon footprints and LCAs?

Product carbon footprints are commonly calculated to ISO 14067, the GHG Protocol Product Standard or PAS 2050. Life cycle assessments follow ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.

What does cradle to gate mean?

A boundary that covers raw materials and manufacturing up to the product leaving the factory. Cradle to grave adds distribution, use and end of life.

Is a design-stage estimate good enough?

For comparing options and finding hotspots during design, usually yes. For public claims, customer requirements or close comparisons, a formal study to a recognised standard is more appropriate.

Can I publish my product's carbon footprint?

Environmental claims must be accurate and supported by evidence. In the UK, the CMA's Green Claims Code sets out what businesses should consider. Early estimates should not be presented as verified figures.
