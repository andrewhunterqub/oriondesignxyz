Product certification basics for hardware start-ups: UKCA, CE, safety and EMC

An overview of how product certification works in the UK for start-ups: which regulations commonly apply, how conformity is assessed and documented, the marking rules for Great Britain and Northern Ireland, and how to plan testing into development. It is general information with official links, not legal advice.

Quick answer

Find out which product regulations apply, design and test to the standards that show compliance, then document it, declare it and mark the product.

Many products sold in the UK, including electrical equipment, anything with a radio, toys and machinery, must meet specific product regulations and carry a conformity marking: UKCA in Great Britain (where CE marking is also recognised for many products) and CE in Northern Ireland, with UKNI in some cases. For most products the manufacturer assesses conformity itself, usually by testing against recognised standards; some higher-risk products need an independent conformity assessment body. Identify the rules at the requirements stage, because they affect the design. This is general information, not legal advice.

Who this applies to

The person who places a product on the market carries most of the responsibility. If you design a product and sell it under your name, you are usually the manufacturer in law, even if a factory makes it for you. Importers and distributors have their own, lighter duties, such as checking the marking and documents are in place and that their name and address appear on the product or its packaging.

Common product regulations in Great Britain

Which regulations apply depends on what the product is and does; one product can fall under several. Common examples:

Regulation Applies to
Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016 Electrical equipment within certain voltage limits (often called the low voltage rules)
Electromagnetic Compatibility Regulations 2016 Electrical and electronic equipment that could cause or be affected by electromagnetic interference
Radio Equipment Regulations 2017 Products that intentionally transmit or receive radio waves, such as Bluetooth, Wi-Fi or cellular devices
RoHS Regulations 2012 Restriction of certain hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment
Toys (Safety) Regulations 2011 Products designed or intended for play by children under 14
General Product Safety Regulations 2005 Consumer products generally, especially where no specific regulation covers a risk, in Great Britain. In Northern Ireland, the EU General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988 has applied since 13 December 2024

Other categories, including machinery, medical devices, pressure equipment, personal protective equipment and construction products, have their own regimes, as do producer-responsibility rules for waste electricals, batteries and packaging (see the Sustainability and product life hub). Connectable consumer products can also be subject to security requirements under the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022; EU cybersecurity requirements for radio equipment may also apply to products sold in the EU or Northern Ireland. Rules change, so check the current GOV.UK guidance for your category.

How conformity is shown

  1. Identify the regulations and essential requirements that apply to the product.
  2. Identify the standards. Designated standards (in Great Britain) and harmonised standards (in the EU) give a presumption of conformity when you meet them. GOV.UK lists designated standards; standards themselves are published by BSI and other standards bodies.
  3. Choose the conformity assessment route. For most electrical, EMC and many radio products the manufacturer can self-assess. Some products, such as certain radio equipment tested without the relevant standards or some toys, need a third-party conformity assessment body.
  4. Design and test. Test against the standards, in-house or at a test laboratory. Laboratories accredited by UKAS (or equivalent bodies abroad) give confidence in the results.
  5. Compile the technical documentation: description, drawings and circuit diagrams, risk assessment, list of standards applied, test reports and the production controls that keep products consistent.
  6. Sign a declaration of conformity listing the product, the regulations and standards, and the manufacturer's details.
  7. Mark and label: conformity marking, manufacturer (and importer) name and address, product identification for traceability, and instructions and safety information in English.
  8. Keep it up to date: design changes, component changes and new versions of standards can all require a review.
Practical rule: use pre-certified radio modules and power supplies where you can. A pre-certified module does not certify your whole product, but it can greatly reduce the radio testing needed and the risk of failure.

UKCA, CE and UKNI

Broadly, products placed on the market in Great Britain use UKCA marking, and CE marking continues to be recognised there for many product types; products in Northern Ireland follow EU rules and use CE marking, with UKNI marking added in some cases. The detail differs by product category and has changed several times, so check GOV.UK's guidance on UKCA, CE and UKNI marking before printing labels. Selling into the EU requires CE marking and, for manufacturers outside the EU, usually an EU-based representative or importer.

Planning certification into development

Development stage Certification work
Requirements List applicable regulations and standards; note design requirements such as creepage distances, enclosure ratings, labelling and battery rules
Engineering prototypes Pre-compliance checks, especially EMC and radio; design reviews against the standards
Design verification Formal testing on production-intent units; fix failures before tooling is final
Pilot production Complete the technical file, sign the declaration, finalise labels and instructions
After launch Control changes, monitor complaints and incidents, keep documentation for the required period

Budget for at least one round of fixes and re-tests: failing a formal EMC or safety test at the first attempt is not unusual, and the cost of a re-test is small next to a late redesign. The Development and prototyping hub places this work in the wider development plan, and how much it costs to develop a hardware product includes compliance in the budget.

Worked example: a small rechargeable Bluetooth device

Illustrative example. A start-up is launching a battery-powered household device with a Bluetooth link to a phone app, sold online in Great Britain and Northern Ireland. Working through the questions above, it identifies:

  • radio equipment rules (it has a Bluetooth radio), which also bring in electrical safety and EMC requirements;
  • RoHS restrictions on hazardous substances in the electronics;
  • battery safety, transport and producer-responsibility requirements for the lithium cell;
  • waste electrical equipment (WEEE) and packaging obligations as a producer;
  • CE marking for Northern Ireland and UKCA (or recognised CE) for Great Britain.

By using a pre-certified Bluetooth module and a certified charger, the team reduces the radio and mains-safety testing on its own product. It books a pre-compliance EMC scan on its engineering prototypes, fixes an emissions problem with a layout change before the circuit board is finalised, then completes formal tests on production-intent units. Electronics in a physical product covers the design decisions, such as antenna placement and cabling, that make this easier.

Common mistakes

Assuming a certified module certifies the product. It helps, but the finished product still has to comply as a whole.
Leaving testing until after tooling. Fixes then mean tool changes and delays.
Relying on a supplier's certificate without checking it. Check that it covers your product, version and standards, and comes from a credible laboratory.
Forgetting labels and instructions. Required markings, addresses, warnings and English instructions are part of compliance.
Treating certification as one-off. Component or design changes can invalidate earlier test results.

Next steps

  1. List the regulations that may apply and check the GOV.UK guidance for each.
  2. Ask a test laboratory for a review of your design and a test plan; book pre-compliance testing early.

FAQ

Does my product need UKCA or CE marking?

If it falls under a product regulation that requires a conformity marking, such as electrical equipment, electromagnetic compatibility, radio equipment, toys or machinery, yes. Great Britain uses UKCA and recognises CE marking for many products; Northern Ireland uses CE marking, with UKNI in some cases. Check GOV.UK guidance for your product category.

Can I certify my own product?

For many products, yes: the manufacturer carries out the conformity assessment, usually by testing to designated or harmonised standards, prepares technical documentation and signs a declaration of conformity. Some higher-risk products, or products not fully covered by standards, need an independent conformity assessment body.

Do I need to use an accredited test laboratory?

It is not always a legal requirement for self-assessed products, but test reports from a laboratory accredited by UKAS or an equivalent body carry more weight with retailers, marketplaces, insurers and regulators, and the laboratory's experience helps avoid mistakes.

If I use a pre-certified Bluetooth or Wi-Fi module, is my product certified?

No. A pre-certified module can greatly reduce the radio testing needed, but the finished product must still meet the radio, EMC and safety requirements as a whole, and you still need the technical file, declaration and marking.

When should I start thinking about certification?

At the requirements stage. Regulations can affect materials, enclosure design, circuit layout, labels and instructions. Pre-compliance testing on engineering prototypes, before tooling is finished, is far cheaper than redesign after a failed formal test.
