Sustainability and product life: where a product's impact comes from and how design reduces it

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about the environmental side of physical products: where impact comes from across the life cycle, which design decisions reduce it most, how to estimate it early, and the UK rules on producer responsibility and environmental claims. Each section links to a deeper guide, a free tool or an official source.

Quick answer

The biggest environmental levers are decided in design: whether the product is needed, how much material it uses, how much energy it consumes, and how long it lasts.

Impact per year of use ≈ (materials + manufacture + distribution + end-of-life impact) ÷ years in service + yearly use-phase impact

For products that do not use energy, materials and manufacture usually dominate, so lighter, simpler and longer-lasting designs help most. For energy-using products, the use phase often dominates, so efficiency matters most. Recycling helps at the end of life but cannot recover the impact of making the product. Make a rough estimate early, while the design can still change, and make only the claims you can back with evidence.

The product life cycle

Every physical product has an impact at each stage of its life. Looking at the whole life cycle stops one improvement simply moving the impact somewhere else: a lighter material that needs replacing twice as often, or a recyclable part that makes the product impossible to repair.

Which stage dominates for your product

The pattern below is a general guide, not a rule; a screening estimate for your own product is the only reliable way to know where to focus.

Product type Stage that often dominates Where to focus first
Passive products: furniture, tools, accessories, housewares Materials and manufacture Material quantity and type, durability, repairability
Energy-using products: appliances, heaters, powered equipment Use (energy over the product's life) Efficiency in typical use, standby power, controls, lifetime
Electronics with short replacement cycles Manufacture, especially electronic components Longevity, upgradeability, battery replacement, repair
Products with consumables (cartridges, filters, refills) Consumables over the product's life Consumable design, refill and return systems
Light, bulky or air-freighted goods Distribution can become significant Packaging volume, flat-packing, shipping mode

Design levers that reduce impact

Lever What it means in practice
Question the need Could an existing product, a service or a smaller product meet the need? See the most sustainable product is the one you never make.
Use less material Right-size walls and sections, remove parts, avoid over-engineering for rare loads
Choose materials deliberately Prefer lower-impact and recycled materials where they meet the requirement; avoid mixing materials that cannot be separated
Design for durability Test against real use, protect the parts that wear, avoid fragile features that end the product's life early
Design for repair Screws instead of glue, accessible wear parts and batteries, spare parts and repair information (see design for repair and disassembly)
Reduce energy in use Efficient components, low standby power, sensible defaults and controls
Design for end of life Easy disassembly, marked plastics, fewer material types, take-back where practical
Right-size packaging Less volume and material, recyclable packaging, protection matched to the real shipping route
Practical rule: many sustainability improvements also cut cost: less material, fewer parts, smaller packaging and fewer returns. Look for those first; they are the easiest to justify.

Why lifetime matters so much

The impact of making a product is spent once, however long it lasts. Spreading it over more years of use is often the most effective improvement available.

Illustrative figures only. Product A has a manufacturing footprint of 20 kg CO₂e and typically lasts 3 years: about 6.7 kg CO₂e per year of use. Product B uses more robust materials and a replaceable battery, with a footprint of 26 kg CO₂e, but typically lasts 8 years: about 3.3 kg CO₂e per year. The "heavier" product has roughly half the impact per year of service.

Why product lifetime matters more than recycling explains this in depth, and the Product Lifecycle Explorer compares lifetime, repair and end-of-life options for your product.

Estimating impact: footprints and LCA

A screening estimate uses typical emission factors for materials, processes and transport to find the biggest contributors quickly. It is ideal during design, when the aim is to compare options rather than publish a number. A full life cycle assessment (LCA) follows a recognised method such as ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, covers several impact categories rather than carbon alone, and is needed if you want to publish robust figures or claims. The GHG Protocol Product Standard is a widely used method for product carbon footprints.

The Carbon Footprint Estimator gives a screening estimate, and estimating product carbon footprint during design explains how to use one well.

Producer responsibility, regulation and claims

Depending on the product, UK producers and importers can have legal responsibilities for the product's end of life, its packaging and its energy performance. These are overviews to check against; take advice for your specific product.

  • Electrical and electronic equipment: WEEE producer and distributor responsibilities.
  • Batteries: waste batteries producer responsibility.
  • Packaging: extended producer responsibility for packaging.
  • Energy-related products: ecodesign and energy labelling requirements.
  • Environmental claims: the Competition and Markets Authority's Green Claims Code sets out how to make claims that are truthful, clear and substantiated.

Common mistakes

Optimising the visible thing instead of the big thing. Recycled packaging matters less than energy in use for an appliance, or lifetime for a durable product.
Making the product harder to repair to make it greener. Glued, sealed designs can be lighter but shorten the product's life.
Estimating impact after the design is fixed. A footprint that can no longer change the design is a report, not a design tool.
Claims without evidence. "Eco-friendly" and "sustainable" without substantiation risk misleading customers and breaching consumer law.
Ignoring producer responsibility costs. Compliance schemes and fees for electrical products, batteries and packaging belong in the cost model.

FAQ

What is the most effective way to make a product more sustainable?

It depends on where the product's impact comes from. For products that do not use energy, using less material and making the product last longer usually help most. For energy-using products, efficiency in use often matters most. A quick screening estimate shows which applies before you start optimising.

Is recycled material always the better choice?

Often, but not always. Recycled material usually has a lower footprint, but only helps if it meets the requirement: a recycled part that fails early can cause more impact than it saves. Check strength, durability and availability, and avoid mixing materials in ways that block recycling later.

What is the difference between a carbon footprint and a life cycle assessment?

A carbon footprint estimates greenhouse gas emissions only. A life cycle assessment follows a standard method such as ISO 14040 and 14044 and covers several environmental impacts across the product's life. A screening footprint is ideal during design; a full LCA is needed for robust published figures.

Do I have legal responsibilities for my product's end of life?

Possibly. In the UK, producers and importers of electrical and electronic equipment, batteries and packaging can have producer responsibility obligations (some schemes apply only above size or tonnage thresholds), and energy-related products can face ecodesign requirements. Check the official GOV.UK guidance for your product and take advice if unsure.

How can I make environmental claims about my product?

Only make claims that are truthful, specific and backed by evidence you can share, as set out in the Competition and Markets Authority's Green Claims Code. Avoid vague terms such as eco-friendly unless you can substantiate exactly what they mean.
