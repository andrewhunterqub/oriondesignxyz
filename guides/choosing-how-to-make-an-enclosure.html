Choosing how to make an enclosure: matching the route to your volume and design

The questions that decide how a product's housing should be made, the eight common routes from a modified off-the-shelf box to an injection-moulded case, how they compare, and how to move from one to the next as your volumes grow.

Quick answer

Choose the enclosure route by how many you will make, how sure you are of that number, and how settled the design is; then check it against appearance, sealing, heat and strength.

For a handful of units, modify an off-the-shelf box or 3D print or machine your own. For small to medium batches, aluminium extrusion with end caps, sheet metal or CNC machining often fit, with vacuum casting for moulded-looking plastic parts. Injection moulding and die casting give the lowest cost per part at steady volumes, but need expensive tools and a stable design. Many products move through two or three of these routes, so design with the next one in mind and compare real quotes before you commit to tooling.

The questions that decide it

Answer each of these briefly before asking for quotes. The first usually settles most of the decision; the rest rule routes in or out.

  • Volume, and how certain it is. How many in the first year and over the product's life? A forecast backed by orders can justify tooling; a hopeful one usually cannot.
  • Appearance. Does it need its own shape, colour and texture, or is a neat, plain box acceptable?
  • Sealing. Will it get wet or dusty? Some routes leave few joints to seal; others leave many. Sealing and waterproofing an enclosure explains why this is a design-stage decision.
  • Heat. Must the case carry heat away? Metal spreads heat far better than plastic; see managing heat in a product enclosure.
  • Strength and drops. Will it be dropped, stood on or bolted to a wall? See designing for drops and impacts.
  • Size. Small housings suit moulding and machining; large, flat-sided cabinets suit sheet metal.
  • Time to market. Tooled routes take longer to the first part; an untooled route can supply trial units meanwhile.
  • Budget for tooling. Tooling is paid before you sell anything, alongside the first batch of stock.
  • Whether the design will change. If trials, certification or a new circuit board could still change the shape, avoid locking it into steel.

The routes

Each route has one job it does well and one main trade-off; the linked guides cover the detail.

Off-the-shelf enclosures, modified by machining

Catalogue enclosures in plastic or die-cast aluminium, often with gasketed lids, have holes and cut-outs machined for your connectors, then a label or printed panel added. This fits prototypes, pilot batches and products where a plain box is acceptable, with no tooling. The trade-off is a generic look and someone else's dimensions. A sealing claim for the empty box does not cover the holes you add, so test the finished unit. Keep machined features simple, as the CNC machining design rules describe.

3D printed

Printing needs no tooling and handles almost any shape, so it suits a changing design and very small batches. The trade-off is a high cost per part that hardly falls with volume, and properties and finish unlike moulded or machined parts. Choosing a 3D printing process explains which process answers which question.

CNC machined

Machining from solid aluminium or engineering plastic gives precise, strong housings in real materials, and anodised aluminium looks premium. It suits small to medium batches and designs that may still change. The trade-off is cost per part: deep pockets take machine time, and much of the material ends up as chips. See the CNC machining design rules.

Sheet metal

Folded steel or aluminium suits larger box-shaped housings, cabinets and covers. Laser cutting and press-brake bending need no part-specific tooling, which suits one-offs to moderate batches; at high volumes, dedicated press tools can lower the cost per part. The trade-off is shape: flat panels of even thickness, with folded corners that need welding or a gasketed lid if the product must be sealed. Sheet metal design basics covers bends, holes and reliefs.

Aluminium extrusion with end caps

A profile cut to length, machined and closed with end caps. Standard profiles need no tooling, and a custom die typically costs far less than a mould or casting die. It gives a tough, heat-spreading metal case with two joints to seal. The trade-off is a straight-sided tube, with anything that changes along the length machined afterwards. Aluminium extrusions for product enclosures covers profile design and end caps.

Vacuum casting

Silicone moulds taken from a master produce tens of parts in resins that imitate production plastics, for samples, trials and pilot batches of a housing that will later be moulded. The trade-off is that the materials are not the moulding grade and moulds wear out, so the cost per part stays well above moulding. From 3D-printed prototype to moulded part describes it as a bridge while the tool is made.

Die cast

Molten aluminium or zinc forced into a steel die gives detailed, rigid housings with ribs and fins at a low cost per part once the die is paid for. It suits steady volumes of housings that need metal for heat, screening or feel. The trade-off is a high up-front die cost and long lead time, plus machining of sealing faces and coating afterwards. See die casting explained, and choosing metals for a product for the alloys.

Injection moulded

Plastic injected into a machined tool gives a low cost per part at volume and great freedom of shape, with snap fits, textures and colour moulded in. It is the usual route for consumer housings in steady thousands. The trade-off is the tool: expensive, slow to make and costly to change, so the design must be stable and the demand believable. See injection moulding explained and choosing plastic materials.

Moving between routes as volume grows

Few products stay on one route. A common, low-risk path for an enclosure has three stages, each committing only when the evidence supports it:

  1. Printed or machined (or an off-the-shelf box) while the design is changing and you are building prototypes and the first trial units.
  2. Extrusion or sheet metal for pilot batches and early sales, where a modest die or no tooling at all gives real production parts.
  3. Moulded or die cast once the design has stopped changing and demand is steady enough to repay the tool.

Not every product needs the last stage; an extruded or sheet-metal housing can serve for the product's whole life.

Design so the next change is easier:

  • Keep the circuit board outline, mounting points and connector positions the same across routes, so the electronics do not change when the housing does.
  • Add draft and even walls early where you expect to mould later; from 3D-printed prototype to moulded part lists the changes a printed part usually needs.
  • Choose a sealing method that can carry across, such as a compressed gasket at the lid or end caps.

To decide when to take the next step, put real quotes for two routes into the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser. It compares a low-volume route against a tooled one, which you can set to injection moulding or die casting, and shows where the tool pays back. Worked example: a small outdoor sensor

Illustrative example. A start-up is developing a small, battery-powered sensor for outdoor posts. It must survive rain and sun and be opened yearly for a new battery. Volumes are uncertain: a pilot with one customer, then perhaps a wider launch.

  1. Printed prototypes. Early rounds are printed to settle the size, battery door and internal fit. Each round changes the shape, so tooling would be wasted.
  2. Machined off-the-shelf box for the pilot. The team buys a gasketed die-cast aluminium catalogue box, has the sensor port and cable gland holes machined, and fits a breathable vent. Complete units are water-tested, because the box's own claim does not cover the new holes. It looks generic, but the customer is testing function.
  3. Custom extrusion for launch. With the board stable, the team commissions a custom profile with PCB slots, screw ports and a mounting slot, closed by gasketed, machined end caps. It looks like their own product, spreads heat and has two joints to seal.
  4. When to revisit moulding or die casting. If orders become steady and a redesign is due anyway, the team will get quotes for a die-cast or moulded housing and compare them with the extrusion. Plastic would need checks on sunlight and heat; a casting would need its sealing faces machined.

This example gives no prices on purpose. Get real quotes for your own housing and enter them in the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser.

Common mistakes

Tooling on a hopeful forecast. A mould paid for on sales that never come is cash you cannot get back. Use an untooled route until demand is proven.
Choosing on unit cost alone. Tooling, lead time and minimum orders can outweigh a lower part price. Compare total cost at realistic volumes.
Leaving sealing and heat until last. Both depend on the route. A plastic case that later needs to shed heat, or a folded box that must be waterproof, can force a late change.
Designing for the prototype process. A housing that prints well may not mould, cast or extrude. Keep the next route in mind from the first serious prototype.
Assuming an off-the-shelf box is already sealed. Every hole you add is a new leak path. Seal each opening and test the finished unit.

FAQ

What is the cheapest way to make a small number of enclosures?

Usually a modified off-the-shelf box, or a printed or machined housing, because none of them needs tooling. Which is cheapest depends on size, finish and the features you need, so ask for quotes on two of them.

Should my enclosure be plastic or metal?

Plastic is lighter, usually cheaper at volume and allows almost any shape and colour. Metal spreads heat, screens electronics, resists impact and feels solid. Start from what the housing must do, then choose the material and route together.

When should I move to injection moulding or die casting?

When the design has stopped changing and demand is steady enough for the tool to pay for itself. Compare real quotes for your current route and the tooled one in the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

Can I start with one route and change later?

Yes, and most products do. Keep the circuit board, mounting points and connector positions the same across routes, and design early parts with the later process in mind, so the change affects the housing and not everything inside it.

Is an extruded enclosure a halfway house or a final answer?

It can be either. For products with a box-like shape, modest volumes or a need for a metal case, an extrusion with end caps can be the right route for the product's whole life. Move on only if the shape, look or cost at volume calls for it.
