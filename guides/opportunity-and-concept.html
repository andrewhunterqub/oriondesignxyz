Opportunity and concept: how to test a product idea and choose what to build

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about the earliest stage of a physical product: proving there is a real problem, checking what already exists, writing requirements that can be tested and choosing the right concept. Each section links to a deeper guide or a free tool.

Quick answer

Prove the problem before you design the product, then choose the concept against written requirements.

The opportunity stage is about evidence, not engineering. Confirm that a specific group of people has a problem they care about, understand what they do about it today, check what already exists (including patents), and turn what you learn into measurable requirements. Only then generate several concepts and choose between them with a structured comparison. This is the cheapest stage to change your mind in, so use it.

Start with the problem, not the product

Most product ideas arrive as a solution: a gadget, a mechanism, a better version of something. The questions that decide whether it is worth developing are about the problem behind it:

  • Who exactly has the problem? A named group you can find and talk to, not "everyone".
  • How often, and how badly? Frequent, painful problems justify a purchase; occasional mild annoyances rarely do.
  • What do they do today? The current workaround is your real competitor, even if it is a cardboard box and some tape.
  • Who pays? In many markets the user, the buyer and the budget holder are different people.
  • Should it exist at all? A physical product uses materials and energy for its whole life. The most sustainable product is the one you never make explains how to test that honestly.

The Product Purpose Framework walks through these questions and scores how well the idea is defined.

Gathering evidence: the evidence ladder

Evidence comes in strengths. What people say in a friendly conversation is weak; what they do, and especially what they pay, is strong. Climb the ladder only as far as the next decision needs: a weekend of interviews may be enough before spending on a sketch model, but not before ordering tooling.

In problem conversations, ask about past behaviour rather than future intentions: "tell me about the last time this happened" is far more reliable than "would you buy this?". Avoid describing your idea until late in the conversation, so you hear about their problem rather than their politeness. How to validate a product idea covers interviews, smoke tests and pre-orders in more detail.

What already exists: competitors, patents and designs

Before investing in development, spend a few hours on what already exists. You are looking for direct competitors, adjacent products that solve the same problem differently, and protected rights that might limit what you can make.

Search Where to look What it tells you
Products on sale Retailers, marketplaces, trade catalogues, crowdfunding platforms Price points, features, reviews and complaints you could answer
Patents The UK patent search and the European Patent Office's Espacenet database Prior ideas, active protection you may need to avoid, and expired patents you may be free to use
Registered designs The UK registered design search Protected appearances you should not copy
Failed attempts Discontinued products, closed campaigns, forum threads Why earlier versions did not work, and whether that has changed

A search like this is a sense check, not legal clearance. If a patent looks close to your product, take advice from a registered patent attorney before committing significant money. How to protect product IP explains the options for protecting your own idea.

Turning evidence into requirements

Requirements are the bridge between what you learned and what the design team builds. Good requirements are specific, measurable and traceable to evidence; vague requirements cannot be tested and quietly grow cost.

Vague Measurable
Lightweight Under 250 g including battery
Long battery life At least 5 days of typical use (defined as 20 readings a day) between charges
Durable Survives repeated drops from 1 m onto concrete without loss of function
Easy to use A first-time user completes setup in under 3 minutes without instructions
Affordable Retail price at or below the target set by the route-to-market model

The figures above are examples of the form, not recommendations. Mark each requirement as essential or desirable, note the evidence behind it, and keep the list short enough that the team can remember it. It will become the basis of your test plan in the development and prototyping stage.

Generating and choosing concepts

Generate several genuinely different concepts before choosing: different working principles, architectures or ways of using the product, not three styling variations of the first idea. Quick sketches, cardboard models and simple rigs are enough at this point.

Then compare them against the requirements in a structured way. A Pugh matrix scores each concept against a reference design, criterion by criterion, which makes trade-offs visible and stops the loudest opinion from winning by default. Often the best outcome is a hybrid that combines the strengths of two concepts.

Practical rule: if one concept wins on every criterion, check the criteria. Real choices involve trade-offs, and a matrix with no trade-offs usually means the criteria were written around a favourite.

How engineers choose between concepts explains the method, and the Pugh Concept Selector runs it for your own concepts.

A first commercial sense check

Before development money is committed, do a rough check that the numbers could work. You need three figures, even as ranges: the price customers would pay, the margins taken by the route to market (retailers, distributors, marketplaces), and therefore the most the product can cost to make. If that target unit cost looks impossible for anything like the product you have in mind, it is far better to find out now.

Target COGS by Route to Market works out the cost ceiling from a price, and the Hardware Business Case Builder brings price, cost, volume and investment together. Building a hardware business case explains what a credible case contains.

Common mistakes

Asking friends whether they like the idea. Politeness is not demand. Ask strangers about their past behaviour instead.
Falling in love with the first concept. Without alternatives there is nothing to compare, and the first idea is rarely the best.
Skipping the search for what exists. Discovering a close competitor or patent after tooling is expensive and avoidable.
Writing requirements that cannot be tested. "Premium", "robust" and "simple" mean different things to everyone until they are measured.
Leaving the numbers until the end. A rough price-and-cost check takes an afternoon and can save a year.

FAQ

How do I know if my product idea is worth pursuing?

Look for evidence that a specific group of people has the problem, cares enough to act on it today, and would pay for a better answer. Conversations about past behaviour, observed workarounds and, above all, small commitments such as sign-ups, letters of intent or pre-orders are much stronger evidence than people saying they like the idea.

Should I check for patents before developing a product?

Yes, a basic search is worth doing early. Use the UK patent search and Espacenet to look for close prior ideas and active protection. A search is a sense check rather than legal clearance, so take advice from a registered patent attorney if anything looks close.

What should a product requirements document contain?

Who the product is for and the problem it solves, the essential and desirable requirements written as measurable targets, constraints such as target cost, size and regulation, and the evidence behind each requirement. Keep it short and update it as you learn.

How many concepts should I develop before choosing one?

Enough to represent genuinely different approaches: commonly three to five. They only need to be developed far enough to compare against the requirements, usually sketches, simple models or rigs.

When should I think about cost and price?

At the opportunity stage, as a rough range. Working back from a realistic retail price through the route-to-market margins gives a target unit cost. If that looks impossible for the product you have in mind, change the idea before spending on development.
