Estimating assembly time during design.

Assembly cost is often locked in long before the manufacturing line exists. Part count, fastening logic, awkward handling, and repeated checks all leave early signals if you know how to read them.

Topic Design for assembly
Audience Engineers and product teams
Tool Assembly Time Estimator
Use it for Architecture comparison

Quick answer

To estimate assembly time during design, count the parts, joins, reorientations and checks in each concept, give each a typical time, and add them up.

Assembly time ≈ (parts × handling and insertion time) + (joins × fastening time) + (reorientations × turn time) + (checks × inspection time)

Multiply the total in seconds by a fully loaded labour rate per second to get labour cost per unit. The result is a comparison tool, not a forecast: it shows which concept is likely to be quicker and cheaper to build, and which parts and joins are driving the time, long before a production line is planned.

Early-stage assembly estimates are imperfect, but they are still highly useful. The goal is not to predict final takt time to the second. The goal is to expose which concept directions are likely to become labour-heavy and which ones are quietly removing future cost.

Best use of the estimate: compare concepts, not absolutes. If one architecture removes joins, handling steps, or reorientation, that signal is more valuable than pretending the estimate is exact.

Where the time usually comes from

In manual or semi-manual assembly, recurring time often accumulates from mundane actions rather than dramatic ones: picking up a part, turning the assembly, aligning a fastener, checking that a feature has seated correctly, or pausing because the next step is not obvious.

Assembly driver What it looks like in the design Why it matters
Part count More individual handled items Each extra part creates handling, orientation, and error potential
Handling difficulty Small, flexible, slippery, sharp or tangling parts; parts that look symmetrical but are not Awkward parts slow the pick and raise the chance of fitting them the wrong way round
Insertion difficulty Tight fits with no lead-in, parts that must be held while something else is fitted, obstructed access or poor visibility Insertion that needs care or a second hand is slower and less repeatable
Join count More screws, clips, adhesives, or repeated fastening steps Joining effort scales directly with labour time
Reorientation The build must be turned repeatedly during the process Operator movement adds non-value time and often increases mistake risk
Inspection burden Frequent checks to confirm fit or function QA time is recurring cost and often indicates design ambiguity

Typical times to use at concept stage

You do not need a stopwatch study to start. Use rough, consistent allowances for each action, and apply the same allowances to every concept you compare. The ranges below are broad, hedged starting points for manual bench assembly of small products; real times depend on the part, the operator, the fixtures and the batch size.

Action Rough allowance What pushes it up
Pick and place an easy part A few seconds Small or nested parts, tweezers, holding down while fitting
Snap fit or push fit A few seconds No lead-in, high insertion force, uncertain engagement
Driving a screw Often several seconds or more Hand starting, mixed screw types, poor access, torque checks
Adhesive or tape Several seconds plus any cure or hold time Surface preparation, careful alignment, waiting before the next step
Turning the assembly over A few seconds Loose parts that must be held in place, heavy or awkward assemblies
Visual or functional check A few seconds to much longer Hidden features, test equipment, recording results

If you have timed a similar product, use those figures instead. The Assembly Time Estimator applies consistent allowances so you can compare concepts quickly.

The classic method: design for assembly

Structured design for assembly (DFA) was formalised by Geoffrey Boothroyd and Peter Dewhurst, whose method estimates handling and insertion time for each part from its features and compares the design against a theoretical minimum number of parts. You do not need the full method to use its core idea. For each part, ask three questions:

  1. Does the part move relative to the parts it joins during normal use?
  2. Must it be a different material, or isolated from the others, for a real functional reason?
  3. Must it be separate so that other parts can be assembled, removed or serviced?

If the answer to all three is no, the part is a candidate for combining with a neighbour. Fasteners, brackets, spacers and covers often fail this test. Treat the result as a prompt, not a rule: a separate part can still be the right choice for cost, tooling, repair or supply reasons.

A few seconds lost in one unit rarely look alarming. A few seconds repeated across every unit, every batch, and every operator become a design decision with financial consequences.

Worked example

Illustrative figures only. Imagine two concepts that create similar user value. Concept A uses 12 parts, 8 screwed joins, 3 reorientations and 2 checks. Concept B uses 9 parts and 5 joins, removes one awkward reorientation, and needs only 1 check because its parts can only fit one way.

Using assumed allowances (lower than the Assembly Time Estimator's defaults of 7 to 18 seconds per part and 6 seconds per reorientation) of 4 seconds to handle and insert each part, 8 seconds per screw, 4 seconds per reorientation and 5 seconds per check:

  • Concept A: (12 × 4) + (8 × 8) + (3 × 4) + (2 × 5) = 48 + 64 + 12 + 10 = 134 seconds.
  • Concept B: (9 × 4) + (5 × 8) + (2 × 4) + (1 × 5) = 36 + 40 + 8 + 5 = 89 seconds.

With an assumed fully loaded labour rate of £30 an hour, Concept A costs about £1.12 of labour per unit (134 ÷ 3,600 × £30) and Concept B about £0.74 (89 ÷ 3,600 × £30). The 45-second saving is worth about £0.38 a unit, or roughly £3,750 over 10,000 units, before counting fewer parts to buy, stock and inspect. The absolute numbers will be wrong in detail; the direction, and the fact that screws account for nearly half of Concept A's time, are the useful findings.

Why this matters before manufacturing engineering starts

  • Assembly cost compounds over pilot runs, launch batches, and production scale-up.
  • Complex assembly often hides reliability and quality risk as well as labour cost.
  • Many assembly burdens are locked in by architecture decisions long before detailed production planning exists.
  • Every part removed also removes a drawing, a supplier line, a stock item and an inspection point, so the saving usually reaches beyond labour. For a product already in production, reducing the cost of an existing product shows how to weigh that saving against tool changes and retesting.

Five design-for-assembly questions to ask in review

  1. Can two parts become one without damaging serviceability?
  2. Can alignment features become more self-locating?
  3. Can the join count be reduced without compromising strength or repair? (Joining methods compared sets out the trade-offs.)
  4. Can the build be completed in fewer orientations?
  5. Does the design force extra checking because the correct state is not obvious?

Common mistakes

Treating the estimate as a quote. Concept-stage figures compare options. Your manufacturer will set real times from their own process, fixtures and labour rates.
Merging parts at any cost. Combining parts can create a complex, expensive moulding or make repair impossible. Check the tooling cost and the service plan before merging.
Counting parts but not joins. Two designs with the same part count can differ greatly in time if one uses screws and the other uses snap fits or locating features.
Ignoring mistake-proofing. A part that can be fitted the wrong way round adds checking time and rework. Make asymmetric parts clearly asymmetric, or fully symmetrical.
Using different allowances for different concepts. The comparison is only fair if each action is valued the same way in every option.
Forgetting cables, labels and packing. Routing wires, fitting labels and packing the product are real steps and are often missing from early estimates.

Use the estimate as a design conversation starter

Look for repeated actions. Repetition is where labour hides. One difficult step repeated eight times is usually more important than one obviously awkward step.
Challenge false precision. The point is not to claim that assembly will take exactly 4.6 minutes. The point is to know which concept is directionally better.
Link time to architecture. If the estimate changes substantially after a small architecture change, that is a strong design signal worth acting on.

FAQ

How do engineers estimate assembly time before production?

They use early proxies such as part count, join count, handling difficulty, reorientation, and inspection effort to compare concepts before process engineering is locked.

What design changes reduce assembly time most?

Reducing part count, removing unnecessary joins, simplifying orientation, and improving part location usually provide the strongest gains.

Why does assembly time matter so early?

Because recurring seconds become recurring cost across pilot, launch, and scaled production, and those costs are often created by decisions made during architecture and concept development.

How long does it take to assemble a product?

It depends almost entirely on the design. A small product with a handful of snap-fitted parts may take well under a minute by hand; one with many screws, cables and checks can take several minutes or more. Count the parts, joins, reorientations and checks to get a first estimate.

What is design for assembly (DFA)?

Design for assembly is the practice of designing parts and products so they are quick, easy and hard to get wrong when assembled. Its core principles are fewer parts, fewer separate fasteners, parts that are easy to handle and self-locating, and an assembly built from one direction with few reorientations. Boothroyd and Dewhurst developed the classic structured method.
