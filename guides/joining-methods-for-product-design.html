Joining methods: screws, snap fits, adhesives and welding

How the common ways of joining plastic and metal parts compare on strength, whether they can be undone for repair, assembly time, tooling, cost and recycling, and how to choose between them for your product.

Quick answer

Choose a joining method by asking whether the joint must ever come apart, how strong and sealed it must be, and how many products you will build.

Screws and threaded inserts are slower to assemble but can be undone again and again, which suits anything that will be serviced or repaired. Snap fits are quick and need no extra parts, but they add features to the mould and must suit the material. Ultrasonic welding, staking and adhesives make strong, permanent joints, which is right for sealed or tamper-proof products and wrong for products that need repair.

Five questions that decide the joint

Every joint trades assembly speed against ease of undoing it. For each joint, ask:

  1. Must it come apart? For repair, battery replacement, servicing or separating materials at the end of life. If yes, rule out permanent joints there.
  2. What load and environment does it see? Drops, vibration, heat, moisture, and whether it must seal (see sealing and waterproofing an enclosure).
  3. Which materials meet? Two parts in the same plastic can be welded; different plastics, or plastic and metal, usually need a mechanical fixing or an adhesive.
  4. How many will you build? Dedicated equipment and mould features pay back over volume; at low volume, simple fasteners usually win.
  5. Should it be visible? Screw heads and weld lines affect appearance; hidden snap fits do not.

Screws into plastic: bosses, thread-forming screws and inserts

A screw into a plastic part needs a boss, a hollow post moulded into the part. The injection moulding DFM checklist gives the usual starting proportions: outside diameter around twice the hole, a boss wall thinner than the main wall, cored from below and supported by gusset ribs or joined to nearby walls. Solid bosses cause sink marks and voids; unsupported bosses crack when screws are driven.

  • Thread-forming screws for plastics form their own thread in a plain moulded hole, with no extra parts or operations. The hole size matters more than the boss size, so follow the screw maker's recommendation for your plastic. The thread wears each time the screw is removed, so they suit joints opened occasionally.
  • Threaded inserts, usually brass, give a machine thread that survives repeated assembly. Heat-set inserts are pressed in with a heated tip that melts the plastic into their knurls; ultrasonic inserts use vibration instead; moulded-in inserts are placed in the tool before each shot, adding handling to every cycle.

Inserts add a part, an operation and cost, but suit products that will be opened for service or repair. A metal insert left in a plastic part complicates recycling, so make it removable or accept that the plastic may be downgraded.

Machine screws in metal

In machined or cast metal, a tapped hole takes a standard machine screw that can be undone many times; allow enough thread engagement for the load, and a thread-locking compound where vibration could loosen it. Sheet metal is usually too thin to tap, so self-clinching nuts, studs and standoffs pressed into the sheet give strong threads without welding.

Screws are reliable, repairable and join different materials. Their costs are the parts and the time: driving a screw is one of the slower steps in a manual build, as the assembly time guide explains.

Snap fits

A snap fit is a flexible feature moulded into one part that deflects as the parts are pushed together and springs back behind a catch on the other. It needs no screws, tools or extra parts, which makes it one of the quickest joints to assemble. Cantilever snap fits, a hook on a short flexible beam, are the usual choice for lids and battery doors. Annular snap fits, a continuous bead around a round part as on a pen cap, are strong and can help to seal, but need more force to assemble.

Design for the material's strain. As the beam deflects, the plastic at its root is stretched. Every plastic has a limit on the strain it can take without cracking or permanently bending, and tough plastics such as polypropylene and nylon tolerate much more than brittle or glass-filled grades. Longer or tapered beams, smaller deflections and a generous root radius all lower the strain; check the allowable value on the datasheet, and see choosing plastic materials for how plastics differ. Plastics creep under constant load, so a closed snap fit should be relaxed, not left bent. To check a hook's root strain and its assembly and separation forces, try the Snap-fit Calculator.

One-way or releasable. A square retaining face is effectively permanent: the catch must break to open. An angled face, or a slot where a flat tool can press the hook back, makes it releasable. For anything that may be repaired, design releasable snap fits and test them through repeated opening.

Tooling. A snap hook is an undercut, and undercuts need side actions or lifters that add cost and maintenance. As the DFM checklist notes, a hook can often be moulded straight-pull by putting a hole in the wall behind it, so a core passes through and forms its underside. Agree snap-fit details with your moulder before the tool is cut. For the strain calculation, angles and living hinges, see designing snap fits and living hinges.

Heat and ultrasonic staking

Staking fixes a part with a hole, such as a circuit board or metal bracket, onto a plastic post. The post's tip is melted and reformed into a head, like a rivet, with a heated tool or ultrasonic vibration. It needs no fasteners and joins different materials, but removing the part means cutting off the heads, so keep it away from batteries and other parts that wear out.

Ultrasonic welding of plastics

Ultrasonic welding presses two plastic parts together and vibrates them at high frequency until the interface melts and fuses. The joint is designed into the moulding, usually with a small ridge called an energy director, and the plastics must be the same or compatible. Amorphous plastics such as ABS and polycarbonate generally weld more easily than semi-crystalline ones such as polypropylene and nylon.

The weld cycle is short and the joint can be strong and sealed, with a clean exterior. The costs are a welding machine, a horn and fixture made for the part, and joint features that must be right in the mould. The product cannot be opened for repair, though a housing welded from one plastic may still recycle as one material.

Adhesives and tapes

Adhesives spread load, join almost any pair of materials and can seal. Their weak point is the process: bond strength depends on surface preparation, removing grease and mould release and sometimes abrading or priming. Low-surface-energy plastics such as polypropylene and polyethylene are hard to bond without special adhesives or treatment.

Many adhesives need a cure time with the parts held or fixtured, and that wait is assembly cost. Double-sided tapes give handling strength straight away, and some are designed to release when stretched so a part can be removed. Most structural adhesives are in practice permanent: a glued housing or battery is a common barrier to repair, as the design for repair and disassembly guide explains, and adhesive on mixed materials makes them harder to separate for recycling.

Welding, brazing, rivets and clinching for metal

  • Welding fuses the parent metal. It is strong and can seal, but adds heat distortion, finishing work and labour, damages coatings such as galvanising, and is permanent.
  • Brazing uses a filler metal that melts below the parts' melting point. It distorts less and can join different metals, but is permanent.
  • Rivets, including blind rivets fitted from one side, are quick, need no heat and join different materials. They can be drilled out for repair.
  • Clinching presses overlapping sheets into an interlocking button. It needs no consumables or heat, so it suits pre-coated sheet, but it is permanent.

Joints between the same metal do not usually stop it being recycled as scrap; the bigger end-of-life concern is plastic, foam or adhesive permanently attached to the metal.

Worked example: closing a small plastic enclosure

Illustrative example. A handheld sensor has a moulded ABS base and lid, a circuit board and a replaceable battery. The team compares closing the lid with four screws into heat-set inserts, snap fits around the edge, or an ultrasonic weld.

Option Serviceability Assembly time Tooling Appearance
Screws and heat-set inserts Best: opens with a screwdriver, many times Slowest: four screws plus inserts Bosses; insertion equipment Screw heads visible unless covered
Releasable snap fits Good if designed for repeated opening Quickest: one push Snap features in the mould Clean, with a release slot
Ultrasonic weld None Short machine cycle Welder, horn and fixture Cleanest; can seal

Using the Assembly Time Estimator's own defaults of 14 seconds per join and £28 an hour, four screws add 56 seconds, about £0.44 of labour per unit (56 ÷ 3,600 × £28), before fitting the inserts. The assembly time guide puts a snap fit at a few seconds. These are defaults, not measured times; time a prototype build to replace them.

Welding is ruled out because the battery must be replaceable. The team chooses releasable snap fits plus two screws into inserts near the battery, so the lid stays secure after a drop, survives many openings and needs half the screw time. A sealed product with no wear parts could reasonably choose the weld.

Common mistakes

Welding or gluing shut a product that needs repair. A permanent joint around a battery or other wear part turns a repair into a replacement.
Snap fits in brittle materials. A beam that is fine in polypropylene can crack in a brittle or glass-filled grade. Check the material's allowable strain before the tool is cut.
Screws straight into soft plastic without a boss designed for them. Wrong hole sizes and solid or unsupported bosses strip, crack or sink. Size the hole to the screw maker's recommendation.
Adhesive with no surface preparation. Mould release, grease and low-surface-energy plastics stop adhesives bonding, and the failure often appears only in the field.

Decision rules

  • Use screws, ideally into inserts, wherever the joint must be opened for repair or service.
  • Use releasable snap fits where speed matters and the material is tough enough.
  • Use welding, staking or adhesives only for joints that never need opening, or where sealing or tamper resistance is required.
  • Avoid permanently joining different materials where they will need separating at the end of life.
  • Count the joins in each concept and estimate their assembly time.

FAQ

Are snap fits strong enough for a product enclosure?

Often, yes, if they are designed for the material and the loads, with enough engagement and a relaxed state once closed. Many enclosures combine snap fits with one or two screws where drop loads or tamper resistance matter.

Should I use thread-forming screws or threaded inserts in plastic?

Thread-forming screws are cheaper and fine for joints opened rarely. Threaded inserts cost more and add an operation, but give a durable machine thread for products that will be opened repeatedly for service or repair.

Can ultrasonically welded parts be repaired?

Not without damage. The joint fuses the two parts, so opening it means cutting or breaking the housing. Use it for sealed products with no parts that need replacing.

Why did my glued joint fail?

The most common causes are poor surface preparation, such as mould release or grease left on the parts, a plastic that is hard to bond such as polypropylene, or moving the parts before the adhesive had cured.

Which joining method is best for recycling?

Joints that can be undone, such as screws and releasable snap fits, make it easiest to separate materials. Permanent joints between the same material matter less for recycling than adhesives or inserts that bind different materials together.
