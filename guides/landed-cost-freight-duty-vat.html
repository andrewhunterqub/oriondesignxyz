Landed cost explained: freight, duty and import VAT for physical products

What it really costs to get a product from a factory to your warehouse: the parts of landed cost, how UK import duty and import VAT are worked out, and an illustrative calculation from factory price to landed cost per unit.

Quick answer

Landed cost is everything it costs to get a product from the factory to your warehouse, ready to sell, not just the price on the supplier's quote.

Landed cost = goods + packaging + freight + insurance + import duty + clearance and port charges + inland delivery (+ import VAT if you cannot reclaim it)

For goods made overseas these extras can add a substantial share on top of the factory price. Duty is charged on the customs value, which in the UK usually includes freight and insurance to the UK border, at a rate set by the product's commodity code. Import VAT is charged on top but can normally be reclaimed by VAT-registered businesses. Build landed cost into the unit cost from the first quote, and check duty rates on the official UK Trade Tariff. This is general information, not tax or customs advice.

The parts of landed cost

Cost What it covers Notes
Goods The supplier's price on the agreed delivery terms (Incoterms) An EXW price leaves more for you to pay than a FOB or DDP price
Packaging Retail packaging, inner cartons, shipping cartons, pallets Sometimes quoted separately from the product
Freight Sea, air, rail, road or courier, plus origin charges Sea is usually cheapest per unit but slow; air and courier are fast and expensive
Insurance Cover for loss or damage in transit Check whose responsibility it is under the Incoterms used
Import duty Customs duty at the rate for the product's commodity code May be reduced or zero under a trade agreement if the goods meet the rules of origin
Import VAT VAT on imported goods, normally at the standard rate Usually reclaimable by VAT-registered businesses; it still affects cash unless accounted for on the VAT return
Clearance and port charges Customs broker fees, terminal handling, documentation Often several separate small invoices; easy to forget
Inland delivery From port or airport to your warehouse or fulfilment centre Can be significant for small shipments
Other Pre-shipment inspection, currency exchange costs, financing the stock in transit Not always in the unit cost, but real

How import duty is worked out

  1. Find the commodity code. Every product has a code that sets its duty rate. See GOV.UK's guidance on finding commodity codes.
  2. Look up the rate for that code and the country of origin on the UK Trade Tariff.
  3. Work out the customs value. Usually the price paid plus the cost of transport and insurance to the UK border. GOV.UK explains how to value imports.
  4. Check for preferences. Goods from a country with a UK trade agreement may pay less or no duty if they meet the rules of origin.
  5. Duty = customs value × duty rate. Import VAT is then charged on the customs value plus duty and certain other costs.

VAT-registered businesses can usually account for import VAT on their VAT return rather than paying it at the border; see GOV.UK on accounting for import VAT on your VAT return. Different arrangements apply to some goods moving into Northern Ireland, so check current GOV.UK guidance if you sell there. A customs broker or freight forwarder can confirm codes and costs for your shipments.

Worked example: from factory price to landed cost

Illustrative figures only. A start-up imports 1,000 units of a small product at £6.00 each FOB (the supplier pays to load the goods onto the ship). The goods share a container (a "less than container load" shipment). The duty rate is assumed at 4 per cent; the real rate depends on the commodity code and origin.

Step Calculation Amount
Goods (FOB) 1,000 × £6.00 £6,000.00
Sea freight and insurance Quoted by the forwarder £760.00
Customs value Goods + freight + insurance £6,760.00
Import duty 4% × £6,760 £270.40
Clearance and UK delivery Broker fee + haulage £300.00
Landed cost (excluding reclaimable VAT) Total ÷ 1,000 units £7,330.40, or about £7.33 a unit
Import VAT (reclaimable) 20% × (customs value + duty), simplified £1,406.08

The landed cost is about 22 per cent above the factory price. On a shipment half the size, the fixed charges (clearance, delivery and the minimum freight charge) would weigh more heavily per unit. The VAT line is simplified: in practice some costs after the border, such as onward delivery, can also count towards the value import VAT is charged on. The Product Cost Estimator includes freight and duty in the unit cost.

Practical rule: compare suppliers on landed cost, not factory price. A cheaper quote on EXW terms from further away can cost more on arrival than a dearer quote delivered to your door.

Common mistakes

Using the factory price in the business case. Margins calculated on the quote, not the landed cost, are too high.
Guessing the duty rate. Rates differ widely between commodity codes; look the code up or ask a broker.
Forgetting the small charges. Port handling, documentation and broker fees add up, especially on small shipments.
Ignoring cash timing. Freight, duty and VAT can be due before the goods are sold; plan them in the cash flow.
Comparing quotes on different Incoterms. Put every quote on the same delivered basis before comparing; comparing manufacturing quotes shows how, with tooling and cash included.

FAQ

What is landed cost?

The total cost of getting a product from the supplier to your warehouse ready to sell: the goods, packaging, freight, insurance, import duty, customs and port charges and inland delivery, plus import VAT if you cannot reclaim it. It is the figure to use in margins and pricing, not the factory price.

How is UK import duty calculated?

Find the commodity code for the product, look up the duty rate for that code and the country of origin on the UK Trade Tariff, and apply it to the customs value, which is usually the price paid plus transport and insurance to the UK border. Trade agreements can reduce the rate if the goods meet the rules of origin.

Do I have to pay VAT on imported goods?

Import VAT is normally charged, but VAT-registered businesses can usually reclaim it and can often account for it on their VAT return rather than paying at the border. It still matters for cash planning, and non-registered businesses bear it as a cost.

How much does freight add to the cost of a product?

It depends on size, weight, distance, mode and shipment size. Sea freight is usually cheapest per unit but slow; air and courier are much faster and more expensive. Fixed charges make small shipments relatively expensive, so get forwarder quotes for your expected shipment sizes.

What is the difference between FOB and landed cost?

A FOB price covers the goods loaded onto the ship at the origin port. Landed cost adds everything after that: sea freight, insurance, duty, customs and port charges, and delivery to your warehouse.
