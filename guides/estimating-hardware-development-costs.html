How much does it cost to develop a hardware product?

Founders often ask what it costs to develop a hardware product as if there is one number. There is not. The useful question is which cost blocks are likely to matter for this product, at this stage, on this route to launch.

Quick answer

Pre-launch cost is usually a stack, not a single project fee.

Pre-launch requirement = engineering + prototypes + tooling + compliance + inventory + launch work + contingency

To estimate hardware development cost, price each block separately for your product and route to market, then add a contingency, commonly 15 to 25 per cent and more for novel technology. Teams usually under-budget because they remember the exciting parts and forget the expensive supporting work around them.

A simple mechanical accessory and a connected, battery-powered device can differ in development cost by an order of magnitude. The number that matters is not an industry average. It is your own stack, built from the decisions your product actually requires, with the uncertain parts clearly marked.

The six blocks most teams should expect

  1. Design and engineering: concept development, CAD, iteration, testing support, and supplier-ready detail.
  2. Prototype builds: multiple prototype rounds, fixtures, sample tools, and validation parts.
  3. Tooling and setup: mould tools, jigs, fixtures, or production setup costs.
  4. Compliance: test labs, standards work, documentation, and re-test risk.
  5. First inventory: stock, packaging, inserts, and pre-launch working capital.
  6. Launch work: imagery, packaging, copy, early commercial assets, and market-entry effort.

Contingency then sits on top of the whole stack, not inside one block.

What pushes development cost up or down

Cost driver Tends to raise cost Tends to lower cost
Electronics Custom PCBs, radios, batteries, firmware and an app No electronics, or proven off-the-shelf modules
Enclosure and parts Many moulded parts, tight tolerances, cosmetic finishes Few parts, standard materials, forgiving tolerances
Regulation Medical, child-facing, food-contact, mains-powered or wireless products Low-risk categories with well-understood standards
Novelty New mechanisms or technology with unknown failure modes Familiar architecture adapted to a new use
Volume and route Retail launch needing large first stock and retail-ready packaging Small direct launch with bridge production

Worked example: a small connected consumer device

Illustrative figures only. A team is developing a battery-powered household device with a two-part plastic enclosure, a custom PCB using a pre-certified Bluetooth module, and a simple companion app supplied by a partner. They plan three prototype rounds and a first production batch of 1,000 units.

Cost block Estimate Main assumption
Design and engineering GBP45,000 Industrial design, mechanical and electronics engineering to production release
Prototype builds GBP14,000 Three rounds, from looks-like and works-like models to near-production samples
Tooling and setup GBP38,000 Injection mould tools for enclosure parts plus test and assembly fixtures
Compliance GBP16,000 Safety, EMC and radio testing for UKCA and CE marking, plus one re-test allowance
First inventory GBP26,000 1,000 units at GBP24 unit cost, plus packaging and inbound freight
Launch work GBP9,000 Photography, packaging artwork, product page and early marketing
Subtotal GBP148,000 All six blocks
Contingency at 20% GBP29,600 Moderate novelty, first product for this team
Pre-launch requirement GBP177,600 Before any sales income

Notice that design and engineering is only about 30 per cent of the subtotal. Founders who budget only for the design fee are often surprised by the other 70 per cent. Note too that the estimate covers pre-launch spend only. Monthly overhead and the cash needed to fund the second stock order belong in the cash flow model.

Practical rule: treat the first estimate as a range. If the low and high ends are far apart, the gap usually points to one or two decisions, such as the number of moulded parts or the certification route, that are worth settling early.

How much contingency to hold

Contingency is the acknowledgement that physical product development creates surprises: a failed test, a tool modification, a component going out of stock, an extra prototype round. A common working range is 15 to 25 per cent of the subtotal. Use the lower end for a simple product built on a proven architecture, and the upper end, or more, for new technology, regulated categories, or a team building its first product. Contingency should be released deliberately as risks close, not spent by default.

Common budgeting mistakes

Prototype count is usually underestimated. The first build rarely answers every important question. Budget for at least two or three rounds.
Compliance arrives later than expected. Teams often budget the first test but not the cost of fixing and re-testing.
First inventory is not just product cost. Packaging, labels, inserts, and freight decisions often land here too.
Tooling changes are not free. Modifications after first samples are normal. Allow for them rather than assuming the first shots will be right.
Contingency is not optional. Leaving it out does not remove the risk. It only moves the surprise to a point where it is harder to fund.

How to use the estimate well

  • separate pre-launch cost from recurring monthly overhead
  • keep the cost blocks visible instead of collapsing them too early
  • connect the total to runway and cashflow, not just fundraising ambition
  • stage the spend so that the largest commitments, usually tooling and first inventory, come after the riskiest questions are answered

FAQ

How much does it cost to develop a hardware product?

It depends heavily on complexity, regulation and launch volume. A simple non-electronic product can reach launch for a modest budget, while a connected or regulated device can cost several times more. The reliable way to answer it for your product is to estimate each cost block separately and add contingency.

How much contingency should a hardware budget include?

A common working range is 15 to 25 per cent of the estimated cost blocks, added on top. Use more for new technology, regulated products or a first-time team, and less for a simple product on a proven architecture.

How many prototype rounds should I budget for?

Most products need at least two or three rounds, moving from proof of concept to looks-like and works-like models and then near-production samples. Each round should answer a specific question. More complex or safety-critical products often need more.

Is tooling a development cost or a production cost?

For budgeting, treat it as an upfront pre-launch cost because it has to be paid before production starts, often in stages. In the business case it is part of the investment the product must recover, alongside design and certification.

What hardware costs do founders most often forget?

The usual gaps are extra prototype rounds, compliance re-testing, tooling modifications, packaging, inbound freight and duty on first stock, and launch assets. Contingency is also frequently left out entirely.
