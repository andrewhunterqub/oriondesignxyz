Crowdfunding a hardware product: funding a first run and delivering it

A practical guide to crowdfunding a physical product: what it is good for, what to have in place before launch, how to set a goal that covers delivering every reward, and how to manage cash, manufacturing and backers after the campaign.

Quick answer

Crowdfunding can fund a first production run and prove demand, but only if the goal covers the real cost of making and delivering every reward.

Launch with a design that is largely finished, quotes from manufacturers, and an audience already waiting. Set the goal from the full cost of fulfilment, including tooling, unit cost, packaging, shipping, platform and payment fees, VAT where it applies, and contingency, not from what seems achievable. Plan for delays, communicate openly with backers, and remember that a successful campaign is the start of manufacturing, not the end of development.

What crowdfunding is good for

  • Funding tooling and a first batch with customer money rather than equity.
  • Testing demand with real payments, which is stronger evidence than interest or surveys.
  • Building an audience and gathering feedback before retail.

It is less suited to products that are still early in development, very expensive to make in small numbers, or hard to ship.

Before you launch

Ready? What it means
Design A working, near-final prototype; the main technical risks resolved
Costs Quotes for tooling, parts, assembly, packaging and freight at your likely quantities
Compliance Known safety and certification requirements, and time and budget to meet them; see product certification basics
Audience An email list or community ready to back on day one, since early momentum matters
Fulfilment A plan for packing, shipping to each country, customs and returns
IP Protection decided before public disclosure; see design registration vs patents

Setting the goal and reward prices

Work out what you need to deliver every reward, then set the goal and prices so the campaign still works if it only just succeeds:

  • Tooling and set-up costs, which do not depend on the number of backers.
  • Unit cost of each reward at the quantity you expect, including packaging.
  • Shipping to backers, which can vary a lot by country and is often underestimated.
  • Platform and payment fees, taken from the money raised; check each platform's current fees.
  • VAT and other taxes that may apply to pledges for rewards; take advice from an accountant.
  • Certification, marketing, and contingency for delays and cost increases.

The Crowdfunding Planner works through these and shows what each backer leaves after costs.

Practical rule: if the campaign only works at the stretch goal, the base goal is too low. Make sure you can deliver at exactly the minimum.

All-or-nothing or flexible funding

Some platforms only collect pledges if the goal is reached; others let you keep what is raised either way. All-or-nothing protects you from being committed to deliver without enough money; flexible funding can leave you with obligations you cannot afford. Check each platform's current rules.

After the campaign

  • Cash arrives once, but costs continue for months; plan it with the Cashflow Projection Tool.
  • Lock the design before tooling; changes after launch are expensive.
  • Update backers regularly, especially with bad news. Silence damages trust more than delays.
  • Plan quality checks before shipping; see quality control and first-article inspection.

Common mistakes

Launching too early. Unresolved technical problems become delays that backers have paid for.
Underestimating shipping. International delivery, customs and damaged parcels can wipe out the margin.
Too many reward options. Each colour, size or bundle adds stock and complexity.
No audience at launch. Campaigns rarely find their audience from the platform alone.

FAQ

Is crowdfunding a good way to fund a hardware product?

It can fund tooling and a first batch with customer money while proving demand, if the design is nearly finished, costs are quoted, and you have an audience ready at launch. It suits early-stage ideas poorly.

How should I set a crowdfunding goal?

From the full cost of delivering every reward at the minimum: tooling, unit and packaging costs, shipping, platform and payment fees, taxes that apply, certification, marketing and contingency. Make sure you can deliver at exactly the goal.

When should I launch a crowdfunding campaign?

When you have a working, near-final prototype, manufacturing quotes, a plan for compliance and fulfilment, IP decisions made, and an audience ready to back on the first day.

Why are crowdfunded hardware products often late?

Common causes include launching before technical risks are resolved, underestimated tooling and certification time, supplier delays and design changes after the campaign. Planning contingency and updating backers openly helps.

Do I need to charge VAT on crowdfunding pledges?

VAT may apply where backers receive a reward in return for their pledge. Rules depend on your circumstances, so take advice from an accountant before setting reward prices.
