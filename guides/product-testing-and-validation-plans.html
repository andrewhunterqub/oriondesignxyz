Product testing and validation plans: proving a product works

How to plan testing for a physical product: the difference between verification and validation, what goes into a test plan, the main types of test, and which tests belong at each stage from early prototypes to production.

Quick answer

Verification checks you built the product right; validation checks you built the right product. A good test plan covers both, linked to your requirements.

Turn each requirement into a test with a method, a sample size and a pass or fail criterion. Plan which tests happen at which prototype stage, from quick engineering checks on early models to formal testing on production-representative units. Include user testing, durability and life tests, transit tests and any certification testing, and record results so decisions can be traced.

Verification and validation

Verification Validation
Question Does the product meet its requirements? Does the product meet users' real needs in real use?
Examples Dimensions, strength, battery life, drop tests, temperature, electrical safety User trials, in-home or field testing, usability sessions
Based on The product requirements document The user, problem and use environment

A product can pass every verification test and still fail validation, if the requirements were wrong. That is why both matter.

What a test plan contains

  • Requirement or question being tested, with its ID.
  • Method: how the test is done, ideally to a named standard or a written procedure.
  • Samples: how many units, made how, at which build stage.
  • Pass criteria: agreed before testing, not after seeing the results.
  • Who and where: in-house, at a supplier or at an accredited test lab.
  • Timing and cost: so tests fit the schedule and budget.

Types of test to consider

Type Examples
Functional Does every feature work as specified?
Performance Capacity, speed, accuracy, battery life
Durability and life Cycle tests on buttons, hinges and latches; wear; fatigue
Environmental Temperature, humidity, water and dust, UV
Mechanical abuse Drops, impacts, crush and misuse
Transit Packaged drop, vibration and compression; see packaging design for shipping
Usability Can target users set up, use, clean and maintain it without help?
Compliance Safety, EMC and other tests for the regulations that apply; see product certification basics

For setting drop heights and orientations and designing the product to pass, see designing a product to survive drops and impacts.

Practical rule: run informal pre-compliance and abuse tests on prototypes before booking formal certification testing. Failing at an accredited lab costs time and money that an early check could have saved.

Test at the right stage

  1. Early prototypes: quick checks of the riskiest functions and user reactions.
  2. Integrated prototypes: fuller functional, performance, durability and usability tests.
  3. Pre-production samples: repeat key tests on units made with production processes, plus formal compliance testing.
  4. Production: ongoing checks; see quality control and first-article inspection.

Common mistakes

Testing without pass criteria. Results become a matter of opinion.
Testing one unit. One pass may be luck; test enough units to be confident, especially for safety.
Testing only in the lab. Real users and real environments reveal problems lab tests miss.
Not retesting after changes. A design or supplier change can undo earlier results.

FAQ

What is the difference between verification and validation?

Verification checks that the product meets its specified requirements. Validation checks that it meets users' real needs in real use. A product can pass verification and still fail validation if the requirements were wrong.

What should a product test plan include?

For each test: the requirement or question, the method, the number and build stage of samples, pass criteria agreed in advance, who will do it and where, and the timing and cost.

When should testing start?

As soon as there is something to test. Early prototypes test the riskiest functions and user reactions; later stages add fuller performance, durability, usability and compliance testing.

What is pre-compliance testing?

Informal testing against the requirements of a regulation or standard before formal certification testing, so problems are found and fixed before paying for accredited lab tests.

How many units should I test?

Enough to be confident the result is not luck, which depends on the test, the risk and the variation between units. Safety-critical tests usually need more units than quick engineering checks.
