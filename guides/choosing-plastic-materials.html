Choosing materials for plastic products: common polymers, trade-offs and how to decide

How to choose a plastic for a physical product: start from the requirements, shortlist from the common polymers, understand grades and additives, and test in the real material before committing to tooling.

Quick answer

Choose a plastic from the product's requirements, not its reputation: load, temperature, chemicals, appearance, regulation, cost and end of life.

For many consumer and enclosure parts, the shortlist is polypropylene (PP), ABS, polycarbonate (PC) or a PC/ABS blend; for wear parts and gears, acetal (POM) or nylon (PA); for soft-touch grips and seals, a thermoplastic elastomer (TPE). Write the requirements down first, shortlist two or three materials, check the datasheets for the specific grade, and test prototypes in the intended material before the tool is cut, because each plastic shrinks differently.

Start with the requirements

Before looking at materials, write down what the part must cope with. These questions eliminate most candidates quickly:

  • Loads: static, impact, fatigue or long-term load (plastics creep under sustained load).
  • Temperature: the highest and lowest temperatures in use, storage and shipping, including inside a hot car or near a heat source.
  • Chemicals and environment: cleaning products, oils, sunscreen, food, outdoor UV and moisture.
  • Appearance: gloss, texture, colour, transparency, scratch resistance.
  • Assembly: snap fits, living hinges, screws, ultrasonic welding, bonding, painting or printing.
  • Regulation: flammability ratings for electrical enclosures, food contact, toys, medical use.
  • Cost and supply: price per kilogram, availability of the grade, and whether recycled grades exist.
  • End of life: whether the material can be identified, separated and recycled.

Common plastics compared

Relative comparisons for typical unfilled grades. Properties vary widely between grades and suppliers, so use this to shortlist, then check the datasheet of the actual grade.

Grades, fillers and additives

Each plastic comes in many grades. Glass or mineral fillers increase stiffness and heat resistance but make parts more brittle and the tool wear faster. Additives give UV stabilisation, flame retardancy, colour, impact modification or antistatic properties. For electrical products, flammability ratings (often to the UL 94 scale, such as V-0) may be required by the product's safety standard. Always specify the grade, not just the polymer family, on drawings and purchase orders.

Practical rule: choose the material before the tool is designed. Plastics shrink by different amounts as they cool, and the tool is cut oversize to suit one specific material. Changing material later can mean changing the tool.

Worked example: choosing a material for a handheld device housing

Illustrative example. A rechargeable handheld device needs a two-part housing. Requirements: survive a 1 m drop, stay rigid at up to 60 °C in a parked car, resist hand cream and common cleaning wipes, take a smooth painted-look finish, and meet the flammability rating required by its safety standard.

Candidate Assessment
ABS Good finish and low cost, but marginal on heat and chemical resistance for this use
Polycarbonate Excellent toughness and heat resistance; risk of stress cracking with some chemicals; harder to get the finish
PC/ABS Balances toughness, heat and finish; flame-retardant grades are widely available. Shortlisted, with chemical tests against the actual creams and wipes
Polypropylene Tough and chemical resistant, but too flexible for a rigid housing and harder to finish

The team chooses a flame-retardant PC/ABS grade, makes prototype housings from it (vacuum casting resins only approximate it, so a few machined or bridge-moulded parts are worth having), and tests drop, heat and chemical resistance before the production tool is cut.

Recycling and end of life

Mark parts with their material identification code so they can be sorted later; the codes used on products and packaging include 1 (PET), 2 (HDPE), 3 (PVC), 4 (LDPE), 5 (PP), 6 (PS) and 7 (other). Avoid permanently joining different plastics where you can, use one material family across parts where possible, and consider recycled grades where they meet the requirement. In practice, recycling of durable products is far less established than packaging, so durability and repairability usually matter more; see the Sustainability and product life hub.

Common mistakes

Choosing a material because a similar product uses it. The other product may have different loads, chemicals or cost targets.
Specifying the family, not the grade. "Nylon" or "ABS" covers materials with very different properties.
Testing only in printed prototypes. 3D-printed and cast parts behave differently from moulded parts in the same nominal material.
Forgetting chemical exposure. Sun cream, cleaning products and oils crack some otherwise suitable plastics.
Ignoring moisture. Nylon changes size and stiffness as it absorbs water; several plastics must be dried before moulding.

FAQ

What is the best plastic for a product enclosure?

There is no single best choice. ABS is a common starting point for indoor enclosures thanks to its finish and cost; PC/ABS adds toughness and heat resistance and is widely available in flame-retardant grades; polycarbonate suits very tough or transparent parts; polypropylene suits parts needing chemical resistance or living hinges. Choose from the requirements and test.

What is the difference between ABS and polycarbonate?

ABS is cheaper, easier to mould and finish, and takes paint and plating well, but has lower heat and impact resistance. Polycarbonate is much tougher, has better heat resistance and comes in transparent grades, but costs more, needs drying before moulding and can stress-crack with some chemicals.

Which plastic is best for gears and moving parts?

Acetal (POM) and nylon (PA) are the usual choices. Acetal has low friction and stays dimensionally stable; nylon is tougher but absorbs moisture, which changes its size and stiffness. Both are available in filled and lubricated grades.

Can I change the plastic after the mould is made?

Sometimes, but it is risky. Different plastics shrink by different amounts, so a tool cut for one material may produce parts out of tolerance in another. Choose the material, and ideally the grade, before the tool is designed.

Are recycled plastics suitable for products?

Often, for parts where their properties are adequate and consistent supply is available. Recycled grades can vary more between batches, so test them, agree specifications with the supplier, and avoid them where regulation requires specific virgin grades, such as some food-contact or medical uses.
