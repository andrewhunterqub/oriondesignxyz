How to find and brief a manufacturer, in the UK or overseas

How to find the right manufacturer for a physical product and brief them well: the types of supplier, where to look, what to check, what to send, and how to compare quotes fairly once delivery terms, freight, duty and minimum orders are included.

Quick answer

Find the right type of manufacturer, brief them with a complete pack, and compare quotes on landed cost and risk, not unit price.

Decide whether you need a specialist part supplier, a contract manufacturer that assembles the whole product, or a design-and-make partner. Build a shortlist through referrals, trade directories and trade shows, then send each the same request for quotation: drawings and CAD, quantities, target cost, quality and compliance requirements, packaging and delivery terms. Compare the replies like for like, including tooling, minimum order quantities, payment terms, freight, duty and lead time, and meet or audit the finalists before committing.

Which kind of manufacturer do you need?

Type What they do Suits
Specialist process supplier Makes parts by one process: moulding, machining, sheet metal, casting, PCB assembly Teams that will manage assembly themselves or through another partner
Contract manufacturer Sources parts, assembles, tests and packs the finished product to your design Products with several parts and processes; teams without their own production
Electronics manufacturing services (EMS) Contract manufacturing focused on circuit boards and electronic products Products where the electronics are the main manufacturing challenge
Design-and-make (ODM) partner Develops and manufactures a product, often adapting an existing design Speed and lower development cost, at the price of less control and ownership
Sourcing agent or trading company Finds and manages factories for you, usually overseas Teams without the time or language to manage factories directly; check who owns the relationship

Where to find manufacturers

  • Referrals: other founders, product designers, component distributors and your existing suppliers usually know who is good at what.
  • Trade bodies and directories: national and regional manufacturing associations, industry directories and economic development agencies such as Invest NI in Northern Ireland.
  • Trade shows: subcontract manufacturing and sector shows let you meet many suppliers and see sample work.
  • Online sourcing platforms: useful for overseas suppliers, but verify that you are dealing with the factory, not just a reseller.
  • The products you admire: markings, teardowns and published case studies can point to the suppliers behind similar products.

Shortlisting: what to check

Check What good looks like
Relevant experience Similar size, materials, processes and quality level; examples they can show
Fit with your volumes Your quantities matter to them; minimum order quantities you can afford
Quality system Documented inspection, traceability and corrective action; certification such as ISO 9001 where relevant
Engineering support A design-for-manufacture review with useful suggestions, not just a price
Communication Clear, prompt answers from someone technical; a named contact
Commercial terms Clear tooling ownership, payment terms, lead times and what happens when parts are rejected
Stability A business that will still be there for your second and tenth order

Briefing: the request for quotation

A good brief gets better quotes faster and shows the supplier you are worth taking seriously. Send every supplier the same pack so the replies can be compared:

  • 3D CAD and 2D drawings with materials, finishes, critical dimensions and tolerances
  • quantities: first order, expected annual volume and how it may grow
  • a target price or cost range, if you have one
  • quality requirements: inspection, acceptance criteria, cosmetic standards
  • compliance needs: marking, test reports, material declarations
  • packaging, labelling and delivery requirements, including the delivery terms you want quoted
  • timeline: when you need samples and first production
  • what you want them to propose: process suggestions, design changes, alternative materials

The documents to prepare before talking to a manufacturer covers each item in detail, and how to protect product IP explains what to have in place before you share it.

Practical rule: tell suppliers the truth about volumes. Inflated forecasts get you a price you will not see in practice and a relationship that sours when the orders do not arrive.

Comparing quotes like for like

Quotes arrive in different shapes. Before comparing, put them on the same basis. Delivery terms matter most: the internationally used Incoterms rules (published by the International Chamber of Commerce) define who pays for what. "EXW" (ex works) means you collect from the factory and pay everything after that; "FOB" (free on board) means the supplier pays to get goods onto the ship; "DDP" (delivered duty paid) means the supplier delivers to you with duty paid.

Illustrative figures only. A team receives three quotes for 2,000 units of an assembled product. Freight, duty and inspection costs are planning assumptions; check duty rates for your product code on the UK Trade Tariff. Import VAT is left out because a VAT-registered business can normally reclaim it.

UK supplier Overseas supplier A Overseas supplier B
Quoted price per unit £8.40 delivered £4.90 FOB £4.20 EXW
Freight and insurance per unit Included £0.65 £0.85 (including inland transport to port)
Duty per unit (assumed 4% of price plus freight and insurance) None £0.22 £0.20
Customs clearance and handling per unit None £0.15 £0.15
Pre-shipment inspection per unit None £0.20 £0.20
Landed cost per unit £8.40 £6.12 £5.60
Minimum order and lead time 500 units, 3 weeks 2,000 units, 8 weeks plus 5 to 6 weeks shipping 5,000 units, 10 weeks plus shipping

Supplier B looks cheapest, but its minimum order is two and a half times the planned quantity, so the real cash commitment is far higher. Supplier A saves about £2.28 a unit against the UK quote, or about £4,560 on this order, but ties up cash for roughly two to three months longer and makes design changes slower. A reasonable plan might start with the UK supplier for the first batches, then move volume overseas once the design and demand are stable. The right answer depends on cash, volumes and risk, not just the per-unit figure. Getting and comparing manufacturing quotes adds tooling ownership, payment timing and the cash each quote needs.

UK or overseas?

Factor UK and nearby Further overseas
Unit price Often higher Often lower, before freight, duty and inspection
Lead time and stock in transit Shorter; less cash tied up Longer; more stock and cash in the pipeline
Minimum order quantities Often more flexible Often larger
Communication and visits Easier, same time zone and language Harder; needs planning or local support
Design changes and problem solving Faster Slower and more costly to iterate
Supply chain depth Varies by process Very deep for some sectors, such as electronics

Importing brings customs and duty obligations; GOV.UK's guidance on importing goods into the UK is the starting point.

Before you commit

  1. Get samples of their work, and of your own part once tooling exists.
  2. Visit or audit the factory, in person or through a third-party inspection company.
  3. Agree the quality plan: inspection points, acceptance criteria, a signed-off golden sample and first-article inspection.
  4. Put the terms in writing: prices, payment schedule, lead times, tooling ownership, warranty and what happens with rejected parts. Supply agreements with a manufacturer lists what to cover.
  5. Start small: a pilot batch tests the relationship before large orders. Planning a pilot production run covers what to measure and agree in advance.

Red flags

A price far below every other quote. Usually a misunderstanding of the specification, or costs that will appear later.
No questions about your design. Good manufacturers ask; silence often means they have not looked closely.
Reluctance to put tooling ownership in writing. It can leave your tools, and your product, tied to one supplier.
Full payment up front with no samples. Deposits are normal; paying everything before seeing parts is a large risk.
Unclear who actually makes the product. A trading company presenting itself as the factory can hide quality and IP risks.

FAQ

How do I find a manufacturer for my product?

Decide first whether you need a specialist part supplier, a contract manufacturer or a design-and-make partner. Then use referrals from designers and other founders, trade bodies and directories, regional agencies such as Invest NI, trade shows and, for overseas suppliers, sourcing platforms, verifying that you are dealing with the actual factory.

What should I send a manufacturer to get a quote?

CAD and drawings with materials, finishes and critical tolerances, realistic quantities for the first order and per year, quality and compliance requirements, packaging and delivery terms, a timeline, and a target cost if you have one. Send every supplier the same pack so the quotes can be compared.

What do EXW, FOB and DDP mean on a quote?

They are Incoterms rules that define who pays for transport, insurance and duty. EXW (ex works) means you take responsibility from the factory door; FOB (free on board) means the supplier pays to load the goods onto the ship; DDP (delivered duty paid) means the supplier delivers to your premises with duty paid.

Is it cheaper to manufacture overseas?

The unit price is often lower, but freight, duty, inspection, larger minimum orders, longer lead times and slower design changes all add cost or cash requirements. Compare landed cost and the cash tied up, not just the factory price; for early, changing products a nearby supplier can be cheaper overall.

How do I check a manufacturer is reliable?

Ask for examples of similar work and references, review their quality system, request samples, visit or commission a third-party audit, and start with a pilot batch. Make sure tooling ownership, payment terms and rejection procedures are agreed in writing.
