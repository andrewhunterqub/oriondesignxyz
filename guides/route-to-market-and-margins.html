Route to market and margins: how sales channels shape price, cost and cash

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about selling a physical product: how each route to market changes the share of the price you keep, how to work back from a retail price to a target cost, and how margins, payment terms and volume decide whether the product pays its way. Each section links to a deeper guide or a free tool.

Quick answer

Your route to market decides how much of the price you keep, when you are paid, and therefore what the product can cost to make.

Target landed COGS = retail price − VAT − channel share − selling costs − the margin the business needs

Selling direct keeps most of the price but carries the costs of finding customers, fulfilment and returns. Marketplaces take a commission and fees. Retailers commonly keep 30 to 50 per cent of the retail price before VAT and may pay on 30 to 90 day terms. Work out the target cost for the routes you will actually use, before the product is engineered, so the design is built to a cost it can afford.

The main routes to market compared

Many hardware businesses use more than one route, and the mix changes as the business grows. Each route has a different share of the price, a different cost of selling, and a different effect on cash.

Route What you keep Main costs to allow for Cash and stock
Direct to consumer (own website) Most of the price Payment fees, paid marketing, fulfilment, customer service, returns Paid at order; you hold the stock
Marketplace The price less commission and fees Commission, advertising to stay visible, fulfilment fees, returns Paid on a settlement cycle; may need stock in their warehouse
Retailer The wholesale price: commonly 50 to 70 per cent of the net retail price Retail-ready packaging, delivery to their depots, promotions, deductions Credit terms, often 30 to 90 days; larger, earlier stock commitments
Distributor then retailer Less than selling to retailers directly, because both take a share Distributor margin on top of retailer margin; less control of pricing Fewer, larger orders; useful for reaching new countries
Business-to-business or OEM supply A negotiated price, often lower per unit but for larger volumes Longer sales cycles, specifications, quality and contract terms Purchase orders and payment terms; forecasts help plan production
Crowdfunding The pledge less platform and payment fees Campaign marketing, rewards, shipping to backers, delivery risk Money before manufacture, but a public promise to deliver

Where the retail price goes

The clearest way to see a route's effect is to follow one retail price down to the money left for making the product. UK consumer prices normally include VAT (standard rate 20 per cent), which never belongs to the business; then the channel takes its share, selling costs are paid, and the business keeps the margin it needs. What is left is the target landed cost of goods.

Practical rule: set the target cost from the least generous route you expect to use at scale. A product engineered to the direct-sales target can become unprofitable the day a retailer lists it.

Margin, markup and the numbers people mix up

  • Margin is profit as a share of the selling price: (price − cost) ÷ price.
  • Markup is profit as a share of the cost: (price − cost) ÷ cost. A product bought for £50 and sold for £100 has a 50 per cent margin but a 100 per cent markup.
  • Gross margin usually means after the cost of goods only; contribution also takes off the variable selling costs of each sale. Contribution is what actually pays for overheads.
  • Retail price with and without VAT: a £120 price including VAT is £100 to the business before anyone else takes a share.

Retailers, distributors and suppliers do not always use these words the same way, so confirm which one is meant before agreeing a number.

Setting the price

Price is set by customers' alternatives and the value the product delivers, not by cost plus a markup. Cost sets the floor; the market sets the ceiling. A few principles:

  • Anchor on alternatives. What does the customer pay today, in money and effort, to solve the problem another way?
  • Keep prices consistent across routes. Undercutting your retailers on your own website damages the relationship; plan the price so every route works.
  • Leave room for promotions. Retail promotions and marketplace discounts come out of someone's margin, usually yours.
  • Test before committing. Pre-orders or a small batch at the intended price tell you far more than a survey.

Cash timing and stock

Margin is only half of the route decision. A retailer order can be excellent for the brand and still strain cash: you pay the factory before shipping, the stock spends weeks in transit, and the retailer may pay one to three months after delivery. Model when money goes out and comes in, not just how much, with the Cashflow Projection Tool and calculating hardware start-up cash flow.

Volume and break-even

Once the contribution per unit is known for each route, the break-even volume follows: fixed costs (development, tooling, overheads) divided by the blended contribution per unit. If the break-even volume is far beyond what the business can realistically sell, the answer is usually to change the route, the price or the product cost, not to hope for more sales. Common mistakes

Treating the retail price as the factory's budget. VAT, the channel and selling costs can take well over half of it before production is paid for.
Forgetting the costs of selling direct. Direct sales keep more of the price, but advertising, fulfilment and returns can take much of the difference.
Confusing margin and markup. A "50 per cent" that means markup to one party and margin to another moves the target cost a long way: on a £100 price, the cost is £50 at a 50 per cent margin but about £66.67 at a 50 per cent markup.
Ignoring payment terms. Profitable growth through retail can still run out of cash.
Setting the target cost after the design. By then most of the cost is locked in. Set it before engineering starts.

FAQ

How much margin do retailers take on hardware products?

Retailers commonly keep 30 to 50 per cent of the retail price before VAT, depending on the category, the retailer and the support they expect. Promotions, deductions and retail-ready packaging requirements can add to that, so ask for the full terms rather than the headline margin.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a share of the selling price; markup is profit as a share of the cost. A product bought for 50 pounds and sold for 100 pounds has a 50 per cent margin but a 100 per cent markup. Confirm which one is meant before agreeing terms.

Is selling direct always more profitable than selling through retail?

Not necessarily. Direct sales keep more of the price, but paid marketing, fulfilment, customer service and returns can absorb much of the difference. Retail can bring volume and visibility with lower selling cost per unit, but at a lower price to you and with slower payment.

How do I work out the target cost for my product?

Start from the retail price, remove VAT, remove the channel's share, remove the selling costs of that route and the margin the business needs. What is left is the most the product can cost to make and land. Use the least generous route you expect to use at scale.

Should I use a distributor?

A distributor can open markets and retailers you could not reach alone, especially abroad, but takes its own margin on top of the retailer's. It suits products with proven demand and enough margin to share; check the target cost works with both margins included.
