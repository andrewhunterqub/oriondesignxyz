Funding and cash: how much a hardware product needs, and where the money can come from

This is the starting point for everything on Orion Design about paying for a physical product: why the funding requirement is set by the cash low point, how the main funding sources compare, how to stage funding to milestones, and why stock and payment terms can strain even a profitable business. Each section links to a deeper guide, a free tool or an official source.

Quick answer

A hardware business needs enough cash to reach the lowest point of its cash curve, plus contingency, and should raise it in stages tied to risk.

Funding needed ≈ deepest cumulative cash shortfall (development + tooling + first stock + overheads − early sales) + contingency

Development is only part of the requirement: tooling and the first stock order are usually paid for months before the money from selling them arrives. Model the cash month by month to find the low point, then match funding to milestones, using cheaper, less dilutive sources such as grants, pre-orders and tax relief for early, risky work and equity or loans once the product and market are better proven.

How much funding a hardware product needs

The question "how much do we need to raise?" is answered by a cash flow forecast, not a budget. Lay out, month by month, the money going out (design, prototypes, tooling, certification, stock, overheads) and coming in (sales, grants, pre-orders). The deepest point of the cumulative total is the cash low point, and that, plus a contingency, is the minimum the business must have access to.

Notice the second dip at month 18 when the next stock order is paid for. Growth needs cash too: every time sales rise, stock has to be bought before the extra sales are paid for. The Startup Cost Planner builds the pre-launch budget, the Cashflow Projection Tool finds the low point, and calculating hardware start-up cash flow explains the method. Building a hardware financial model shows how unit cost, margin, break-even and cash timing connect into one set of numbers.

Funding sources compared

Each source has a cost, whether in equity given up, interest, time spent applying or obligations to customers. Terms, eligibility and schemes change, so check current details with the official sources linked below before relying on any of them.

Source Fits best when… What it costs you Watch out for
Founders, friends and family The idea is early and unproven Your own money, or relationships if it goes wrong Put terms in writing, even with family
Customer money (pre-orders, deposits, pilots) Demand can be shown before stock is made A promise to deliver, often by a date Delays and refunds; only take what you can deliver
Crowdfunding A consumer product with a clear story and an audience Platform and payment fees, campaign effort, public commitment Underpriced rewards and shipping costs; manufacturing delays in public
Grants and innovation funding Genuinely innovative technical work with a defined project Time to apply and report; often match funding required Competitive, slow, and usually paid in arrears
R&D tax relief The company spends on qualifying research and development Record-keeping and claim preparation Rules change; arrives after the spend; get qualified advice
Start-up and business loans There is a credible plan to repay from trading Interest and repayments, sometimes personal guarantees Repayments start before the product may be profitable
Angel investors There is early evidence and a route to a much larger business Equity, and some influence Valuation and terms; SEIS and EIS reliefs can matter to UK angels
Venture capital The business could grow very large, fast Significant equity and expectations of rapid growth Many hardware businesses do not fit the model; that is not a failure

Matching funding to stages and risk

Raising everything at once is rarely possible and usually expensive: before risks are retired, investors price them in. A staged approach raises enough for the next milestone that materially reduces risk, then raises again on better terms.

Stage Main risk being retired Funding that often fits
Idea and evidence Is there a real problem and market? Founders' time and money; small grants or competitions
Proof of principle and prototypes Does it work, and can it be made? Innovation grants, R&D tax relief, friends and family, early angels
Pre-production and launch Will customers buy at the planned price? Pre-orders, crowdfunding, angels, start-up loans
Growth Can sales scale profitably? Equity rounds, stock and invoice finance, trading profits
Practical rule: tie each round of funding to a milestone a funder can see: a working prototype, a passed test, a signed retailer, a sold-out batch. Milestones are what make the next round cheaper.

Working capital: the cost of stock and slow payment

Working capital is the cash tied up between paying for stock and being paid for it. For hardware it is often larger than founders expect, because factories commonly want a deposit at order and the balance before shipping, minimum order quantities force larger batches, goods spend weeks in transit, and retailers may pay 30 to 90 days after delivery. A profitable order can therefore still require a lot of cash. Plan batch sizes, deposits and payment terms alongside the route to market, as described in the Route to market and margins hub.

What investors and funders look for

Funders want to see that you understand your own numbers: unit cost and margin by route, the cash low point, what the money is for, and which milestone it buys. Equity investors also look at the size of the opportunity and the team. Hardware-specific questions often include the tooling plan, certification route, supplier risk and gross margin. The Investor Metrics and KPIs tool and hardware investor metrics that actually matter explain which figures to prepare, and the Hardware Business Case Builder brings them together.

Support in the UK and Northern Ireland

Starting points for official information (eligibility and terms change, so always check the current details). Funding routes for hardware start-ups in the UK and Northern Ireland explains how to choose and apply for each.

  • Innovate UK: the UK's innovation agency, which runs competitions for innovation funding.
  • Invest NI: Northern Ireland's economic development agency, with support for innovation, R&D and growing businesses.
  • R&D tax relief: HMRC guidance on Corporation Tax relief for research and development.
  • Seed Enterprise Investment Scheme (SEIS): tax reliefs for investors in early-stage companies, which can make angel investment easier to raise.
  • Start Up Loans: government-backed personal loans for starting a business.
  • GOV.UK business finance and support finder: searches grants, loans and support by location and sector.

University spin-outs should also talk to their institution's technology transfer office about proof-of-concept funding and investment routes.

Common mistakes

Raising the development budget instead of the cash low point. Tooling, stock and the months before sales are paid for often need more money than the design work.
Treating grants as quick money. Applications take time, many are competitive, and payment is often in arrears against spend.
Forgetting the second stock order. Success needs cash too: more sales mean more stock bought in advance.
Taking pre-order money without a delivery plan. Customer money is the cheapest funding until delivery slips; then it becomes the most expensive.
No contingency. A failed test, a tool modification or a delayed shipment can each move the low point by months.

FAQ

How much money do I need to launch a hardware product?

Enough to reach the lowest point of your cumulative cash forecast, plus contingency. That includes development, prototypes, tooling, certification, first stock and overheads until sales income catches up, not just the design budget. It varies enormously with the product, so build your own forecast rather than relying on an average.

What funding is available for hardware start-ups in Northern Ireland?

Options include Invest NI support, Innovate UK competitions, R&D tax relief, Start Up Loans, angel investment (where SEIS and EIS reliefs can help) and customer funding such as pre-orders. Availability and terms change, so check the official sources and use the GOV.UK business finance and support finder for current schemes.

Should I use crowdfunding to fund manufacturing?

It can work well for consumer products with a clear story and an existing audience, because it provides money and evidence of demand before stock is made. Price rewards to cover the full landed cost, fees and shipping, and only promise delivery dates your manufacturing plan can meet.

Why do profitable hardware businesses run out of cash?

Because stock is paid for before it is sold, and customers such as retailers may pay weeks or months after delivery. Rapid growth makes this worse, as each larger order needs more cash up front. A month-by-month cash forecast shows the problem before it happens.

When should a hardware start-up raise equity investment?

Usually once there is evidence that reduces risk, such as a working prototype, test results or early sales, so the business can raise on better terms. Equity suits businesses with a route to substantial growth; many good hardware businesses are better funded through customers, grants, loans and profits.
