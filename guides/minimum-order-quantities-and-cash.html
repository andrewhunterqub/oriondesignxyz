Minimum order quantities and cash: planning your first production order

Why suppliers set minimum order quantities, how much cash a first order really needs, how to compare a lower unit price with the stock it creates, and practical ways to negotiate and fund the first run.

Quick answer

A minimum order quantity (MOQ) decides how much cash you must spend before you sell anything, so plan it alongside your sales forecast, not just your unit cost.

Suppliers set MOQs because each production run has set-up costs. Larger orders usually lower the unit price, but tie up cash in stock that may take months to sell. Work out the cash needed for the first order, deposits, tooling, freight and duty, and compare it with realistic sales. Then negotiate: smaller first runs, staged deliveries, shared materials or a higher unit price for a lower quantity are often possible.

Why suppliers set MOQs

  • Set-up costs: changing a mould, a print run or a production line takes time and creates scrap, which must be spread over enough units.
  • Material buying: raw materials, colours and components are often bought in minimum quantities themselves.
  • Capacity: small runs take up line time that could go to larger customers.

Understanding which reason applies tells you what to negotiate.

The cash picture

The first production order is often the biggest single cash outlay in a hardware start-up. Add up:

Cost When it is usually paid
Tooling Often in stages, for example on order and on sample approval
Production deposit On order, commonly a share of the order value
Production balance Before shipment or on delivery, depending on terms
Freight, duty and import VAT On shipping and at import, before you can sell
Packaging, inspection and storage Around production and after delivery

Payment terms vary by supplier and relationship; agree them in writing. Then set the outlay against when sales actually arrive, with the Cashflow Projection Tool. Landed cost per unit comes from the Landed Cost Calculator.

Illustrative figures only. A supplier quotes £6.00 a unit at an MOQ of 3,000 or £7.50 at 1,000. The larger order costs £18,000 against £7,500, before tooling, freight and duty. If realistic sales are about 150 a month, 3,000 units is 20 months of stock: the lower unit price saves £4,500 but ties up £10,500 more cash for well over a year, with the risk of design changes or slow sales. For many start-ups, the smaller order is the safer first step.

Practical rule: compare the order with months of realistic sales, not with the unit price saving. Stock you cannot sell is cash you cannot use.

How to negotiate a lower MOQ

  • Ask what drives the MOQ and offer to solve that: use a standard colour or material the supplier already stocks.
  • Pay more per unit for a smaller first run.
  • Commit to an annual volume delivered in smaller call-off batches.
  • Pay for the set-up separately, so the supplier is covered for a short run.
  • Reduce variants: fewer colours and sizes at launch means larger quantities of each.
  • Choose the right process: some processes suit low volumes better. The Production Strategy Planner and Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser help compare options.

Funding the first order

Pre-orders and crowdfunding can fund production with customer money while also testing demand; see the Crowdfunding Planner. Other options include trade or stock finance, retailer orders with agreed payment terms, and grants or investment; see funding routes in the UK and Northern Ireland.

Common mistakes

Chasing the lowest unit price. A big first order can leave no cash for marketing, which is what sells the stock.
Forgetting the extras. Freight, duty, import VAT, packaging and inspection can add substantially to the order value.
Too many variants at launch. Each colour or size may carry its own minimum.
Ignoring design changes. Early customer feedback often leads to changes; a large first batch locks in the first version.

FAQ

What is a minimum order quantity?

The smallest order a supplier will accept for a part or product, usually because each production run has set-up, material and capacity costs that must be spread over enough units.

Should I order more to get a lower unit price?

Only if you can sell the stock in a reasonable time and still have cash for marketing and running the business. Compare the order with months of realistic sales, not just the unit price saving.

How can I negotiate a lower MOQ?

Find out what drives the minimum, then offer to address it: use stocked materials or colours, accept a higher unit price, pay for set-up separately, reduce variants, or commit to an annual volume delivered in smaller batches.

How much cash do I need for a first production order?

Add tooling, the production deposit and balance, freight, duty, import VAT, packaging, inspection and storage, and set them against when sales arrive. A cash flow projection shows the peak amount you need.

How do start-ups fund their first order?

Common routes include pre-orders and crowdfunding, retailer orders with agreed terms, trade or stock finance, grants and investment. Pre-orders have the advantage of testing demand at the same time.
