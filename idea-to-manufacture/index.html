Idea to manufacture: how a physical product gets made

Developing a physical product follows a recognisable sequence: discover the problem, define what the product must do, choose a concept, prototype, verify the design, industrialise it for production, then launch. Pick the stage you are at to see what happens there, what it typically takes, what goes wrong, and which tools and guides help.

Quick answer

Seven overlapping stages, each ending with evidence that it is safe to move on.

Simple products can reach first production within a year; electromechanical, connected or regulated products often take one to two years or more. Most of the time and money goes into prototyping and industrialisation, and most expensive surprises come from moving to the next stage before the evidence is in. The timescales below are typical ranges for a moderately complex product and vary widely.

1

1. Discover

Is this a problem worth solving, and for whom?

Understand the problem, the people who have it and how they cope today, before any design work. Talk to potential users, watch how they currently solve the problem, glance at competitors and existing patents, and form a rough view of how many people might buy.

Typical duration

Typically 2–8 weeks

What drives cost

Low: mostly time and a few conversations.

Outputs

  • A clear problem statement and target user
  • Evidence from real conversations, not assumptions
  • A first list of must-have requirements
  • A rough sense of market size and price expectations

Common pitfalls

  • Building something before checking anyone needs it
  • Asking leading questions, or only asking friends and family
  • Mistaking polite interest for demand
Ready to move on when: People describe the problem without prompting, and some say what they would pay or switch from to solve it.

2. Define

What exactly must the product do, at what cost?

Turn what you learned into a product requirements document: what the product must do, how well, for whom, at what target cost and volume, through which sales route, and which regulations apply. This is the brief every later decision is checked against.

Typical duration

Typically 2–6 weeks

What drives cost

Low: desk work, plus any specialist advice on regulations.

Outputs

  • Product requirements (functions, performance, size, environment)
  • Target cost (COGS) worked back from the price and sales route
  • Expected volumes for year one and beyond
  • A list of standards and certification that may apply

Common pitfalls

  • Vague requirements ("robust", "easy to use") that cannot be tested
  • No target cost, so the design drifts expensive
  • Discovering certification needs after the design is finished
Ready to move on when: Requirements are specific enough to test against, and the target cost leaves a workable margin for the chosen route.

3. Concept

Which design direction should we develop?

Generate several genuinely different ways of meeting the requirements: sketches, quick models, rough architectures. Compare them against the requirements and target cost, then choose one direction to develop, with the reasons written down.

Typical duration

Typically 3–8 weeks

What drives cost

Low to moderate: design time, quick models and early CAD.

Outputs

  • Several concepts explored, not just the first idea
  • A chosen concept and product architecture
  • A first bottom-up cost estimate
  • The key technical risks to test next

Common pitfalls

  • Falling for the first idea
  • Choosing on appearance alone
  • Not costing concepts until later, when changing direction is expensive
Ready to move on when: One concept clearly fits the requirements best, and its biggest risks are known and testable.

4. Prototype

Does it work, and does it work for people?

Build and test prototypes that each answer specific questions: looks-like models for size and feel, works-like rigs for the mechanism or electronics, then integrated prototypes that combine both. Iterate the design and CAD between rounds.

Typical duration

Typically 2–6 months, over several rounds

What drives cost

Moderate, and rising with each round as parts get closer to production quality.

Outputs

  • Prototypes that each answered a defined question
  • Test results and user feedback
  • Refined CAD and an updated bill of materials
  • A clearer cost estimate and assembly approach

Common pitfalls

  • Prototypes that do not answer a specific question
  • Polishing appearance before the function is proven
  • Assuming 3D-printed parts behave like moulded ones
Ready to move on when: The integrated prototype meets the key requirements, and the remaining changes are refinements rather than redesigns.

5. Verify

Is the design proven, safe and ready to freeze?

Test production-intent parts against the requirements: performance, durability, drops, temperature, user trials, and the pre-compliance or certification testing the product needs. When the evidence is in, freeze the design so tooling can start.

Typical duration

Typically 1–4 months; longer for regulated or electrical products

What drives cost

Moderate to high: test labs, certification and production-intent parts.

Outputs

  • Test reports against each requirement
  • Certification plan or results where needed
  • A frozen design with controlled drawings
  • Updated cost and sustainability estimates

Common pitfalls

  • Leaving certification until after tooling
  • Skipping reliability and drop tests to save time
  • Freezing the design while known problems remain
Ready to move on when: The design passes its key tests, certification has a clear path, and no further changes are expected that would affect tooling.

6. Industrialise

Can it be made reliably, at the right cost and volume?

Prepare the design and supply chain for production: design for manufacture, choosing processes and suppliers, making tooling, approving first-off samples, setting up quality checks, packaging and assembly instructions, then a pilot run.

Typical duration

Tooling commonly takes several weeks to a few months, plus sample iterations; often 2–5 months overall

What drives cost

Usually the largest single block: tooling, first inventory and supplier set-up.

Outputs

  • Production drawings, tolerances and a full bill of materials
  • Tooling made and first samples approved
  • Supplier agreements, quality and inspection plan
  • Packaging and a pilot production run

Common pitfalls

  • Cutting tooling before the design is frozen
  • No inspection plan, so quality problems surface at the customer
  • Minimum order quantities and lead times that strain cash
Ready to move on when: Pilot units meet the specification and the landed unit cost fits the target.

7. Launch

Can we sell, deliver and support it sustainably?

Produce the first stock, set up sales channels and fulfilment, prepare marketing, customer support, returns and warranty, and plan how feedback from early customers will feed into improvements and the next version.

Typical duration

Typically 1–3 months of preparation, overlapping with industrialisation

What drives cost

Inventory, launch marketing and working capital; this is where cash is usually tightest.

Outputs

  • First production stock and packaging
  • Sales channels, pricing and fulfilment in place
  • Support, returns and warranty processes
  • A plan for feedback and the next iteration

Common pitfalls

  • Underestimating the cash tied up in inventory
  • No returns or warranty process
  • Treating launch as the end rather than the start of learning
Ready to move on when: Units are selling at the planned margin, and cash covers the time until revenue arrives.

FAQ

What are the stages of developing a physical product?

A common way to describe it is seven stages: discover the problem, define the requirements, choose a concept, prototype, verify the design, industrialise for production, and launch. The stages overlap in practice, and most products loop back at least once as testing reveals something new.

How long does it take to take a product from idea to manufacture?

It depends heavily on complexity. A simple mechanical product can reach first production within a year; electromechanical, connected or regulated products often take one to two years or more. Prototyping and industrialisation, including tooling, usually take the most time.

How much does it cost to develop a physical product?

Costs vary by an order of magnitude between a simple moulded item and a connected, certified device, so a single average is misleading. Build your own estimate block by block, as explained in how much it costs to develop a hardware product, and use the Startup Cost Planner.

Do I need a prototype before talking to a manufacturer?

Not necessarily for a first conversation, but manufacturers can only quote accurately against a defined design: CAD, drawings, materials, volumes and quality expectations. A tested prototype makes the conversation far more productive. See what to have ready.

Can I skip stages to launch faster?

You can compress them, but skipping usually moves the cost later. Skipping discovery risks building the wrong product; skipping verification risks tooling changes, failed certification or returns. Running stages in parallel where it is safe, and testing the riskiest assumptions first, is usually the faster route overall.
