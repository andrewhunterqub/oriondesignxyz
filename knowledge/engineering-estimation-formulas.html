Engineering estimation formulas.

The formulas behind Orion's free tools and guides in one place, each with its terms, a short worked example, the usual mistake, and the tool that calculates it for you.

Quick answer

Use a formula to frame the decision, then check it against supplier quotes and real project figures.

These formulas are for early estimates: comparing options on the same assumptions and finding which input matters most. Unit cost: COGS and the cost to the business

Direct cost = materials + process + bought-in parts + packaging + labour Labour = build minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate Scrap allowance = direct cost × scrap rate Overhead = (direct cost + scrap) × overhead rate COGS = direct cost + scrap + overhead Unit cost to the business = COGS + (tooling ÷ units the tooling will make) + landed-cost additions per unit

Terms: process is machine rate × cycle time ÷ parts per cycle, plus finishing; the labour rate is fully loaded; overhead is applied after scrap. Tooling sits outside COGS so you can see how it changes with volume.

Illustrative figures from the manufacturing cost guide: £4.80 material, £2.40 process, £5.50 bought-in parts, £4.80 labour (12 minutes at £24 an hour) and £1.20 packaging make £18.70 direct. A 6% scrap allowance (£1.12) gives £19.82 and a 14% overhead (£2.77) gives a COGS of £22.59 (the tool, which does not round between steps, shows £22.60). £12,000 of tooling over 10,000 units adds £1.20 and freight, insurance, clearance and duty add £1.60, so the unit cost is £25.39. At 2,000 units the same tool adds £6.00 and the unit cost becomes £30.19.

Common mistake: pricing from material, process and bought-in parts alone (£12.70 here), which is about half the real figure.

Landed cost

Customs value = goods + packaging + origin charges + freight + insurance Duty = customs value × duty rate Import VAT = (customs value + duty) × VAT rate Landed cost = customs value + duty + clearance + delivery + other (+ import VAT if you cannot reclaim it)

Terms: the duty rate comes from the product's commodity code and country of origin on the UK Trade Tariff; the customs value usually includes transport and insurance to the UK border. The import VAT line is simplified, as some costs after the border can also count towards it.

Illustrative figures from the landed cost guide: 1,000 units at £6.00 FOB is £6,000; freight and insurance of £760 make a customs value of £6,760; duty at an assumed 4% is £270.40; clearance and delivery add £300. Landed cost is £7,330.40, about £7.33 a unit, 22% above the factory price. Import VAT of £1,406.08 is reclaimable by a VAT-registered business.

Common mistake: comparing suppliers on factory price when their quotes use different Incoterms. Calculate it with the Landed Cost Calculator.

Crossover volume: when tooling pays back

Total cost of a route = setup or tooling cost + unit cost × quantity Crossover volume between routes A and B = (setup B − setup A) ÷ (unit cost A − unit cost B) With no setup cost for A: break-even volume = tooling cost ÷ (prototype unit cost − moulded unit cost)

Terms: route A is cheap to start but dear per part (3D printing, CNC, vacuum casting); route B, usually injection moulding, has the higher setup and lower unit cost and is cheaper in total above the crossover. The third line is the form used in the injection mould break-even guide.

Illustrative figures (the Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser defaults): a £6,500 mould making parts at £0.70 against 3D printing at £18 with no setup crosses over at £6,500 ÷ £17.30 ≈ 376 units. But vacuum casting at £950 setup and £4.20 a part only gives way to moulding at (£6,500 − £950) ÷ (£4.20 − £0.70) ≈ 1,586 units.

Common mistake: calculating break-even against the most expensive alternative only. The straight-line model is also a simplification: silicone moulds for vacuum casting wear out after a limited number of parts, as the processes comparison shows, and design changes can mean tool modifications.

Assembly time and labour cost

Assembly time ≈ (parts × handling time) + (joins × fastening time) + (reorientations × turn time) + inspection time Labour cost per unit = total seconds ÷ 3,600 × hourly labour rate

Terms: the Assembly Time Estimator assumes 7, 12 or 18 seconds to handle and insert each part (simple, moderate or complex) and 6 seconds per reorientation; you set the time per fastener and the inspection time. These are planning assumptions, not measured times.

Illustrative figures (the tool's defaults): 12 moderate parts × 12 s = 144 s; 8 fasteners × 14 s = 112 s; 5 reorientations × 6 s = 30 s; plus 20 s of inspection gives 306 seconds, about 5.1 minutes. At £28 an hour that is 306 ÷ 3,600 × £28 ≈ £2.38 of labour per unit. The assembly time guide compares two concepts using its own, lower step times.

Common mistake: comparing concepts with different time assumptions. The absolute figure is rough; the difference between concepts on the same assumptions is the useful part.

Margin, markup and the price for a margin

Margin = (price − cost) ÷ price Markup = (price − cost) ÷ cost Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin) Price needed for a margin m = cost ÷ (1 − m) Shelf price = each price in the chain ÷ (1 − that partner's margin), then × (1 + VAT rate)

Terms: margin is a share of the selling price; markup is a share of the cost. Retailers and distributors usually talk in margin.

Illustrative figures: a product costing £50 that sells for £100 has a 50% margin but a 100% markup, and a 50% markup is only a 33% margin. With the Pricing Ladder defaults, an £8.00 landed cost at a 50% brand margin is £16.00 to the retailer; a 45% retailer margin makes £29.09 before VAT, and 20% VAT makes a shelf price of £34.91, about 4.4 times landed cost.

Common mistake: adding a percentage to cost when the buyer means margin. A "40% markup" leaves far less room than a 40% margin needs. See retail and distributor margins explained.

Target COGS from retail price

Price before VAT = RRP ÷ (1 + VAT rate) Retained price = price before VAT × (1 − channel's share) Target COGS = retained price − hidden selling costs − (retained price × target margin)

Terms: the channel's share is the retailer's margin or the marketplace fee (zero when you sell direct); hidden selling costs are fulfilment, payment fees, returns, marketing and retail support; the target margin is the share of the retained price the business needs to keep.

Illustrative figures from the revenue and margin guide: a £180 RRP is £150 before 20% VAT. Sold direct with £43 of selling costs and a 30% target margin (£45), target COGS is £150 − £43 − £45 = £62. Through a retailer keeping 45%, the retained price is £82.50; less £8.13 of costs and £24.75 of margin, target COGS is £49.62.

Common mistake: working from the RRP without removing VAT and the channel's share first. Check, too, whether a marketplace charges its fee on the price including or excluding VAT: the guide's example charges 15% on the VAT-inclusive £180, while the Target COGS tool applies the fee to the price before VAT, which gives a slightly higher retained price (£127.50 rather than £123.00).

Contribution, break-even and margin of safety

Contribution per unit = net selling price − variable cost per unit Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (round up) Contribution margin ratio = contribution per unit ÷ net selling price Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio Margin of safety = (forecast units − break-even units) ÷ forecast units

Terms: the net selling price is what you receive after VAT and channel deductions; variable cost is everything that rises with each unit (landed COGS, fulfilment, payment fees); fixed costs are what must be recovered regardless of volume, such as development, tooling and certification.

Illustrative figures from the break-even guide: £65,000 of fixed cost, a £49 net price and £22 of variable cost give £27 of contribution. Break-even is 65,000 ÷ 27 = 2,407.4, so 2,408 units, or about £118,000 of revenue; the contribution margin ratio is about 55%. Against a forecast of 3,500 units the margin of safety is about 31%.

Common mistake: using the shelf price. Sold through a retailer keeping 40%, the same product nets £29.40, contribution falls to £7.40 and break-even rises to about 8,784 units. Run your own figures in the Break-even Calculator.

Development budget and contingency

Subtotal = engineering + prototypes + tooling + compliance + first inventory + launch work Contingency = subtotal × contingency rate Pre-launch requirement = subtotal + contingency

Terms: a common working range for contingency is 15 to 25 per cent of the subtotal: more for new technology, regulated products or a first-time team, less for a simple product on a proven architecture. The Startup Cost Planner also shows a suggested funding target 10% above the total, as a buffer for delays.

Illustrative figures from the development cost guide: a subtotal of £148,000 with 20% contingency (£29,600) needs £177,600 before launch.

Common mistake: trimming contingency to make the budget look affordable. It does not remove the risk; release it as risks close instead.

Cash flow, runway and acquisition payback

Closing cash = opening cash + cash received − cash paid Net burn = monthly operating spend − monthly gross profit Runway (months) = cash in the bank ÷ net burn CAC = sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers CAC payback (orders) = CAC ÷ gross profit per order after fulfilment

Terms: cash is counted when it moves, not when a sale is booked, so payment delays and stock bought in advance both matter, and runway should include committed stock orders.

Illustrative figures from the investor metrics guide: £18,000 of marketing brings 600 customers, so CAC is £30; each order leaves £54 after landed COGS and fulfilment, so CAC is recovered within the first order. Net burn of £30,600 against £420,000 in the bank gives about 13.7 months of runway. In the cash flow guide, £27 of contribution a unit means about 352 units a month just to cover £9,500 of overhead.

Common mistake: reading profit as cash. The Investor Metrics tool divides CAC by gross profit per unit, so include fulfilment in unit COGS there to match the guide's stricter definition. Model the months in the Cashflow Projection Tool.

Tolerance stack-up: worst case and RSS

Nominal gap = Σ dimensions that open the gap − Σ dimensions that close it Worst case = nominal ± Σ tolerances RSS (statistical) = nominal ± √(Σ tolerance²)

Terms: each dimension in the chain has a nominal size and a symmetric ± tolerance (convert +0.2/−0 to the mid-point ± 0.1). Worst case assumes every part is at its limit at once; RSS (root sum of squares) assumes independent, random, centred variation.

Illustrative figures (the Tolerance Stack-up Calculator default): a 60.00 ± 0.15 mm opening holds a 59.40 ± 0.15 mm lid, so the nominal gap is 0.60 mm. Worst case is 0.60 ± 0.30, a 0.30 to 0.90 mm gap; RSS is 0.60 ± √(0.15² + 0.15²) ≈ ± 0.21, about 0.39 to 0.81 mm. With four parts at ± 0.10 each, worst case is ± 0.40 but RSS only ± 0.20.

Common mistake: relying on RSS for a short chain, a low volume or a safety-critical fit. Its "most assemblies" prediction holds only when processes are well centred, so many engineers check both. See CNC machining design rules for what tolerances cost.

Practical rule: change one input at a time and watch which one moves the answer most. That input is where a supplier quote, a test or a customer conversation is worth the most.

FAQ

What is the formula for injection mould break-even volume?

Tooling cost ÷ (prototype unit cost − moulded unit cost), when the alternative has no setup cost; otherwise subtract its setup cost from the tooling cost first. A £6,500 tool making £0.70 parts against £18 printed parts breaks even at about 376 units.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a share of the selling price; markup is profit as a share of the cost. £50 cost and a £100 price is a 50% margin but a 100% markup.

How do I work out target COGS from a retail price?

Remove VAT and the channel's share to find the price you receive, then take away hidden selling costs and the margin the business needs. What is left is the most the product can cost to make and land.

Should I use worst-case or RSS tolerance stack-up?

Worst case for safety-critical fits, short chains, low volumes or off-centre processes; RSS for longer chains from stable processes in reasonable volumes. Many engineers check both.

How accurate are these estimating formulas?

Only as good as their inputs. Use them for early comparisons, then replace assumed figures with supplier quotes, test results and real sales data.
