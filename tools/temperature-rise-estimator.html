Quick answer

The component's temperature is the ambient plus its power multiplied by the total thermal resistance of the path out.

Each step from the component to the outside air resists the flow of heat, and the steps add together. The last step, from the outer surface to the air, is often the largest, so a bigger or metal surface usually helps most. Compare the result with your target, then confirm it by test.

How this works

The tool follows the method in simple first estimates: once the product has settled, all the power going in leaves as heat, and the temperature rise is that power multiplied by the thermal resistance of the path. Resistances in a chain add together. Lengths entered in millimetres are converted to metres for the area.

Area (from mm): A = length × width ÷ 1,000,000 Surface to air: R_surface = 1 ÷ (h × A) Internal steps: R_int = R_comp-case + R_pad + R_extra Total: Rθ = R_int + R_surface Rise: ΔT = P × Rθ Component: T_comp = T_ambient + ΔT Allowed rise: ΔT_allowed = T_target − T_ambient Margin: T_target − T_comp Maximum power: P_max = ΔT_allowed ÷ Rθ Area needed: R_allowed = ΔT_allowed ÷ P − R_int A_needed = 1 ÷ (h × R_allowed) Outer surface: T_surface ≈ T_ambient + P × R_surface

Worked example from the guide. A 2 W board in a sealed housing, 40 °C ambient and a 65 °C target. An aluminium lid of 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01 m² with h ≈ 10 W/m²·°C gives 1 ÷ (10 × 0.01) = 10 °C/W. Add the pad and contact surfaces at about 1 °C/W: Rθ ≈ 11 °C/W. ΔT = 2 × 11 = 22 °C, so the component reaches about 40 + 22 = 62 °C, just under the target. These are the tool's default inputs.

The status compares the rise with the rise your target allows (target minus ambient): up to 80% of it passes, 80–100% is marginal, and above 100% is over the target. The guide's example uses 22 of 25 °C (88%), so it shows as marginal: under the target, but with little room. If the internal resistances alone use up the allowed rise, no surface area can meet the target and the tool says so.

Assumptions and limits

  • Steady state. The product has run long enough for every temperature to stop rising. Warm-up takes time, so short bursts of power run cooler than this estimate, but a long burst will reach it.
  • A single path. All the heat is assumed to flow through one chain of resistances. In reality some also leaves through the walls, the base and the cable, so the real temperature should be slightly lower. If another path matters, the estimate becomes pessimistic.
  • The lid is uniform in temperature. The whole surface is treated as being at one temperature. A plastic or thin surface leaves a hot spot over the component and sheds less heat than its area suggests; metal spreads heat far better.
  • Solar gain is not included. Sunshine on the housing can add more heat than the electronics produce. For outdoor products, the result applies to shade only; choose the colour and test in sun.
  • h is a rough figure. The real combined coefficient depends on orientation, size, finish, nearby surfaces and any air movement. Replace the default with better data, or work it out from a test.
  • Touch temperature is a separate check. The outer surface temperature shown is an estimate only. The limits for how hot a surface may get come from your product's applicable safety standard, which you can identify through product certification basics. This tool does not quote them.
  • Confirm by test. Measure a prototype with thermocouples on the hottest parts and the housing above them, at your highest ambient and heaviest realistic load, until the readings stop rising.

How to read your result

  • Pass: the rise uses no more than 80% of what your target allows. Check the outer surface against the touch limits in your safety standard, and confirm by test.
  • Marginal: under the target, but small changes in power, pad thickness or ambient could push it over. Improve the largest resistance first, then test.
  • Over target: the component is likely to run too hot. The maximum power and the surface area needed show how far you are from a working design.

The largest resistance in the chain dominates. If it is the surface to air, add area, ribs or fins, or move to a metal lid; if it is the pad, bridge the gap with a thinner or better pad. FAQ

Where does the default h of 10 W/m²·°C come from?

It is the rough combined convection and radiation figure engineers often use for a surface in still air, as in the guide's worked example. The real value depends on the surface's orientation, size and finish and on any air movement. Once you have a prototype, measure the power and the surface and air temperatures, and use h = P ÷ (A × (T_surface − T_ambient)) in place of the default.

What thermal resistance should I use for the pad?

Take it from the pad maker's datasheet for your thickness, compression and contact area, and allow something for the contact surfaces either side. The 1 °C/W default is the illustrative figure from the guide, not a value for any particular pad.

Why might a measured temperature differ from the estimate?

The estimate assumes one heat path, a lid at an even temperature, steady state and no sunshine. Heat leaking through the walls makes reality slightly cooler; a hot spot on the lid, a dark finish in sun or a higher real power makes it hotter. Use the estimate to choose a direction, then confirm with thermocouples.

Does this tell me whether the outside is safe to touch?

No. It gives a rough outer surface temperature, but the touch limits depend on the material, how long the surface is touched and the type of product, and they come from your product's applicable safety standard. Check the measured surface temperature against that standard.
