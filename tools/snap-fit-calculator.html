Quick answer

A cantilever snap fit works if the strain at the root of the beam stays below what the material can take.

The root strain depends only on the beam's length, its thickness and how far it bends. The material's stiffness then sets how much force that takes, and the hook angles and friction turn that into the force to push the parts together and pull them apart.

How this works

The tool uses the standard formulas from published snap-fit design guidance for a cantilever with a constant rectangular cross-section, treated as a beam fixed at the wall and loaded at the hook. Units are millimetres, newtons and megapascals (N/mm²), so forces come out in newtons.

Strain at the root: ε = 1.5 × t × y ÷ L² Share of permissible strain = ε ÷ ε_perm Deflection force: P = (b × t² ÷ 6) × (E × ε) ÷ L Assembly (mating) force: W = P × (μ + tan α) ÷ (1 − μ × tan α) Separation force: W′ = P × (μ + tan α′) ÷ (1 − μ × tan α′) Largest deflection at ε_perm: y_max = ε_perm × L² ÷ (1.5 × t)

Strain is entered and shown as a percentage, but the formulas use it as a fraction (2% = 0.02). If 1 − μ × tan α′ is zero or less, the formula has no finite answer: friction holds the retention face shut however hard you pull, so the joint is effectively self-locking and will only come apart if the hook is deflected by hand or with a tool. The same applies to the lead-in face: if 1 − μ × tan α is zero or less, the hook will jam instead of assembling.

The status compares the root strain with your permissible strain: up to 80% passes, 80–100% is marginal, and above 100% is too high.

Assumptions and limits

  • The beam has the same rectangular section along its length and is rigidly fixed at the root. The load acts at the hook.
  • Tapering the beam or adding a root radius changes the result. A beam that tapers in thickness towards the hook spreads the strain more evenly and deflects further for the same root strain. A generous root radius lowers the stress concentration that a sharp corner adds; a sharp root can crack well below the calculated strain.
  • The material is treated as linear elastic. Past the linear region, published guidance uses the secant modulus at the working strain, which is lower than the flexural modulus, so the forces here are likely to be on the high side.
  • The mating part is assumed rigid. If it also flexes, the real forces and strain are lower.
  • Permissible strain depends on the material, the grade, temperature, moisture and whether the joint is assembled once or many times. Use your supplier's design data for your conditions.
  • Friction varies with the material pair, surface finish and lubrication, and is often uncertain. Try a range of values.
  • 3D-printed prototypes behave differently. Printed parts are weaker across their layers, use different materials and have different stiffness, so a printed hook can break where a moulded one would not, or survive where it would not. Use prints to check fit and feel, not to prove the strain.
  • This is a first estimate. Confirm the design by testing moulded parts, including repeated assembly if the joint will be opened.

How to read your result

  • Pass: the root strain is comfortably below the permissible strain. Check the forces suit the user: a joint that is too easy may come apart, and one that is too stiff may be hard to assemble by hand.
  • Marginal: close to the limit. Small changes in moulded thickness or material batch could push it over. Lengthen the beam, thin it, or reduce the undercut.
  • Too high: the hook is likely to crack or take a permanent set. The largest deflection allowed at your permissible strain shows how much undercut the beam can manage as it stands.

Strain falls with the square of the beam length, so lengthening the beam is usually the strongest lever. FAQ

What permissible strain should I use for a snap fit?

Use the figure from your material supplier's design data for the exact grade. Suppliers often give a higher limit for a joint assembled once than for one opened and closed many times, and the limit falls with temperature and, for some plastics, moisture. The default in this tool is an example only.

How do I reduce the strain in a snap-fit hook?

Lengthen the beam first, because strain falls with the square of the length. Then make the beam thinner, reduce the undercut depth, taper the beam towards the hook, and add a generous radius at the root.

What does self-locking mean here?

When the retention face is steep enough for the friction on it, pulling on the joint can never push the hook out of the way, however hard you pull. The joint will only open if someone deflects the hook by hand or with a tool, or the hook breaks.

Will a 3D-printed snap fit behave like a moulded one?

No. Printed parts are weaker between layers and are often in a different material, so their strain limit and stiffness differ. Use printed hooks to check fit and feel, then test moulded parts before you rely on the design.
