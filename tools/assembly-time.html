Worked example

A 12-part product with 8 joins, moderate handling, and 20 seconds of QA time often lands near the point where small part-count reductions create meaningful labour savings. Removing two joins and one orientation step can cut recurring assembly effort more than teams expect.

How to use the estimate

This tool is most useful when comparing concepts, not validating final takt time. If one direction uses fewer components, easier location features, or less repeated fastening, the delta in labour usually matters more than the absolute number.

  • Use part count as a proxy for handling events.
  • Treat joins as repeated manual effort that scales directly with labour cost.
  • Use reorientation count to capture awkward fixture or operator movement.
  • Add QA time to reflect inspection and confirmation effort that often gets forgotten.

Method and assumptions

Handling time = part count × seconds per part (7 simple, 12 moderate, 18 complex) Joining time = fasteners × seconds per fastener Reorientation time = reorientations × 6 seconds Total seconds = handling + joining + reorientation + inspection Labour cost per unit = total seconds ÷ 3,600 × hourly labour rate

Assumptions

  • Handling times per part are fixed allowances for manual assembly by a trained operator, chosen by the complexity setting.
  • Each reorientation of the assembly takes about 6 seconds.
  • The labour rate is the fully loaded rate you enter.

Limits

  • It does not include learning curves, fixtures, line balancing, rework or automation.
  • Real times depend on part design, access, tooling and operator skill; time a prototype build to calibrate.

How to read your result

The first figure is the estimated hands-on time to build one unit. The second turns that into labour cost per unit at your chosen rate, and the third shows what that adds up to across the batch. The chart shows where the time goes, which is usually more useful than the total itself.

  • Comfortable: simple handheld or housewares products typically land at around one to three minutes. If yours does, assembly is unlikely to be your main cost problem.
  • Worth a look: small electromechanical products with wiring, a PCB and several fastened joints often sit somewhere between three and ten minutes. Check whether joining or reorientation dominates the chart, because those are the easiest to design out.
  • Worrying: if labour cost per unit is a large share of your target unit cost, or one slice of the chart is far bigger than the others, the design probably needs simplifying before tooling is committed.

Common next steps are combining parts, swapping screws for snap fits or self-locating features, and arranging the build so everything goes in from one direction. Re-run the estimate after each change to see which one moves the number most. Save your result to your product plan so the labour assumption sits alongside your cost and pricing work. If the estimate is high and the fix is not obvious, a short conversation with Andrew can help you see where the design could be simplified.

FAQ

How do you estimate assembly time during design?

Break the task into handling, joining, reorientation, and checking so design decisions can be compared before final process engineering.

What increases manual assembly cost fastest?

Repeated joins, awkward part handling, and designs that force operators to keep turning the product all add recurring labour quickly.

Can this replace a formal DFA study?

No. It is deliberately lightweight. Use it to narrow concepts, then follow with a detailed DFA review once geometry and process are more stable.

How can I reduce assembly time in my design?

Reduce part count first, then make the remaining parts easy to handle and fit: self-locating features, a single assembly direction, fewer loose fasteners, and parts that cannot be fitted the wrong way round. Snap fits can save time but need careful design if the product must be serviced or repaired.

Is fewer parts always better?

Usually for assembly time, but not always overall. Combining parts can make tooling more complex, make repair harder, or force a compromise in material choice. Weigh assembly savings against tooling cost, serviceability and sustainability before merging parts.
