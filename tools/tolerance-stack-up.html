Quick answer

A stack-up adds up the variation of every dimension in a chain to see the range a gap or fit can take.

Nominal gap = Σ (+ dimensions that open the gap) − Σ (dimensions that close it) Worst case = nominal ± Σ tolerances RSS (statistical) = nominal ± √(Σ tolerance²)

Worst case assumes every part is at its limit at once, so it is safe but can demand very tight tolerances. RSS assumes variation is random and independent, so it predicts the range most assemblies will fall in.

How to set up the chain

  1. Pick the gap or fit you care about, such as a lid clearance or the end float in a bearing stack.
  2. Trace the chain of dimensions from one side of the gap to the other, through each part that sets it.
  3. Mark each dimension as opening the gap (+) or closing it (−).
  4. Enter each nominal and its ± tolerance, then set the minimum (and, if relevant, maximum) gap you need.

Worst case or RSS?

Use worst case for safety-critical fits, small production runs, few parts in the chain, or processes that tend to run off-centre. Use RSS for longer chains in reasonable volumes from stable processes, where every part being at its limit at once is very unlikely. RSS predicts roughly 99.7 per cent of assemblies within range only when each dimension is normally distributed, centred and has its tolerance set at three standard deviations; real processes often fall short of that, so many engineers add a safety factor or check both.

How to read your result

  • Meets the requirement in the worst case: the fit works even with every part at its limit. Check the other end too, as an over-large gap can rattle or look uneven.
  • Meets it only statistically: most assemblies will be fine, but some may fail. Decide whether that is acceptable, or tighten the largest contributors.
  • Fails both: change the nominal gap, tighten the critical tolerances, remove parts from the chain, or add an adjustable or compliant feature.

Tolerances must be achievable by the process: see the injection moulding DFM checklist and ask suppliers what they can hold. Tight tolerances cost money in tooling, inspection and scrap, so tighten only the dimensions that matter most. Save your result to your product plan.

FAQ

What is a tolerance stack-up?

A calculation of how the tolerances of several parts in a chain combine to change a gap or fit. It shows the range the gap can take when each part varies within its tolerance, so you can check the assembly will still work.

What is the difference between worst-case and RSS stack-up?

Worst case adds the tolerances directly, assuming every part is at its limit together. RSS (root sum of squares) combines them statistically, assuming independent random variation, which gives a narrower range that most assemblies will fall within.

When should I use worst-case tolerance analysis?

For safety-critical fits, low volumes, short chains, or processes that are not well centred. In those cases the statistical assumptions behind RSS may not hold, and a failure could be costly.

How do I handle unequal tolerances such as +0.2/−0?

Convert them to a symmetric form: use the middle of the tolerance band as the nominal and half the band as the plus-or-minus tolerance. A 10 mm dimension at +0.2/−0 becomes 10.1 mm ± 0.1 mm.

Does this calculator handle angles or 3D stacks?

No. It handles one-dimensional chains, which cover many everyday fits. Stacks involving angles, form or position tolerances, or several directions at once need a more detailed analysis or specialist software.
