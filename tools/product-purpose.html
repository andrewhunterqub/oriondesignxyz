Why these four axes matter together

Products can be technically possible and commercially attractive while still offering weak human value or poor planetary outcomes. This model forces those tensions into one visible frame early enough to change the brief.

A product that scores highly on technical feasibility and economy but poorly on humanity and planet should trigger a harder discussion, not a faster launch.

Questions for the review meeting

  • Does this solve a meaningful problem for someone?
  • Is the material and lifecycle burden defensible?
  • Can the product sustain itself commercially?
  • Can the team reliably deliver and support it?

Method and assumptions

Overall score = human value + planet impact + economic viability + technical feasibility (each 0–10, out of 40)

Assumptions

  • The four axes are weighted equally.
  • Benchmark products are scored on the same four axes for comparison; their scores are judgements, not measurements.

Limits

  • The result reflects your own ratings, so it is only as good as the evidence behind them; ask others to score independently.
  • The shape of the radar chart often says more than the total.

How to read your result

The overall score adds your four ratings together, out of 40. The ranking places your concept among a set of benchmark products scored the same way, and the radar chart shows the shape of the concept, which is often more telling than the total.

  • Strong: a total in the low thirties or above with no axis below about 6 suggests a balanced concept worth developing further. The next job is to test those scores with real users, suppliers and numbers.
  • Mixed: a total in the twenties, or a lopsided radar, usually means one or two axes are carrying the concept. Work out what would lift the weakest axis and whether that change is realistic.
  • Worrying: any single axis at 3 or below is a warning sign whatever the total. A product that is easy to build and profitable but offers little real value, or carries a heavy environmental burden, tends to struggle later.

Scores are only as honest as the people giving them, so it helps to have two or three people score independently and compare. Save your result to your product plan so the reasoning behind the concept is recorded early. If the team cannot agree on technical feasibility in particular, talking it through with Andrew can help separate real engineering risk from uncertainty.

FAQ

What is the Product Purpose Framework for?

It helps teams evaluate whether a product idea is strong enough across value, impact, viability, and feasibility to justify further investment.

Can a commercially viable product still score badly?

Yes. Commercial viability alone does not guarantee genuine human value or acceptable planetary impact.

How should I score each of the four dimensions?

Score against evidence rather than hope: what customers have told you, what the materials and energy use actually are, what the numbers say, and what has been proven technically. It helps to have someone outside the project score it too. The value is in the conversation about why scores differ, not in the exact number.

What should I do if one dimension scores low?

Treat it as a pointer, not a verdict. A low human-value score suggests revisiting the user need; a low planet score suggests looking at materials, lifetime or energy use; a low economic score points to cost or pricing; a low technical score means there is risk to retire before committing more. Re-score after changing the concept.

Can I compare several concepts with this framework?

Yes. Score each concept separately and compare the shapes on the radar chart. For a more structured, weighted comparison against engineering criteria, follow up with the Pugh Concept Selector.
