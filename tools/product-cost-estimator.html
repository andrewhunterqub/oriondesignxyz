Quick answer

A realistic cost estimate includes more than materials and process.

COGS = materials + bought-in parts + process + labour + packaging + scrap allowance + overhead

Early estimates usually fail because the product team only prices the obvious physical parts and ignores the cost of making the product repeatedly.

Worked example

A simple consumer product might carry GBP4.80 of raw material, GBP2.40 of process cost, GBP5.50 of bought-in parts, GBP4.80 of labour, and GBP1.20 of packaging. Add 6% scrap and 14% overhead, and the fully loaded COGS reaches roughly GBP22 per unit.

What this estimator is trying to stop

  • Setting a retail price before the recurring build cost is credible.
  • Underestimating the effect of bought-in parts, labour, and scrap.
  • Sending a product into route-to-market planning with a weak COGS target.

How to read your result

The first figure is your fully loaded cost of goods sold (COGS): what it costs to make, pack and finish one unit once scrap and factory overhead are included. The second is the gross margin left at the selling price you entered, and the third is what a year of production would cost at your stated volume. The breakdown shows which cost lines carry the most weight.

  • Healthy: a common rule of thumb for physical products is a retail price of roughly three to five times COGS, which leaves room for retailer, distributor and marketing costs. If you are well inside that range, the cost base is probably workable.
  • Borderline: if retail price is only around two to three times COGS, selling direct may still work, but retail or distribution channels are likely to squeeze you hard.
  • Worrying: if margin is close to zero or negative, or one cost line dwarfs the rest, revisit the design, the bought-in parts or the volume assumption before going further.

Whatever the result, check the largest cost lines first. Bought-in parts and labour are often easier to reduce through design changes than raw material. Save your result to your product plan so the COGS figure carries through to pricing and break-even work. If the numbers look tight and you are not sure where the savings are, a second opinion from Andrew can help identify where design for manufacture would make a difference.

FAQ

What should be included in an early manufacturing cost estimate?

Materials, bought-in parts, process cost, assembly labour, packaging, scrap allowance, and overhead loading should all be considered.

Why do early cost estimates come in too low?

Because teams often omit hidden but recurring costs such as packaging, labour burden, rejects, and factory overhead.

How accurate is an early product cost estimate?

At concept stage, an estimate built from typical rates is a planning figure, and it is common for the final cost to differ by a wide margin, often in the region of 25–50%. Accuracy improves as the design firms up, parts are specified, and real supplier quotes replace assumptions. Record your assumptions so you can see which ones changed.

Should tooling cost be included in the unit cost?

Keep tooling separate as a one-off investment, then look at its effect when spread over your realistic volume. Mixing the two hides the decision about when tooling is worth paying for. The Manufacturing Payoff Visualiser shows where a tooled route overtakes lower-setup options.

What is landed cost, and why does it matter?

Landed cost is the cost of a finished unit once it reaches your warehouse: the factory price plus freight, insurance, import duty and handling. For products made overseas it can add significantly to the ex-works price, so use landed cost, not the factory quote, when checking margins and pricing.
