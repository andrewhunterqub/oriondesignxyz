Why process mapping helps

Many projects fail because everyone assumes the same sequence of work exists in their head. A visible process map makes dependencies, missing gates, and false urgency easier to spot.

  • Discovery and definition reduce wasted build cycles.
  • Verification prevents teams from mistaking a prototype for a product.
  • Industrialisation should happen before launch pressure forces bad decisions.

Method and assumptions

Emphasis per stage = starting profile for the project stage + adjustments for complexity, regulation, manufacturing novelty and launch window, kept between 5% and 100%

Assumptions

  • Higher complexity adds emphasis to define, prototype, verify and industrialise; heavier regulation adds verification and industrialisation; new or complex manufacturing adds prototyping and industrialisation.
  • A launch window of 6 months or less adds definition, verification and launch effort; 12 months or more allows more discovery and prototyping.

Limits

  • The percentages show relative emphasis for planning, not effort, cost or time.
  • Turn the map into a schedule with the Timeline Planner.

How to read your result

The map scores six stages of work, from discovery through to launch, by how much attention each deserves in your next few months. The two highest become your primary and secondary priorities. The percentages are relative emphasis, not time or budget, so read them as a ranking rather than a plan.

  • Priorities match your stage: at idea stage you would expect discovery and definition to lead, and at industrialisation you would expect verification and production readiness to lead. If that is what you see, your plan and your stage line up.
  • Several stages scoring high at once: this is typical of complex or regulated products. It usually means work needs to overlap, for example planning compliance testing while prototypes are still being refined.
  • Compressed schedule warning: a launch window of six months or less is flagged because most physical products need longer to move from feasibility to production. Either the scope needs trimming or the date needs testing against supplier lead times.

Use the top two stages to decide what the next three to six months should deliver, and write down the evidence that would show each one is complete. Save your result to your product plan so the sequence sits alongside your budget and timeline. If the map suggests a lot needs to happen in parallel, a conversation with Andrew can help you work out a realistic order.

FAQ

What are the main stages of designing a physical product?

Most projects move through discovery, definition, prototyping, verification, industrialisation and launch. The names vary between companies, but the logic is the same: understand the problem, decide what to build, prove it works, then prove it can be made repeatably.

How long does product development usually take?

It depends heavily on complexity, regulation and manufacturing route. A simple moulded item with a known process can move in a matter of months, while a regulated electromechanical product often takes well over a year. Supplier lead times for tooling and certification are frequently what set the pace.

Do I have to finish one stage before starting the next?

No. Stages overlap in practice, and good projects plan that overlap deliberately. What matters is not committing to expensive, hard-to-reverse steps such as production tooling before the evidence from earlier stages is in place.

When should I start thinking about manufacturing?

Earlier than most people expect. Material, process and assembly choices shape the design from the concept stage, and leaving them until the design is finished often leads to costly rework. A short manufacturing review during definition is usually time well spent.
