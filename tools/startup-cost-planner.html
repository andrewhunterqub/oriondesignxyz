When to use this

Use the startup cost planner before the funding target or launch budget hardens.

The planner is most useful when a product has moved beyond a loose idea but the team has not yet committed to tooling, compliance spend, first inventory, or launch activity. It turns scattered cost assumptions into one pre-launch budget.

What the result means

The headline budget combines development, prototypes, tooling, compliance, first stock, launch work, and contingency. The monthly burn figure spreads that budget across the months to launch, which helps reveal whether the planned pace is realistic.

If the result looks uncomfortable, do not only reduce contingency. Look for staged decisions: a smaller prototype build, delayed tooling, a narrower launch, or a supplier conversation that can replace guesswork with real numbers.

What to do next

  • Check whether the biggest cost category is based on evidence or optimism.
  • Separate costs needed to learn from costs needed to launch.
  • Use the cashflow tool after this to see whether the launch plan creates a runway problem.
  • Bring uncertain line items into supplier, compliance, or product design conversations early.

How to read your result

Compare the total budget with the cash you have or can realistically raise, and the monthly burn with what your team can sustain.

  • Comfortable: the budget, including contingency, fits within the funds you have or have firm commitments for, with a buffer left over for delays. Contingency of around 15% to 25% is a common starting point for a first hardware product.
  • Borderline: the total only fits if contingency is trimmed or if every supplier quote comes in at the low end. Plan staged decisions so you can pause before the largest spend, usually tooling and first inventory.
  • Worrying: the budget is well beyond available funding, or the monthly burn is higher than the business can sustain for the months to launch. Look at a narrower first product, a lower-volume manufacturing route, or a funding round timed before the major commitments.

Hardware projects often take longer than planned, so if the timeline is tight, test what an extra few months of burn would add before treating the figure as final. Save your result to your product plan to build up a complete picture of cost, cash and launch timing.

If tooling or compliance make up a large share of the total and you have not yet had quotes, it can be worth a second opinion before you set a funding target. You can get in touch with Andrew if that would help.

FAQ

Should contingency be removed to make the budget look better?

No. A lower contingency does not remove risk. It usually just hides it until the project is harder to change.

Is this the same as a cashflow forecast?

No. This estimates pre-launch budget. Cashflow also needs timing, payment delay, stock purchases, overhead, and sales ramp assumptions.

How much contingency should a hardware budget include?

Early in development, a contingency of roughly 15–25% is common, with more for novel technology, regulated products or first-time teams. Reduce it as risks are retired by testing and supplier quotes, rather than removing it to make the plan look cheaper.

Which costs do first-time founders most often forget?

Certification and testing, tooling modifications after first samples, packaging design and print, freight and import duty, first inventory and minimum order quantities, insurance, returns and warranty, and the time it takes to get there. Each one is small enough to overlook and large enough to matter.

Do I need all of this money before launch?

Not necessarily all at once. Costs fall at different times: design and prototypes first, then tooling and certification, then inventory. Use the Cashflow Projection Tool to see when cash is needed, which helps plan funding in stages.
