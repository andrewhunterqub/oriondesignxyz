What a fast business case should answer

A concept does not need a full spreadsheet model before it deserves scrutiny. It does need a clear answer to whether the reachable volume and unit economics can support the planned spend.

  • How much of the market can the team realistically reach?
  • What share of that reachable audience converts into sales?
  • How long would it take gross contribution to recover development spend?

Method and assumptions

Units sold = market units × reachable share × conversion rate Revenue = units sold × price Gross profit = units sold × (price − COGS) Annual contribution = gross profit − annual operating costs Year-one result = annual contribution − development spend Break-even units = (development spend + annual operating costs) ÷ (price − COGS) Payback (months) = development spend ÷ annual contribution × 12

Assumptions

  • Sales happen at one price and COGS in a steady first year.
  • Development spend is a one-off cost recovered from annual contribution.

Limits

  • Market size, reach and conversion are your assumptions; small changes move the result a lot, so test ranges.
  • Timing, cash flow, financing, tax and price erosion are not included; see the Cash Flow Projection.

How to read your result

Read the recommendation card first, then look at how much the result depends on the two percentages: reachable share and conversion.

  • Promising: the case shows positive contribution and development spend is repaid within roughly one to two years, using share and conversion figures you can point to evidence for, such as pre-orders, pilot customers or comparable products.
  • Borderline: payback stretches beyond two years, or the case only turns positive when reachable share or conversion is nudged upwards. The opportunity may still be worth pursuing, but it needs stronger evidence or a leaner cost base.
  • Weak: the tool shows a contribution gap, so gross profit does not cover annual operating cost. Revisit price, unit cost, the size of the team behind the product, or whether a narrower first market would be easier to win.

Whatever the outcome, run a cautious version with half the share and conversion you first entered. If the case still holds, it is far easier to defend to investors or a board. Save your result to your product plan so the business case sits alongside your cost, margin and cashflow work.

If the decision depends on a large development or tooling spend, it can help to have someone challenge the assumptions before you commit. You can get in touch with Andrew if that would be useful.

FAQ

How do I estimate addressable market units?

Start from the number of people or organisations who have the problem and could buy the product in a year, not the total population or the whole category. Count bottom-up where you can, for example households with a specific appliance or labs running a specific test, rather than relying on broad market-size headlines.

What is a realistic reachable share for a new product?

For a new brand, the share of a market you can actually reach through your chosen channels and budget is usually small, often in the low single figures. If the case only works at a high share, treat that as a warning sign rather than a target.

Should development spend include tooling?

Yes. Include design and engineering, prototypes, injection mould tooling, certification and other one-off costs needed before you can sell. The payback figure then shows how long gross contribution takes to recover that investment once overheads are covered.

Is this enough for an investor pitch?

It is a screening model, not a financial forecast. It is useful for deciding whether an idea deserves deeper work and for framing the conversation, but investors will expect a monthly model with cash, ramp-up and evidence behind each assumption.
