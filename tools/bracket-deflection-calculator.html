Quick answer

A bracket bends in proportion to the load and the cube of its length, and resists it with its stiffness, E × I.

The material sets E and the shape of the section sets I, the second moment of area. Doubling the thickness of a flat bracket makes it eight times stiffer, while doubling its length makes it bend eight times as far. The stress check then tells you whether the material will spring back or take a permanent bend.

How this works

The tool uses the standard Euler–Bernoulli beam formulas for a straight, elastic beam with the same section along its length. Units are millimetres, newtons and megapascals (N/mm²), so deflections come out in millimetres and stresses in megapascals. A mass is turned into a force with g = 9.81 m/s². For the spread load, F is the total load along the length, so w = F ÷ L.

Second moment of area, I, and distance to the outer fibre, c: Solid rectangle: I = b × h³ ÷ 12 c = h ÷ 2 Rectangular tube: I = (B × H³ − b × h³) ÷ 12 c = H ÷ 2 inner sizes b = B − 2t, h = H − 2t Round bar: I = π × d⁴ ÷ 64 c = d ÷ 2 Round tube: I = π × (D⁴ − d⁴) ÷ 64, d = D − 2t c = D ÷ 2 Cantilever, end load: δ = F × L³ ÷ (3 × E × I) M_max = F × L Supported, centre load: δ = F × L³ ÷ (48 × E × I) M_max = F × L ÷ 4 Cantilever, evenly spread load: δ = w × L⁴ ÷ (8 × E × I) M_max = w × L² ÷ 2 Bending stress: σ = M_max × c ÷ I Share of allowable: σ ÷ σ_allow Safety factor = σ_allow ÷ σ Deflection limit: δ_limit = L ÷ n, or the distance you enter Thickness needed (solid rectangle, width b held): for deflection: h = ∛(12 × F × L³ ÷ (k × E × b × δ_limit)), k = 3, 48 or 8 for stress: h = √(6 × M_max ÷ (b × σ_allow))

The default example. An aluminium flat bar 30 mm wide and 5 mm thick, held as a cantilever 100 mm long with 20 N (about 2 kg) at the end. I = 30 × 5³ ÷ 12 = 312.5 mm⁴. δ = 20 × 100³ ÷ (3 × 69,000 × 312.5) ≈ 0.31 mm, against an L ÷ 200 limit of 0.5 mm (62%). M_max = 20 × 100 = 2,000 N·mm, so σ = 2,000 × 2.5 ÷ 312.5 = 16 MPa: 16% of the 100 MPa example allowable stress, a safety factor of 6.25.

The status takes the worse of the two checks, deflection against its limit and stress against the allowable stress: up to 80% passes, 80–100% is marginal, and above 100% is over. The safety factor is simply your allowable stress divided by the calculated stress, so it is only as good as the allowable figure you enter.

Assumptions and limits

  • Small deflections. The formulas hold while the deflection is small compared with the length. If the result is more than about a tenth of the length, treat it as a sign the part is far too flexible rather than as an accurate figure.
  • Linear elastic behaviour. The material is assumed to spring back fully. Above the yield stress, metals take a permanent bend and the formulas no longer apply.
  • A fully fixed root. The cantilever cases assume the root cannot rotate at all. Real joints are less stiff: screws, rivets, welds, thin walls and plastic bosses all let the root turn a little, so a real bracket usually bends more than the estimate, sometimes much more.
  • Self-weight is ignored. Only the load you enter is counted. For long or heavy beams, add the beam's own weight as a spread load.
  • No buckling, fatigue or stress concentrations. The tool does not check thin walls or flanges buckling, cracks growing under repeated or vibrating loads, or the higher local stress at holes, slots, notches and tight bends. Keep holes away from the root, and use generous bend and fillet radii, as in sheet metal design basics.
  • Plastics creep. A plastic part under a load that stays on keeps bending slowly over time, and faster when warm. The short-term modulus on a datasheet will underestimate the deflection, so use long-term (creep) modulus data for your load, time and temperature.
  • Confirm by test or FEA. This is a first estimate. Check the real part by loading a prototype and measuring it, or with a finite element analysis that includes the real joint and any holes.

How to read your result

  • Pass: the deflection and the stress each use no more than 80% of your limits. Check the joint at the root, which is usually the weak point, and confirm by test.
  • Marginal: within your limits, but small changes in thickness, material batch or a less rigid root could push it over. Thicken the section or shorten the length, then test.
  • Over: the bracket is likely to bend too far or take a permanent set. The thickness needed shows how far a flat bracket is from working; for other sections, try a deeper section.

Depth in the direction of the load is the strongest lever: deflection falls with the cube of the thickness. Shortening the length helps almost as much. A stiffer material helps only in proportion, which is why an aluminium part often needs thicker sections than a steel one, as choosing metals for a product explains. For a bent sheet part, a flange or return along the edge adds depth cheaply. FAQ

Which elastic modulus should I use?

Use the figure from the datasheet for your exact material. The buttons fill in typical textbook values only: about 69,000 MPa for aluminium alloys and about 200,000 MPa for steel. Plastics vary much more by grade, temperature and moisture, so the ABS figure is an example only, and a load that stays on needs the long-term (creep) modulus.

What deflection limit should I use?

Set it from what the bracket must do: a gap that must not close, a display that must not visibly droop, or parts that must stay aligned. Expressing it as a fraction of the length, L ÷ n, scales it with the size of the part; the default of L ÷ 200 is an example, not a rule.

Why does my real bracket bend more than the estimate?

Usually because the root is not fully fixed. Screws, thin mounting walls and plastic bosses let the root rotate, which adds to the deflection at the tip. Holes and slots near the root, a softer material than expected and, for plastics, creep all add more. Measure a prototype to find out how much.

What safety factor should I aim for?

It depends on how well you know the loads and the material, and what happens if the part fails. The tool divides your allowable stress by the calculated stress; if you enter the yield strength, a factor of 1 means it is just starting to bend permanently. Shocks, drops and repeated loads need more margin, and fatigue needs its own check.
