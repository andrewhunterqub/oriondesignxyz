Quick answer

The best route is the one that leaves enough money for both COGS and margin after hidden selling costs.

Target COGS = retained price after VAT and route deductions - hidden selling costs - required margin

Retailer routes usually create the hardest COGS ceiling because wholesale deductions remove the largest share of the RRP before logistics and account costs are counted.

Why route to market changes what your product can cost

The same product can carry very different economics depending on whether it is sold direct, through a marketplace, or through retail. The retail price may stay the same while the amount left for COGS changes dramatically.

  • DTC keeps more of the selling price, but you absorb warehousing, packaging, delivery, site fees, and customer acquisition.
  • Marketplace sales can look simpler, but marketplace fees and fulfilment extras can erode the margin quickly.
  • Retail can unlock scale, but wholesale pricing means the product usually needs to be designed to a much harder COGS target.

Example output

A product that feels healthy at a 180 GBP RRP might afford a very different COGS ceiling depending on whether the sale is direct, through Amazon or Etsy, or through a retailer taking a standard margin.

Method and assumptions

Price after VAT = RRP ÷ (1 + VAT rate) Kept per route = price after VAT × (1 − route fee or retailer margin) Target profit = kept × target operating margin Target COGS ceiling = kept − route costs − target profit

Assumptions

  • Route costs (warehousing, packaging, logistics, fees, marketing) are per-unit figures you enter.
  • The target operating margin is taken as a share of the amount each route keeps.
  • The marketplace fee is applied to the price after VAT. Some marketplaces charge on the VAT-inclusive price, which leaves slightly less.

Limits

  • It shows a ceiling for unit cost, not a forecast of sales or profit.
  • It does not include fixed overheads, promotions, returns or payment terms; use the Launch Margin Planner for those.

How to read your result

For each route, compare the maximum COGS the route can support with your current COGS. The gap between them is the clearest signal.

  • Comfortable: your current COGS sits well below the ceiling for the route you plan to launch through, leaving room for cost increases, currency changes or a promotional price.
  • Borderline: COGS is only just under the ceiling, or it works for direct sales but not for retail. That is common at early stages, but it means the product either needs cost reduction before retail or a launch plan that starts direct.
  • Worrying: COGS is above the ceiling for every route you are considering. The price, the product cost or the target margin needs to change, and design decisions such as part count, materials and assembly method are usually the biggest lever.

As a rough cross-check, many teams planning a retail launch aim for a landed COGS of around a fifth to a third of the retail price including VAT, because retailer margin, VAT and selling costs take so much of the shelf price. Direct routes can carry a higher COGS, but only if marketing and fulfilment costs are counted honestly. Save your result to your product plan to record the target COGS for each route alongside your cost estimate.

If there is a large gap between current COGS and the target, it is worth a design for manufacture review before tooling is committed. You can get in touch with Andrew if you would like help working out where the cost could come from.

FAQ

What retailer margin should I assume?

It varies by category and retailer, but margins of roughly 30% to 50% of the selling price excluding VAT are common for consumer products, and distributors may take a further share. Use the figure from a real conversation with a buyer as soon as you have one.

Why is the RRP entered including VAT?

Because that is the price customers see on the shelf or website. The tool removes VAT first, since VAT is collected on behalf of the tax authority and is not revenue for the business. For B2B products where customers reclaim VAT, you can still enter the gross price and the standard rate.

Should DTC marketing really be counted per unit?

Yes. Direct sales avoid retailer margin, but customers still have to be found, and advertising often becomes one of the largest costs per sale. Dividing your planned marketing spend by the units it should generate gives a more honest comparison with retail.

Can I sell through several routes at once?

Yes, and many hardware brands do. Use this tool to set a COGS target that works for the hardest route you plan to use, then use the Revenue and Margin Simulator to see the combined result of a mixed route plan.
