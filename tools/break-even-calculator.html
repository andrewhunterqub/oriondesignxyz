Quick answer

Break-even units depend on contribution, not just revenue.

Break-even units = fixed costs / (selling price - variable cost)

If the contribution per unit is weak, the required sales volume rises quickly even when the top-line number looks attractive.

Use this after product cost is credible

Break-even is useful when the variable cost per unit already reflects reality. If the COGS figure is still optimistic, the break-even point will be optimistic as well.

How to read your result

Start with the contribution per unit, then compare the break-even volume with the number of units you have real evidence you can sell.

  • Comfortable: break-even sits well below the volume you expect to sell in the first year or two, and contribution is a healthy share of the price you receive. A drop in demand or a small cost increase would delay payback rather than threaten it.
  • Borderline: break-even is close to your realistic sales forecast, or contribution is a thin slice of the price. Small changes in unit cost, discounting or channel terms will move the break-even point a long way, so test a few pessimistic cases.
  • Worrying: break-even is higher than any volume you can credibly reach, or contribution is near zero or negative. Selling more will not fix this; the price, the unit cost or the fixed cost base needs to change first.

In the first two cases, check that the variable cost includes everything that scales with each sale, such as packaging, fulfilment, payment fees and returns, not just the factory price. In the last case, look at design for manufacture, a different route to market, or a smaller first tooling commitment before adding volume. Save your result to your product plan so the break-even volume sits alongside your cost and cashflow assumptions.

If the result depends heavily on a unit cost that has not yet been quoted by a supplier, it is worth a second opinion before you commit to tooling. You can get in touch with Andrew if you would like to talk it through.

FAQ

What counts as a fixed cost for a hardware product?

Costs you pay regardless of how many units you sell: design and engineering, tooling, certification, and ongoing overheads such as salaries, rent and software. Decide whether you are testing recovery of one-off launch costs or a year of running costs, and keep that consistent when you compare scenarios.

Should the selling price include VAT or retailer margin?

No. Use the price you actually receive per unit, excluding VAT and after any retailer, distributor or marketplace share. For a product sold through retail, that is usually the wholesale price, which can be roughly half of the shelf price or less.

What should go into the variable cost per unit?

Everything that is spent each time a unit is made and sold: the bill of materials, assembly, packaging, freight and duty, fulfilment, payment fees and an allowance for returns or warranty. Leaving these out is the most common reason a break-even point looks better than it really is.

Why does my break-even jump when I change the price slightly?

Break-even divides fixed costs by contribution per unit, so when contribution is small, a modest change in price or cost changes it by a large percentage. That sensitivity is itself useful information: it shows how much margin of safety the plan really has.
