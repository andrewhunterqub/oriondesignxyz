What this estimate is useful for

Early carbon decisions are usually directional rather than exact. If a concept carries heavy material burden, energy-intensive use, or unnecessarily long transport routes, that is enough to justify design changes before detailed LCA work begins.

A relatively small product can still become carbon-heavy if its use phase dominates. Equally, a passive product may be driven mostly by material selection and transport assumptions.

Use the estimate to compare

  • material substitutions
  • manufacturing route changes
  • transport assumptions
  • durability and energy-consumption trade-offs

Method and assumptions

Material = mass × material factor × (1 − recycled share × 0.35) Process = mass × process factor Transport = mass × distance × transport factor Use = annual energy (kWh) × lifetime (years) × 0.193 Total = material + process + transport + use (kg CO₂e)

Assumptions

  • Material factors are typical cradle-to-gate values per kilogram, and process factors are typical allowances per kilogram processed; both are built into the tool for comparison rather than certification.
  • Recycled content lowers the material factor by up to 35% at 100% recycled.
  • Use-phase electricity uses a fixed grid factor of 0.193 kg CO₂e per kWh, close to a recent UK grid average; official factors are updated each year.

Limits

  • It is a design-stage estimate, not a footprint to ISO 14067 or an LCA; see product carbon footprint vs LCA.
  • Packaging, consumables, end of life and real supplier data are not included; factors vary by source and region.
  • Do not use the result for public environmental claims.

How to read your result

The total is an estimate of the greenhouse gas emissions linked to one product over its life, in kilograms of CO2 equivalent. The embodied figure covers material, manufacturing and transport before the product is switched on. The use-phase share tells you how much of the total comes from the energy the product draws while in service.

  • Use phase above roughly half: typical of products that plug in or run on batteries every day. Energy efficiency, standby power and lifetime are where design effort pays back most.
  • Material dominates: common for passive products such as housewares and enclosures. Reducing mass, choosing a lower-impact material or raising recycled content will move the number more than anything else.
  • Transport looks large: this usually points to air freight. Sea freight is far lower per kilogram, so check whether air is genuinely needed beyond urgent first shipments.
  • Manufacturing stands out: often a sign of machining or printing parts that could be moulded or formed at volume.

Treat the result as a way to rank options rather than a figure to publish. If you need a number for customers, retailers or regulators, a formal lifecycle assessment with supplier data is the right next step. Save your result to your product plan so sustainability choices sit beside cost and production decisions. If you are unsure which lever to pull first, a short conversation with Andrew can help.

FAQ

How can you estimate product carbon footprint during design?

Use early assumptions for material mass, manufacturing process, transport, energy use, and recycled content to compare concepts before detailed LCA work.

Is this a full lifecycle assessment?

No. This is a concept-stage estimator intended to compare options and expose major drivers, not replace a formal LCA.

Which part of a product's footprint usually matters most?

It depends on the product. For products that use energy, such as heaters, kettles or chargers, the use phase often dominates the total. For passive products such as housewares, furniture or enclosures, materials and manufacturing usually account for most of it. Running both scenarios in the estimator shows which side your product sits on, and therefore where design effort pays off.

How accurate are the carbon factors for materials and processes?

Published factors vary with the data source, region, recycled content and the boundaries used, so treat the result as a comparison between design options rather than a declared figure. For a formal footprint, use a recognised lifecycle database and method, such as ISO 14040/14044 for lifecycle assessment or ISO 14067 for product carbon footprints.

Can I use this result in marketing or on packaging?

Not on its own. It is an early design estimate. Public environmental claims in the UK need to be accurate and backed by evidence, and the Competition and Markets Authority's Green Claims Code sets out what that means. Use this tool to make better design decisions, then commission or carry out a proper assessment before making claims.
