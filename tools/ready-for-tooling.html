Ready to Commit to Tooling?

Production tooling is usually the largest commitment between prototype and launch, and the hardest to reverse. Answer ten questions about testing, design stability, demand, cash and supplier terms to see whether now is the time, and what to close first if not.

Quick answer

Commit to production tooling when the design has stopped moving, demand is evidenced and the cash covers tools and first stock together.

Readiness = Σ (importance × answer) ÷ Σ (importance × 2), where Yes = 2, Partly = 1, No = 0 Critical gap = a most-important question answered No

Testing, design freeze, demand and cash carry the most weight, because they are the usual causes of expensive tool changes or tools that never pay back.

Method and assumptions

Each answer scores Yes 2, Partly 1, No 0, multiplied by the question's importance weight Readiness % = total score ÷ maximum possible score × 100 Gaps are ranked by weight × points missing

Assumptions

  • Importance weights reflect how costly each gap usually is once tooling has started.
  • A critical gap is a top-weighted question answered No.

Limits

  • It is a self-check, not a design review; have the parts reviewed for manufacture by your toolmaker.
  • See the DFM Checker and DFM checklist.

How to read your result

  • About 80% or more with no critical gaps: ready to go ahead, with the remaining gaps closed in parallel.
  • About 55 to 80%, or one critical gap: possible, but with avoidable risk. Close the top gaps first if you can.
  • Below about 55%, or several critical gaps: too early. Work through the gaps in the order listed.

The weights and thresholds are Orion Design's rules of thumb for planning, not a formal standard. Each gap links to the guide or tool that helps close it. Save your result to your product plan and repeat the check as you progress.

FAQ

When should I invest in injection mould tooling?

When production-intent parts have passed testing, the design is frozen, demand is evidenced well beyond the tooling break-even volume, and you can fund the tools and first stock together. Before then, bridge tooling or low-volume processes usually cost less overall.

What is bridge tooling?

Cheaper, quicker tooling, often aluminium or soft steel, used for early production while design and demand settle, before committing to hardened production tooling.

What happens if I change the design after tooling?

Some changes can be made by removing steel from the tool; others need welding, new inserts or a new tool. Changes cost time and money, and some are not possible at all.

How much should I allow for tool modifications?

Plan for at least one round of modifications after first samples, in both time and budget. The toolmaker can tell you what is typical for your parts.

Is anything I answer stored?

Not on a server. The check runs in your browser, and your answers are remembered in your own browser's storage so they are still there when you come back; clearing this site's data removes them.
