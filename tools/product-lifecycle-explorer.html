What lifecycle thinking changes

Lifecycle performance is not only a materials question. Product lifetime, in-use energy, repair access, and recovery design can all shift the total impact and business resilience of a product.

  • Longer life spreads embodied impact across more years of service.
  • High use-phase energy can dominate total impact even for lightweight products.
  • Repairability and recovery create options at the end of the first ownership cycle.

Method and assumptions

Longevity = min(100, years × 10 + repairability × 6 + upgradeability × 4) Repair = repairability × 20 · Upgrade = upgradeability × 20 Materials = min(100, recycled % × 0.9 + 10 with take-back) Recovery = min(100, recovery % × 0.8 + 15 with take-back) Circularity = 0.3 longevity + 0.2 repair + 0.15 upgrade + 0.15 materials + 0.2 recovery Use-phase CO₂e = annual kWh × years × 0.193

Assumptions

  • Repairability and upgradeability are your 1–5 ratings.
  • The weighting favours a long, repairable life, reflecting that lifetime usually matters more than recycling.
  • Use-phase electricity uses a fixed grid factor of 0.193 kg CO₂e per kWh, close to a recent UK grid average; official factors are updated each year.

Limits

  • The scores are a structured comparison for design discussions, not a recognised circularity metric or certification.
  • Real impacts depend on materials, use and end-of-life systems; see the Carbon Footprint Estimator.

How to read your result

The circularity score combines longevity, repair, upgrade, material content and recovery into a single figure out of 100. The dominant driver names the factor most likely to shape the product's overall impact, and the use-phase carbon signal estimates the emissions from electricity used over the product's life. The radar chart shows which of the five areas are pulling the score up or down.

  • Strong: as a rough guide on this tool's scale, a score above about 70 with no very weak point on the radar suggests a product designed to last and to be recovered. Check that the lifetime and repair claims can be backed up in testing and service planning.
  • Middling: scores between about 40 and 70 are typical of conventional products. The weakest point on the radar is usually the cheapest place to improve, often by making the battery or a wear part replaceable.
  • Worrying: a score below about 40, or a dominant driver of short product life, means the product is likely to be replaced rather than repaired. If use-phase energy dominates, efficiency gains will matter far more than recycled content.

Try changing one input at a time to see which lever moves the result most, then focus design effort there. FAQ

What does circularity mean for a physical product?

It describes how well a product keeps its materials and value in use. That covers lasting a long time, being repairable and upgradeable, using recycled material, and being easy to take apart and recover at the end of life.

Is a longer product life always better?

Usually, because the impact of making the product is spread over more years of use. The exception is a product that uses a lot of energy, where replacing it with a much more efficient design can sometimes be the better choice.

How can I make a product easier to repair?

Use screws or clips rather than glue where parts may need to come apart, make batteries and wear parts replaceable, and keep fasteners to a small number of common types. Supplying spare parts and simple repair guidance helps just as much as the design itself.

Does adding recycled material reduce quality?

Not necessarily, but it can change how a material behaves and looks. Recycled plastics may vary in colour and strength between batches, so test them early and specify an acceptable range with your supplier rather than switching late in development.
