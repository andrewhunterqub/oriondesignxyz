Why this tool matters

Teams often jump between feasibility, user need, cost, and sustainability without a common structure. A weighted matrix forces the discussion into the open so trade-offs are visible and contestable.

If one concept scores well on value but weakly on manufacturability, the right response may be to derisk the concept rather than discard it. The matrix should guide questions, not shut them down.

Use it well

  • Keep criteria stable across all concepts.
  • Weight only the factors that actually matter to the decision.
  • Use the result to identify the next de-risking step, not just a winner.

Method and assumptions

Weighted score for each concept = Σ (criterion weight × concept score) Weights and scores are 1 to 5

Assumptions

  • Each criterion's weight reflects its importance to this product; all concepts are scored against the same criteria.
  • The gap between the top two concepts indicates how clear the choice is.

Limits

  • Scores are judgements; a small lead can come from scoring noise, so test the result by changing weights.
  • Use it to structure a discussion and record the reasoning, not to make the decision automatically.

How to read your result

Each concept's score is the sum of its ratings multiplied by the weight of each criterion. With five criteria scored and weighted from 1 to 5, the highest possible score is 125. The results show the leading concept, its score, and how far ahead it is of second place. The recommendation card flags whether that lead looks clear or close.

  • Clear lead: a gap of 8 points or more is treated as a clear lead here. Carry the leader forward, but look at its lowest-scoring criterion, because that is usually your next de-risking task.
  • Close decision: a small gap means the answer depends on the weights. Try nudging the most debated weight up or down by one. If the winner changes, the matrix is telling you that the team has not yet agreed what matters most.
  • Worrying: if every concept scores low, or the leader has a 1 or 2 on customer value or technical feasibility, the right move may be to generate better concepts rather than pick the least weak one.

A quick prototype or a supplier conversation often settles a close call faster than more debate. Save your result to your product plan so the reasoning behind the chosen concept is easy to revisit later. If the decision carries significant tooling or engineering risk, an independent view from Andrew can help before you commit.

FAQ

What is a Pugh concept selector?

A structured comparison matrix that scores concepts against the same criteria so teams can make design trade-offs transparently.

When should you use a concept selection matrix?

Use it when several options remain plausible and the project needs a shared rationale for prioritising which concept to carry forward.

How do I choose the criteria and weights?

Draw criteria from the product requirements: what the product must do, cost, manufacturability, reliability, user experience and sustainability. Five to eight criteria is usually enough. Agree the weights as a team before scoring any concept, so the weighting is not bent to favour a preferred idea.

What should I do if two concepts score almost the same?

Treat it as a tie rather than a win. Look at which criteria separate them, test the uncertainty behind those scores with a quick prototype or calculation, or combine the strongest features of both into a new concept and score that.

Does the highest score mean the concept is the right one?

It means it best fits the criteria and weights you chose, based on what you know now. Check how sensitive the result is to small weight changes, and remember the matrix structures a decision rather than making it for you.
