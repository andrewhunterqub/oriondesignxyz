Product Plan Builder

A product plan covers seven questions: what the product is and who it is for, how it will be developed, how it will be made and at what cost, its environmental impact, how it will be sold, how it will be funded, and how the idea is protected. Use any of the free tools, press Save to my product plan, and your results collect here in that structure, ready to print, download or share.

1

Opportunity and concept

Who is it for, what problem does it solve, and why this concept rather than the alternatives? Manufacturers, investors and grant panels all start here, and a clear answer makes every later conversation shorter.

Questions this section should answer

  • Who is the customer and what is the job the product does for them?
  • Which concepts were considered, and why did this one win?
  • How big is the opportunity, roughly?
2

Development and prototyping

How the product gets from idea to a design that is ready to make: which stages need most effort, what each prototype must prove, and where assembly complexity is building up.

Questions this section should answer

  • What is the highest-risk technical assumption, and how will you test it?
  • What will each prototype round prove?
  • How many parts and operations does the design need?
3

Manufacturing and cost

How the product will be made at each volume, what it costs per unit, and when tooling becomes worthwhile. This is usually the first thing a manufacturer or investor will test.

Questions this section should answer

  • Which process makes sense at 100, 1,000 and 10,000 units?
  • What is the estimated cost per unit, and what drives it?
  • When will you commit to tooling, and what has to be true first?
4

Sustainability and product life

Where the product's environmental impact sits and how long it will last in use. Retailers, procurement teams and funders increasingly ask for this, and early design choices make the biggest difference.

Questions this section should answer

  • Where does most of the carbon footprint come from?
  • How long will it last, and can it be repaired?
  • What happens to it at end of life?
5

Route to market and margins

How the product reaches customers, what each channel takes from the retail price, and how many units must sell before fixed costs are repaid.

Questions this section should answer

  • Direct, marketplace or retail: what does each leave you per unit?
  • What is the target factory cost (COGS) for the chosen route?
  • How many units until break-even?
6

Funding and cash

How much is needed before launch, when cash runs tight, and which numbers an investor will look at first.

Questions this section should answer

  • What does it cost to reach launch, including contingency?
  • When is cash at its lowest point?
  • Which KPIs show the business is working?
7

IP, partners and compliance

How you will protect the idea while talking to manufacturers, designers and investors, and what you need before those conversations start.

Questions this section should answer

  • What needs protecting, and how?
  • Which documents will a manufacturer ask for?
  • Who do you need to talk to next?

Quick answer

A hardware product plan connects the design to the numbers.

A useful plan for a physical product shows why the concept was chosen, how it will be developed and made, what each unit costs, what the sales channel leaves behind, and how much cash is needed before revenue arrives. Most early plans are weak on manufacturing route and unit cost, and that is where manufacturers, investors and grant assessors usually probe first.

How to use the Product Plan Builder

  1. Describe your product above so the plan has context.
  2. Open any tool from the sections, try your own numbers, and press Save to my product plan.
  3. Add notes under each section: assumptions, supplier quotes, open questions and decisions.
  4. Use Print or save as PDF to share it with a co-founder, manufacturer, mentor or funder.

Is it a full business plan?

No. It is the product and engineering backbone of one. A complete business plan also covers the team, marketing, competitors and financial statements. But a plan that is clear on concept, manufacturing route, unit cost, margin and cash is far easier to turn into one, and far more convincing.
