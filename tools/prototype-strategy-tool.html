When to use this

Choose the prototype route around the question the build must answer.

A prototype is not automatically better because it looks more production-like. The right route depends on quantity, fidelity, urgency, budget, and whether the design is mature enough to justify tooling commitment.

How to read your result

The score is a route-fit signal. A high score means the method is a reasonable match for the current build brief, not that it is the final manufacturing route. Close scores should trigger a discussion about what evidence the next prototype must create.

  • A clear leader: when the top route is a few points ahead of the rest, the brief is pointing firmly one way. Get quotes for that route and confirm lead time before planning the build around it.
  • Two routes close together: this is common and usually means the brief is mixing goals, such as wanting appearance and function from one build. Splitting into a looks-like and a works-like prototype often resolves it.
  • Tooled routes ranking highly early on: if bridge tooling or pilot injection comes out on top while the design is still changing, treat that as a warning. Tooling changes are slow and costly, so check design stability first.

If CNC and 3D printing score closely for a functional validation build, the better choice may depend on tolerance, material behaviour, and whether cosmetic finish matters yet. If bridge tooling wins, check that the design is stable enough first.

What to do next

  • Write down the one decision the prototype must unlock.
  • Separate looks-like, works-like, and production-like requirements.
  • Check whether the selected route creates reusable learning or only a better-looking sample.
  • Use production strategy planning before moving from prototype to bridge or pilot production.

Save your result to your product plan so the prototype route sits alongside your budget and timeline. If you are unsure what the next build needs to prove, a short conversation with Andrew can help shape the brief.

FAQ

Should the prototype use the final production process?

Only when the question requires it. Early builds often need speed and learning more than production fidelity.

When is bridge tooling sensible?

Bridge tooling is useful when demand or test needs exceed one-off prototypes, but the product is not ready for full production commitment.

How many prototype rounds does a product usually need?

Most products go through several: early looks-like and works-like models, an integrated prototype that combines form and function, and pre-production parts made by or close to the production process. Simple single-material products may need fewer rounds; electromechanical or regulated products usually need more. Plan each round around the question it must answer.

Is 3D printing good enough for testing?

For form, fit, ergonomics and many functional checks, yes, and it is quick and cheap to iterate. Printed parts can behave differently from moulded or machined parts in strength, stiffness, surface finish and tolerance, so final validation, drop tests and certification testing usually need production-intent parts.

What is the difference between looks-like and works-like prototypes?

A looks-like prototype represents appearance, size and feel; a works-like prototype proves the mechanism, electronics or performance, often without the final form. Splitting them lets you answer design and engineering questions in parallel before combining them into an integrated prototype.
