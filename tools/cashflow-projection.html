Quick answer

Profitable growth can still kill cash.

Closing cash = opening cash + cash in - cash out

For hardware teams, the dangerous gap is often the time between paying for stock and being paid by customers, distributors, or retailers.

What this tool is showing

The model tracks monthly units, revenue timing, stock cost, fixed overhead, and one-off month 1 spend. That is enough to show whether the business is on course to dip below zero even if demand looks encouraging.

Use it for

  • estimating runway pressure before launch
  • testing the effect of payment delay from channels or customers
  • seeing whether higher sales volumes actually increase short-term cash strain

How to read your result

The most important figure is the lowest cash point across the twelve months, and when it happens, rather than the closing balance.

  • Comfortable: cash never falls below a buffer of roughly three to six months of fixed overhead, and the curve flattens or turns upwards within the year. The plan can absorb a late payment or a slower sales month.
  • Borderline: the lowest point comes close to zero, or the buffer drops below about three months of overhead. One delayed customer payment, a larger stock order or a slip in launch timing could tip the business into a shortfall.
  • Worrying: cash goes negative at some point in the year. Note the month: that is the date by which you need more funding, better payment terms, or a smaller commitment to stock and overhead.

If the result is borderline or worrying, try the payment delay and growth inputs first, because faster growth often deepens the dip when stock is paid for before customers pay you. Then look at staging stock purchases, negotiating deposits or shorter terms, and timing the fundraise so it lands well before the lowest point. Many founders aim to raise with twelve to eighteen months of runway, as fundraising itself usually takes several months. Save your result to your product plan to keep the cash picture next to your startup costs and margin assumptions.

If the projection only works with growth or payment terms you have not yet tested with real customers, a second opinion can help. You can get in touch with Andrew to talk through the assumptions.

FAQ

Why does faster growth make my cash position worse?

Each extra unit needs stock paid for before the customer pays you, so rapid growth pulls more cash out of the business in the short term. This is normal for hardware and is why profitable products can still run out of cash while scaling.

What payment delay should I use for retail or distributor sales?

Direct online sales are usually paid in the same month. Retailers and distributors commonly pay 30 to 90 days after invoice. One or two months is a sensible starting point until you have agreed terms in writing; the tool models up to three months, so you can test the longest common terms.

Does this include VAT, deposits or tooling instalments?

No. The model keeps things simple: month 1 spend is a single lump, and stock and overhead are paid in the month they occur. If your supplier asks for deposits or you pay tooling in stages, adjust the opening cash and month 1 spend to reflect what actually leaves the bank and when.

How much cash buffer should a hardware startup keep?

There is no single right number, but keeping several months of fixed overhead in reserve is a common rule of thumb, and more if you depend on large stock orders or slow-paying customers. Hardware lead times mean problems take time to fix, so a thin buffer leaves little room to react.
