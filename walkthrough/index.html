Walkthrough: one product from idea to manufacture

This page follows one product, a rechargeable bike light, through all seven stages of development and through the free tools on this site. Each number is carried forward: the target cost set in week three is the one the cost estimate is checked against, the unit cost in the estimate is the one used in break-even, and the tooling quote is the one in the launch budget. Every tool button opens the tool already filled in with the walkthrough's inputs, so you can see the same result and then change it.

Please note: Project Lantern is an illustrative product; all figures are invented for teaching and are not data about any real product or client.

The scenario in brief

A £50 bike light that only works if it lands in the UK for about £14.

A two-person start-up plans a rechargeable front bike light: a moulded two-part plastic housing, an LED driver board, a lithium-ion battery and USB-C charging. It will sell for £50 including VAT, about 40% direct from its own website and 60% through bike shops and other retailers, with a first-year target of 5,000 units. A contract manufacturer overseas makes and assembles it, and it is shipped by sea to a UK warehouse. The retail route sets the hardest limit, so the whole project is designed to a landed cost of about £14 per unit.

An illustrative product; all figures are invented for teaching and are not data about any real product or client.

1. Discover

Is a better rechargeable bike light a problem worth solving?

Plan section 1: Opportunity and concept

The founders spend five weeks talking to 30 commuters and two bike shop owners. The recurring complaints are lights that are stolen when the bike is locked, mounts that crack in winter and batteries that die without warning. Shop owners add that they will not stock a light they cannot sell for £50 or less. Before any design work, the team scores the idea on human value, impact, viability and feasibility.

What they decided

  • Focus on commuters: a light that clips off in seconds, survives winter and shows remaining charge.
  • Treat £50 as the price ceiling, because that is what the shops said they could sell.
  • Work on planet impact from the start, since it scored lowest: a replaceable battery and a long service life.

What the tool shows: 28 out of 40, ranked 27th of 61 benchmark products, with planet impact the weakest score at 5 out of 10. The tool reads this as a mixed profile and suggests improving planet impact through material choice, fewer parts and a longer service life.

2. Define

What must it do, and what can it cost?

Plan section 5: Route to market and margins

The team writes a short requirements document: 400 lumens, at least 3 hours on the high setting, rain resistance, a tool-free clip mount, USB-C charging and a charge indicator. They then work back from the £50 price to what the product is allowed to cost through each sales route. Their channel assumptions are £0.50 warehousing and £0.30 secondary packaging per unit; £3.50 delivery, £1.50 site and payment fees and £6 marketing for each direct sale; and a 45% retailer margin with £0.50 inbound logistics and £1 of trade spend for each retail sale. A marketplace is not planned for launch.

What they decided

  • The retail route is the tightest, so it sets the cost limit for the whole product.
  • Design to a landed cost of £14, leaving about £2 of headroom under the retail ceiling for price rises and promotions.
  • List the standards that apply now (electrical safety, EMC and battery transport rules) so certification is planned, not discovered.

3. Concept

Which design direction should they develop?

Plan sections 1 and 3: Opportunity, and manufacturing route and unit cost

Three directions are sketched and roughly modelled: a two-part clip-on plastic housing, an aluminium tube body, and a light built into the seatpost. They are scored against user value, feasibility, manufacturing, sustainability and commercial fit. The winner is then costed straight away, before anyone becomes attached to it. The first layout has 18 parts, including a separate lens, a gasket and six screws.

What they decided

  • Develop the two-part clip-on housing: it wins clearly on feasibility and commercial fit.
  • Accept that the first estimate is over target and make cost the main question for the prototype stage.
  • Attack assembly and part count first, because they are cheaper to change now than after tooling.

4. Prototype

Does it work, and can it be made for less?

Plan section 2: Development and prototyping

The first integrated build is 15 units with a budget of about £80 each, to test the clip mount, heat from the LED, battery life and water resistance. In parallel the CAD is reworked for cost: the front housing becomes the lens by moulding it in clear material, snap fits replace four of the six screws, and the gasket is replaced by a moulded-in seal around a single button. Two screws are kept on purpose so the battery can be replaced, which answers the low planet score from stage 1.

What they decided

  • Machine the first prototype housings, as they need real fit and function more than appearance.
  • Cut the part count from 18 to 12 and the screws from six to two.
  • Build a line-by-line bill of materials so each cost can be quoted and challenged.

5. Verify

Is the design proven and ready to freeze?

Plan sections 2, 4 and 6: Development, sustainability, and funding

Production-intent parts go through drop, rain, temperature and battery-life tests and an electrical pre-compliance check. Before freezing the design, the housing is checked against moulding rules. The first pass, with 0.5° draft on a textured surface, 0.25 mm inside corners and two undercuts, has three likely problems. After revision (3° draft, 1 mm radii, thinner ribs, smaller screw bosses and no undercuts) it passes every check. Two moulders quote, and the team accepts £14,000 for a two-cavity production tool. With that quote in hand, the launch budget is rebuilt, because the next stage spends most of it.

What they decided

  • Freeze the design only after the revised housing passes the DFM check and the moulder's own review.
  • Ship by sea rather than air: it is cheaper and, on these figures, keeps the footprint lower.
  • Budget for 14 months to launch, with 15% contingency on top.

6. Industrialise

Can it be made reliably at the target cost?

Plan section 3: Manufacturing and cost

The team checks that a £14,000 tool is the right call at 5,000 units a year, compared with keeping the housings on vacuum casting (£2,400 set-up, £6.50 a set), CNC machining (£300, £11.00) or 3D printing (£16.00). The moulded housing set costs £2.00, which is the BOM's £1.10 of material plus £0.90 of moulding. The unit cost is then rebuilt with the BOM totals and the revised assembly time, and finally the cost of getting each shipment of 2,000 units to the UK warehouse is added: £150 origin charges, £1,000 sea freight, £40 insurance, 2.7% duty, £150 clearance and £250 UK delivery.

What they decided

  • Commit to the production tool: at 5,000 units it is clearly cheaper than any soft-tooled route.
  • Use £13.99 landed, not the supplier's £12.83, in every price and cash calculation from here on.
  • Order 2,000 units first, which matches the £28,000 of first inventory in the budget.

7. Launch

Does the price work for everyone, and does the cash last?

Plan sections 5 and 6: Route to market, and funding and cash

With the landed cost fixed, the team checks that the retail chain still works at £50, works out what an average sale is really worth after channel costs, and tests how long it takes to recover the one-off costs and whether cash lasts the first year. The funding raised matches the planner's £110,055 target, so if the contingency is not spent, about £23,000 is left at launch (£10,005 of buffer plus £13,050 of contingency). Running costs are kept to £2,500 a month in year one, with the founders taking no salary.

Direct: £41.67 − £11.80 channel costs = £29.87 · Retail: £22.56 − £2.30 = £20.26 · Blend: 0.4 × £29.87 + 0.6 × £20.26 ≈ £24.10

What they decided

  • Offer retailers a price that leaves the brand a 38% margin; at 39% the shelf price needed would be £50.04, just over the ceiling. The resulting £22.56 wholesale price is 36p below the £22.92 assumed at the define stage, and the blend below uses it.
  • Plan on £24.10 net revenue and £14.00 cost per unit (the £13.99 landed cost, rounded), so each sale contributes £10.10.
  • Treat the £59,000 of one-off costs (engineering, prototypes, tooling, compliance and launch) as a year-two recovery, and protect the contingency as working capital.

What the team learned

  • The sales route set the cost, not the factory. The retail route allowed £16 per unit at £50; direct sales allowed £22. Designing to the tighter figure from the start is what kept retail open.
  • Costing the concept early paid off. The first estimate of £16.23 was over the ceiling before shipping. Because it was known at the concept stage, the fix was a CAD change, not a new tool.
  • Assembly and part count were the cheapest savings. Going from 18 parts to 12 and from six screws to two cut assembly from 6.0 to 3.5 minutes. Combined with the BOM review, the unit cost fell from £16.23 to £12.83.
  • Landed cost is the real unit cost. Freight, duty and delivery added £1.16 a unit. Using the supplier price instead would have overstated margin on every sale.
  • Year one pays the running costs, not the development. Break-even on the £59,000 of one-off costs comes at 5,842 units, early in year two. Without the cost-down, at about £17.50 landed, it would need 8,940 units.

FAQ

Is Project Lantern a real product?

No. It is an illustrative product, and all the figures are invented for teaching. They are not data about any real product or client. The figures are chosen to be plausible so the connections between stages are realistic, but your own costs, prices and timescales will differ.

Why does the walkthrough use the retailer price rather than the website price?

Because it is the tightest limit. At £50 the retail route leaves room for a £16 unit cost, against £22 for direct sales. A product designed to the retail figure can be sold through either route; one designed to the direct figure cannot be sold through shops at a profit. See why route to market matters.

Why is the landed cost used instead of the factory price?

The factory price leaves out freight, insurance, duty, clearance and delivery to your warehouse. In this walkthrough they add £1.16 a unit, about 9%. Prices, margins, break-even and cash flow should all use the landed figure. The landed cost guide explains each part.

Can I open the tools with my own numbers instead?

Yes. Each button opens the tool with the walkthrough's inputs, and you can change any of them. Use "Copy link to this result" to keep or share your version, and "Save to my product plan" to collect results in the Product Plan Builder. Nothing is stored on a server.
